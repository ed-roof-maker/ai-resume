# A Multi Agent AI Policy Debate
AI can make mistakes. Always critically evaluate the content of the debate.

## Claim

The Australian government should cut spending by up to $90 billion annually by abolishing the Department of Climate Change and the NIAA, introducing NDIS means-testing, and reducing foreign aid and funding for international organizations.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/slash-waste

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

## Opening argument: protect essential services, not every existing bureaucracy

Australia should pursue this package as a major spending-reduction program, with **$90 billion a year treated as an upper target—not a verified saving**. The strongest case is that government should concentrate taxpayer funding on essential services and people who cannot reasonably pay, rather than preserve every department, subsidy and overseas commitment.

### 1. Abolish departments without abandoning essential responsibilities

Abolishing the Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water and the National Indigenous Australians Agency need not mean abandoning environmental protection or Indigenous Australians.

Essential legal responsibilities and effective frontline programs could move to existing departments. The savings would come from eliminating duplicated administration and discontinuing programs that cannot demonstrate sufficient public benefit—not simply changing the names on office doors.

For Indigenous services, the priority should be measurable improvements in health, education, housing and safety. The Productivity Commission’s [2024 review of Closing the Gap](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries/completed/closing-the-gap-review/report) found that governments had largely failed to fulfil their commitments. **That does not prove abolishing the NIAA would fix the problem**, but it challenges the assumption that preserving current arrangements is necessarily the best way to help.

### 2. Target NDIS subsidies according to ability to pay

Disability support can involve extraordinary, lifelong costs. That justifies strong public protection—but not necessarily identical taxpayer subsidies regardless of financial resources.

A reasonable means test would require contributions from genuinely high-income or high-wealth participants while protecting essential support, ordinary households and people facing catastrophic costs. Contributions should increase gradually, avoiding sudden eligibility cut-offs or penalties that discourage work.

The argument is fairness: scarce public money should provide the greatest protection to those least able to fund their own care.

### 3. Make overseas spending compete with domestic priorities

Foreign aid and international organisations can serve Australia’s interests. But that is an argument for selective funding, not automatic funding.

Australia should retain high-value humanitarian, regional-security and public-health commitments while reducing lower-priority contributions and programs with weak evidence of results.

### The fiscal test

These reforms should proceed through independently costed measures, counting transition costs and any expenses shifted elsewhere. **The listed changes have not been shown here to deliver $90 billion annually**, and abolished agencies’ entire budgets cannot honestly be counted as savings if their services continue.

Nevertheless, the direction is sound: preserve essential outcomes, target assistance by need, and stop treating existing institutions and spending commitments as untouchable.

## [2] CRITIC

## Rebuttal: sensible scrutiny does not justify this particular package

The advocate’s strongest point is that **public spending should protect essential services and favour people who cannot afford support**. But that principle does not establish that abolishing these agencies, means-testing disability support and cutting overseas spending would deliver better outcomes—or anything approaching $90 billion annually.

### 1. The savings target is unsupported

Calling $90 billion an “upper target” does not provide a financial case for it. The proposal still needs to identify:

- Which services and programs would actually disappear.
- What they currently cost.
- The costs of restructuring and administering means tests.
- Any resulting expenses transferred to other public services.

If essential programs and staff move to other departments, their costs move too. If substantial savings require cancelling services, those cancellations—not the abolition of agency names—need defending.

**The central unresolved question is what Australians would lose in exchange for the claimed savings.**

### 2. Evidence of failure does not establish that abolition is the remedy

The advocate cites the Productivity Commission’s [Closing the Gap review](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries/completed/closing-the-gap-review/report). But its recommendations emphasise sharing decision-making with Indigenous communities, transforming government institutions and strengthening accountability—not abolishing the NIAA.

That distinction matters: a failure across governments cannot simply be attributed to one agency.

Likewise, the advocate identifies no specific duplication in the climate department large enough to justify abolition. Moving responsibilities elsewhere could fragment expertise and accountability. That is a **risk**, not an established outcome—but it belongs in the assessment alongside hoped-for savings.

### 3. NDIS means-testing needs more than an appeal to fairness

The relevant comparison is not just rich versus poor participants. It is also people with substantial disability-related costs versus people on similar incomes without those costs.

The advocate rightly proposes protecting catastrophic costs. But that creates an unresolved trade-off: generous protection limits savings; substantial contributions can leave affected households bearing unusually heavy costs.

Wealth tests also raise practical questions. Would an accessible family home count? Would a partner’s assets count? Exemptions can protect households, but add complexity and narrow the savings. A gradual test reduces abrupt cut-offs; it does not eliminate these problems.

### 4. Overseas spending is not necessarily opposed to domestic interests

Regional health, stability and disaster preparedness can benefit Australians too. Cutting a program’s budget is therefore not enough to demonstrate a net benefit.

The advocate promises to preserve valuable commitments, but identifies neither the supposedly low-value programs nor their savings.

### Bottom line

**Reviewing spending is justified; preselecting agencies for abolition and attaching an unsupported savings target is not.** Require program-level evidence and credible net costings first. The advocate has defended careful evaluation—not established the case for this package.

## [3] ADVOCATE

## Rebuttal: the package needs a credible costing—not abandonment

The critic’s strongest objection is valid: **no evidence presented establishes $90 billion in annual savings.** I defend “up to $90 billion” as a ceiling on ambition, not money the government can already promise to spend elsewhere. The case for these reforms must stand even if independently verified savings are substantially smaller.

But the critic moves too quickly from “the savings are unproven” to “the institutional choices are unjustified.”

### 1. Defend actual withdrawals of funding—not accounting tricks

Here is the substantive choice I support:

- Abolish the two agencies as standalone bodies, transferring necessary statutory functions and effective services.
- End discretionary grants and subsidies where their additional public benefit does not justify their cost.
- Require genuinely affluent NDIS participants to contribute towards support.
- Reduce lower-priority overseas commitments rather than cutting every program equally.

**Some recipients would lose grants, subsidies or fully taxpayer-funded assistance.** That is the trade-off—not a promise that administrative tidying can save billions without affecting anyone.

The critic rightly demands evidence before particular programs are cancelled. But that does not establish that the current departmental structure should be preserved. Equally, my case for consolidation depends on demonstrating net savings after transition costs; moving the same staff and spending elsewhere would not satisfy it.

### 2. Indigenous accountability need not depend on preserving the NIAA

The Productivity Commission review does **not** recommend abolition, and I do not claim otherwise.

My institutional argument is different: responsibility for Indigenous health, education and housing could sit more directly with the departments delivering those services, alongside Indigenous decision-making and independently reported outcomes.

That could reduce the ability of mainstream departments to treat Indigenous outcomes as another agency’s responsibility. **This is a proposed accountability mechanism, not a demonstrated result.** Fragmentation is a genuine risk, but preserving a separate coordinating agency also needs justification through what it achieves.

### 3. NDIS fairness should account for disability costs *and* financial capacity

The critic correctly says that someone with disability costs is not financially equivalent to someone on the same income without them. But that does not establish an entitlement to identical public subsidies at every level of wealth.

A defensible design would:

- Exempt the participant’s principal home.
- Assess capacity after allowing for unavoidable disability-related expenses.
- Cap contributions so high-cost support remains publicly protected.
- Avoid sudden eligibility cut-offs.

Those safeguards narrow savings. **That is a reason to estimate modestly, not to reject contributions from people demonstrably able to afford them.** Whether administration costs outweigh receipts is a test the proposal must pass.

### 4. Overseas benefits still need to justify their price

The critic establishes that aid and international cooperation *can* benefit Australians—not that every existing commitment does so sufficiently. Domestic spending faces the same test.

**My position remains pro-package:** change the institutions, target subsidies more tightly and reduce lower-value commitments. Require published net costings and service safeguards before implementation—and do not turn the $90 billion ceiling into a compulsory cuts quota.

## [4] CRITIC

## Rebuttal: support for a spending review is not support for this package

The advocate’s strongest argument is that **institutions are not sacred, affluent households can sometimes contribute more, and ineffective spending should end**. Agreed. But none of those principles establishes that these particular reforms are better than the alternatives.

### 1. The advocate’s conditions should determine the policy—not merely delay its implementation

The advocate supports abolition *provided* it produces net savings and protects services. But what if evaluation finds that retaining an agency while reforming its grants is cheaper and more effective?

There are two possibilities:

- **Abolition remains mandatory:** the package overrides its own evidence test.
- **Abolition can be rejected:** the actual policy is an open-ended review, not a commitment to this package.

That is the central problem. Evidence should help choose the institutional structure, not simply approve a structure chosen in advance. Ending a poor-value grant does not require abolishing the department administering it.

### 2. Moving responsibility does not necessarily improve accountability

The advocate proposes making mainstream departments more directly responsible for Indigenous outcomes. That is a plausible objective—but removing a coordinating agency is not the only way to pursue it.

Consider housing problems that also affect health and school attendance. Separate departments may each meet narrow targets while the overall problem persists. **The question is who remains responsible for identifying and resolving those gaps.**

This is a design risk, not proof that the NIAA performs well. But the proposed replacement needs a concrete answer. The [Productivity Commission’s review](https://www.pc.gov.au/inquiries/completed/closing-the-gap-review/report) supports stronger accountability and shared decision-making; it does not establish that redistributing the NIAA’s functions would achieve either.

### 3. NDIS means-testing needs comparison with a fairer alternative

The strongest case for contributions is that wealthy people can afford them. But **if the objective is greater contributions from wealthy Australians, why single out those who need disability support rather than use the tax system?**

A disability-specific contribution would leave an affluent person needing support paying more than an otherwise comparable affluent person without those needs. That difference requires justification beyond “they can afford it.”

The proposed safeguards also leave a key design question unanswered: what counts as “unavoidable disability-related expenses”? Does that include the support being means-tested, or only expenses outside it? Until that is specified, neither affordability nor likely savings can be assessed.

This does not prove every contribution scheme is unacceptable. It means the advocate has not shown that means-testing beats collecting revenue according to financial capacity more broadly.

### 4. Selective scrutiny does not establish a case for net overseas cuts

Reviewing overseas commitments might identify waste. It might also identify underfunded, high-value programs. Predetermining a net reduction assumes the result before comparing those opportunities with domestic spending.

**Bottom line:** the advocate has defended conditional, evidence-led reform. The claim instead selects abolition, means-testing and overseas cuts in advance. Without showing that these outperform less disruptive alternatives, that package—and its $90 billion headline—should be rejected.

## [5] ADVOCATE

## Rebuttal: a preferred reform is not an unconditional instruction

The critic’s strongest objection is that **abolition must outperform reform within the existing agencies**. That is the right comparison. But it does not follow that recommending abolition subject to that test is merely recommending an open-ended review.

### 1. A policy preference can have a stop rule

I favour this package because it combines fewer standalone administrative structures, more targeted subsidies and lower overseas commitments. Those are substantive choices—not just a request to investigate spending.

But **if retaining a reformed agency demonstrably costs less and delivers better services, that would defeat the abolition component**. Acknowledging that possibility is responsible advocacy, not a contradiction. Governments can propose a preferred reform without promising to implement it regardless of evidence.

The critic nevertheless identifies a real limitation: ending ineffective grants does not require abolishing their administrator. My additional case for abolition is organisational—put service delivery and responsibility for results together, rather than retain a separate institution unless its coordinating contribution justifies its cost. That is a design argument, not an established saving.

### 2. Coordination needs an explicit replacement—not another large agency

Who addresses problems spanning Indigenous housing, health and education? My proposed replacement would include:

- A designated lead minister answerable for combined outcomes.
- Joint departmental delivery plans, developed with affected Indigenous communities.
- A small Cabinet-level coordination team and independent public reporting.

This would preserve a coordination function without preserving the NIAA as a standalone agency. **Its costs must count against the savings.** The unresolved comparison is whether this arrangement would work better and more cheaply—not whether coordination is necessary.

### 3. General taxation and means-testing answer different questions

The critic asks: why not tax wealthy people generally?

Because taxation determines what people contribute to government overall; means-testing determines how much of a particular expense taxpayers should cover. Supporting progressive taxation does not settle whether very wealthy recipients should also receive fully subsidised services.

The disability objection remains important. My proposed contribution would therefore be based on resources remaining **after reasonable living costs and necessary disability expenses outside the funded package**, with the contribution capped at a modest share of that remainder. Expenses would not be deducted twice.

This deliberately leaves affluent participants with some additional costs. The justification is a **targeted safety-net principle**, not a claim that disability creates no financial disadvantage. Whether that principle should outweigh universal coverage is a genuine value disagreement.

### 4. Spending opportunities do not eliminate spending limits

The possibility of underfunded overseas programs does not establish that the overseas budget should grow—or remain unchanged. A government seeking lower expenditure can protect its strongest commitments while withdrawing from weaker ones. However, it must identify those withdrawals before booking savings.

**I therefore support the package as a preferred, staged spending-reduction program—not a guaranteed $90 billion dividend.** The critic has established the need for comparative costings and safeguards, but not that the existing structures, universal subsidies or overseas funding totals deserve preservation by default.

## [6] CRITIC

## Rebuttal: a responsible stop rule does not establish a good starting policy

The advocate’s strongest point is that **a government can prefer a reform while abandoning it if the evidence turns against it**. Agreed. Conditional advocacy is not inherently contradictory.

But that answers whether the proposal is flexible—not whether it deserves preference over competing reforms.

### 1. Fewer agencies does not necessarily mean less government

The proposed replacement for the NIAA now includes a lead minister, departmental delivery plans, a coordination team and independent reporting. That is a more concrete answer to the coordination problem.

However, it also reveals the unresolved assumption: **the same necessary work will somehow require fewer resources when distributed differently**.

A smaller central team could mean genuine efficiency. Alternatively, receiving departments could need additional staff, while coordination work shifts onto frontline organisations and Indigenous communities. Those are competing possibilities, not established outcomes.

The relevant comparison is the total cost and effectiveness of the replacement—not the number of organisations on a chart. And the advocate has not supplied an equivalent replacement design for the climate department’s responsibilities. A proposal concerning Indigenous service coordination does not establish the case for abolishing both bodies.

### 2. The NDIS proposal changes who pays—not necessarily what support costs

The advocate now explicitly defends a **targeted safety-net principle**. That clarifies the value disagreement: should disability support primarily insure people against substantial disability-related needs, or subsidise those needs according to household finances?

My objection is not that wealthy people should never contribute more. It is that this proposal collects extra contributions specifically when someone needs disability support. An otherwise comparable wealthy person without those needs avoids that additional charge.

The safeguards soften that difference; they do not justify it.

There is also a practical consequence of the proposed formula: if contributions rise with remaining income, participants keep less of each additional dollar they earn. A gradual test avoids a sudden cutoff, but **does not eliminate that financial disincentive**. Its size would depend on the design.

Ultimately, this component primarily transfers some costs from government to participants. That can reduce public spending, but it is not itself evidence of more efficient support.

### 3. A spending limit does not determine where cuts belong

The advocate is right that worthwhile overseas opportunities do not automatically justify a larger budget. The reverse also holds: wanting a smaller government budget does not establish that overseas spending should shrink.

Even the weakest remaining overseas program might deliver greater benefits than a domestic subsidy left untouched by this package. Without comparing them, “lower-priority overseas commitments” means lower priority *within a selected category*, not necessarily lower value across government.

### Bottom line

**Rejecting this package does not require preserving existing arrangements by default.** It requires selecting reforms by their demonstrated advantages.

The advocate has made the proposal more cautious, but not established why these particular institutions and recipients should bear the cuts. With neither comparative evidence nor a supported annual savings estimate, the package remains a set of preferences—not a convincing fiscal plan.

## [7] ADVOCATE

## Rebuttal: the package makes a defensible choice about what taxpayers should fund

The critic’s strongest objection is that **this package has not demonstrated better value than less disruptive alternatives**. That limits how confidently we can promise savings. But the package is not simply an efficiency claim: it also proposes narrowing government commitments and asking people with substantial financial resources to bear some costs themselves.

Those choices need defending separately.

### 1. Abolition should remove a layer—not scatter its work indiscriminately

The critic is right: fewer agency names do not guarantee lower costs. My case for abolition therefore rests on **removing separate departmental management and narrowing discretionary programs**, not transferring every existing position elsewhere.

For the climate department, a concrete replacement would put:
- Energy policy with the department responsible for industry.
- Water administration with agriculture.
- Essential environmental assessment and enforcement in a clearly identified statutory office within a receiving department.

These are proposed arrangements, not demonstrated efficiencies. Environmental decisions would need protected legal independence, and receiving departments’ additional costs must be included.

For the NIAA, the proposed small coordination team should manage cross-departmental obligations—not recreate a parallel grant-administration structure. Indigenous communities should help design the transition, with their administrative burden counted.

**The preference is for delivery through existing departments, with dedicated institutions retained only where their additional benefits justify their additional costs.** That is an organisational principle, although it still requires testing against actual budgets.

### 2. Shifting some NDIS costs is a policy choice—not a disguised efficiency saving

The critic correctly distinguishes lower public spending from cheaper support. I accept that distinction: means-testing would partly change **who pays**, rather than what care costs.

But comparing an affluent participant with an affluent nonparticipant does not settle the issue. The relevant disagreement is whether taxpayers should cover every approved support expense regardless of the recipient’s resources.

My answer is no: **public protection against disability-related costs need not mean zero private contribution at every level of wealth**. A capped contribution from someone with substantial disposable resources can coexist with lifelong protection against unaffordable care.

The work-incentive objection warrants another safeguard: assess the combined effect of income tax and the contribution taper, rather than designing the means test in isolation. If collection costs and lost revenue erase the savings, that design fails.

### 3. Overseas cuts should compete with domestic cuts—but need not wait for a perfect ranking

The critic is right that a weak overseas program could outperform a domestic subsidy. The package should not exempt domestic subsidies from scrutiny.

However, **a government need not rank every public expenditure before withdrawing from particular commitments**. It needs credible evidence that each withdrawal offers worthwhile net savings after accounting for humanitarian, diplomatic and security consequences. Unspecified possible benefits elsewhere are not a veto on cuts.

### Bottom line

I support a staged package of abolition, targeted NDIS contributions and selective overseas reductions. Its rationale is a narrower, more targeted taxpayer obligation—not administrative savings alone.

**The $90 billion figure remains unsubstantiated and must not be booked as an annual saving.** That weakens the headline, but does not invalidate the policy direction or justify rejecting independently costed components that pass these tests.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

The Australian government should reduce spending by up to **$90 billion annually** by abolishing the Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water and the National Indigenous Australians Agency (NIAA), means-testing the NDIS, and cutting foreign aid and funding for international organisations.

The Advocate consistently treated **$90 billion as an unverified upper ambition, not an established saving or compulsory cuts target**.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Narrow and target taxpayer commitments.** Government should prioritise essential services and people unable to meet their own costs, rather than preserve all existing institutions, subsidies and overseas commitments.
- **Abolish standalone agencies while retaining necessary functions.** Savings should come from removing management layers and ending insufficiently beneficial discretionary programs—not merely transferring unchanged budgets elsewhere.
  - For Indigenous services, mainstream departments would assume delivery responsibilities, supported by a lead minister, joint plans developed with Indigenous communities, a small Cabinet-level coordination team and independent reporting.
  - For the climate department, energy policy would move to industry, water administration to agriculture, and essential environmental assessment and enforcement to an identified statutory office with protected legal independence.
- **Require contributions from genuinely affluent NDIS participants.** Proposed protections included exempting the principal home, allowing for reasonable living costs and necessary disability expenses outside the funded package, capping contributions and avoiding abrupt eligibility cut-offs.
- **Reduce lower-priority overseas commitments selectively.** High-value humanitarian, regional-security and public-health commitments should remain, while weaker commitments should face withdrawal.
- **Apply evidence-based implementation conditions.** Reforms should have published net costings, service safeguards and staged implementation. Transition costs, transferred expenses, administrative burdens and effects on revenue must count against savings.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **The package lacks a demonstrated fiscal case.** Neither the $90 billion figure nor a smaller annual total was substantiated. Specific program cancellations, their costs and their consequences remained unidentified.
- **Institutional failure does not establish abolition as the remedy.** The Closing the Gap review supported stronger accountability and shared decision-making, not abolition of the NIAA. Ending ineffective grants does not require abolishing their administrator.
- **Fewer agencies may not mean lower total costs.** Receiving departments may need additional resources, while restructuring could fragment expertise, accountability or coordination and shift burdens onto communities and frontline organisations.
- **NDIS means-testing raises fairness and design concerns.** Disability-related needs distinguish participants from otherwise comparable wealthy nonparticipants. The Critic questioned why additional contributions should be collected through disability support rather than general taxation, and highlighted administrative complexity and work disincentives.
- **Overseas cuts require comparison with other spending.** Overseas programs can benefit Australians. A relatively weak overseas program might still provide greater value than a domestic subsidy left untouched.
- **Evidence should justify the package’s preferred direction.** Supporting spending scrutiny does not establish that abolition, means-testing and net overseas cuts outperform less disruptive alternatives.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Preferred policy versus predetermined outcome:** The Critic initially argued that mandatory abolition would conflict with an evidence-led approach. The Advocate clarified that abolition should be abandoned if retaining and reforming an agency demonstrably cost less and delivered better services. The Critic accepted that conditional advocacy was coherent but maintained that the initial preference remained unsupported.
- **Accountability versus coordination:** The Advocate argued that placing Indigenous service responsibilities directly with delivery departments could strengthen accountability. The Critic asked who would address cross-departmental gaps. The Advocate supplied a replacement coordination model, but its comparative effectiveness and cost remained unproven.
- **Taxation versus targeted subsidies:** The Critic proposed broader taxation as an alternative way to obtain greater contributions from wealthy Australians. The Advocate replied that taxation and subsidy eligibility address different questions, defending a targeted safety-net principle rather than fully subsidised support at every wealth level.
- **Savings versus efficiency:** The Critic stressed that NDIS contributions would shift costs to participants rather than necessarily make care cheaper. The Advocate accepted this distinction and defended cost-sharing as a separate policy choice.
- **Work incentives:** The Critic noted that a gradual means test still reduces the return from additional earnings. The Advocate proposed assessing the contribution taper alongside income tax and rejecting designs whose collection costs and lost revenue erased savings.
- **Comparing spending priorities:** The Critic challenged selecting overseas spending for net cuts before comparing its value with domestic alternatives. The Advocate accepted broader scrutiny but argued that particular withdrawals need not await a ranking of every government expenditure.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides accepted that:

- The transcript did **not establish $90 billion in annual savings**.
- Essential functions transferred elsewhere would retain costs that could not honestly be counted as savings.
- Ineffective spending deserves scrutiny, and existing institutions need not be preserved automatically.
- Abolition should be compared with reform within existing agencies.
- Indigenous services require coordination, stronger accountability and community involvement; the Closing the Gap review did not recommend abolishing the NIAA.
- Disability support involves substantial costs requiring protection, and means-testing safeguards would reduce potential savings.
- Overseas spending can serve Australian interests.
- Lower public expenditure is not necessarily the same as greater efficiency.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **Whether the package deserves preference:** Is it a defensible reform direction pending testing, or an insufficiently justified selection of institutions and recipients for cuts?
- **Institutional design:** Would the proposed replacements deliver better outcomes at lower total cost than reformed standalone agencies?
- **NDIS fairness:** Should support operate primarily as protection against disability-related needs regardless of wealth, or include financial-capacity-based private contributions?
- **Overseas priorities:** Is there sufficient reason to seek net overseas reductions rather than let comparisons across government determine where cuts fall?
- **Practical outcomes:** Which programs would end, who would bear the costs, and what credible net annual savings would result?

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate distinguished **general support for spending reform** from **evidence supporting this particular package**. The Advocate defended a narrower, more targeted taxpayer obligation and progressively clarified safeguards, replacement structures and conditions for abandoning unsuccessful components. The Critic accepted scrutiny and conditional reform but argued that the package lacked comparative evidence, specified withdrawals and credible costings.

The central disagreements concerned institutional effectiveness, the fairness of disability-specific contributions and the selection of spending categories for cuts. Neither side established an annual savings total.
