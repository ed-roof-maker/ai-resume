# A Multi Agent AI Policy Debate
AI can make mistakes. Always critically evaluate the content of the debate.

## Claim

The Australian government should fund 30% of oil and gas exploration costs in Commonwealth waters in exchange for public equity stakes of up to 30% in resulting production licences.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/oil-and-gas

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

## Opening argument: public risk should buy public ownership

Australia should fund 30% of offshore oil and gas exploration costs **in return for a commercially fair public stake**, because that gives Australians a direct share of successful projects—not just the risks and costs associated with developing them.

**1. Australians should earn an owner’s return from a public resource.**  
Taxes remain essential, but equity provides a different benefit: a share of profits, access to project information and a voice in major decisions. If public money helps discover a valuable field, taxpayers should hold a valuable asset in return—not simply have subsidised a private company.

There is a real precedent for this model. Norway’s [State’s Direct Financial Interest](https://www.norskpetroleum.no/en/framework/the-states-revenues/) gives the state ownership interests in petroleum licences, with corresponding shares of investment, costs and income. That does not prove Australia would achieve the same returns, but it demonstrates that public participation is commercially workable.

**2. Shared exploration risk can unlock worthwhile investment.**  
Exploration requires substantial spending before anyone knows whether a commercially viable discovery exists. Government participation could help projects proceed where that uncertainty discourages private investment. Requiring private investors to fund the remaining 70% preserves a strong incentive to reject poor prospects and control costs.

The important qualification: **this must be investment, not an automatic subsidy.** Projects should pass independent commercial assessment, competitive selection and a test of whether public participation adds genuine value.

**3. The strongest objections call for safeguards—not outright rejection.**  
Dry wells would cost taxpayers money; successful discoveries would not guarantee profits. Public ownership also does not make fossil fuels clean or guarantee cheaper domestic energy.

Those risks justify:
- A capped, transparent investment portfolio.
- A default 30% stake, with any smaller stake justified by independently assessed equivalent value.
- Full accounting for future development costs and decommissioning liabilities.
- Investment tests incorporating climate commitments and scenarios of falling fossil-fuel demand.

**Bottom line:** where offshore exploration is commercially and environmentally justified, Australia should negotiate as an investor-owner rather than offer support without ownership. A carefully priced 30% contribution can secure a lasting public share of the upside while leaving most capital—and most risk—with private investors.

## [2] CRITIC

## Public ownership is better than a giveaway—but that does not justify funding exploration

The advocate’s strongest point is that **taxpayers who invest should receive a fair share of the upside**. Agreed as a principle of transaction design. But it does not establish that taxpayers should make this investment in the first place.

### 1. The alternative is not necessarily a subsidy without ownership

Australia can leave exploration risk with private investors while collecting taxes and resource revenues from successful projects. Public ownership must therefore outperform **not subsidising exploration**, rather than merely outperform an unconditional handout.

The advocate has not shown that funding offshore exploration is a better use of public money than other investments—or reducing public debt. “Australians own the resource” does not, by itself, explain why they should also finance the search.

### 2. The proposed bargain leaves its biggest financial question unanswered

Paying **30% of exploration costs** is not the same as paying 30% of the cost of a producing field.

What happens after a discovery?

- **If government must fund its share of development, operations and closure**, the initial exploration contribution opens the door to substantial additional commitments.
- **If its stake is exempt from those costs**, private partners must absorb them, and the commercial value of that arrangement needs justification.
- **If government declines further funding**, can its stake be diluted?

The [Norwegian model cited by the advocate](https://www.norskpetroleum.no/en/framework/the-states-revenues/) involves corresponding shares of costs, investment and income. That establishes that state participation is possible—not that this particular exploration-for-equity formula offers Australia good value.

Moreover, **“up to 30%” is a ceiling, not a guaranteed return**. A default 30% stake and independent valuation are additions to the proposal, not benefits secured by its wording.

### 3. Private participation does not establish public value

Investors supplying the remaining 70% would still have incentives to control costs. But that does not prove government support is necessary.

There is a difficult selection problem:

- If a project would proceed anyway, public funding need not unlock additional investment.
- If it would not proceed, government must explain why taxpayers should take exposure that private capital considers unattractive.

Some projects could pass that test. The advocate has not identified a market failure that justifies adopting this funding rule.

### Bottom line

Competitive selection, climate tests and liability accounting are sensible safeguards. **They limit potential damage; they do not establish an investment case.** Australia should not commit to funding a fixed share of offshore exploration merely because equity is preferable to a giveaway. The case for spending public money must come first.

## [3] ADVOCATE

## The real test is value for money—not whether private investors could act alone

The critic is right about the strongest objection: **equity is not enough to justify the spending.** The proposal must beat leaving exploration privately funded, after accounting for risk and the opportunity cost of public money.

But that supports a selective investment programme—not a blanket rejection of public participation.

### 1. Separate the 30% funding rate from the decision to invest

Government should fund 30% of exploration costs **only for projects that pass an investment test**. The percentage determines how an approved investment is shared; it need not mean every exploration proposal receives funding.

That test should require an independently assessed return above the government’s financing cost, with allowances for dry wells, commodity-price risk, climate policy and future liabilities. Competing proposals should be ranked within a limited budget.

The critic says safeguards do not establish an investment case. Correct—but **project-level valuation can establish that case**. Neither of us has evidence that every qualifying opportunity would fail it. The defensible policy is permission to make demonstrably worthwhile investments, not an obligation to spend regardless.

### 2. Make the ownership bargain explicit

I would implement the proposal as follows:

- Government pays 30% of an agreed, audited exploration budget.
- Before spending begins, it secures a contractual right to a specified interest—normally 30%—in any resulting production licence.
- That interest carries its corresponding share of subsequent costs and liabilities; it is **not a free claim on production revenue**.
- Before development commitments are made, government must approve further investment or pursue an agreed sale or dilution mechanism. Existing obligations cannot simply be abandoned.

A smaller stake would require independently demonstrated equivalent value; otherwise, reject the deal. These are necessary contractual details, not benefits automatically guaranteed by “up to 30%”.

### 3. The critic’s investment dilemma is too narrow

“If private investors would proceed anyway, public participation is unnecessary; if they would not, it is unattractive” misses an important distinction: **a worthwhile project need not be financeable on every investor’s preferred terms**.

Sharing exposure can make a large, uncertain investment acceptable without making its underlying economics poor. That is a commercial possibility, not proof that Australian projects currently face such a constraint.

Moreover, additional exploration is not the only possible public benefit. A fairly priced ownership stake can earn returns even in a project that would otherwise proceed. Taxes would still apply; equity would purchase an additional, risk-bearing claim. The decisive question remains whether that claim is worth its cost.

**Bottom line:** adopt the 30%-for-equity model as a tightly budgeted investment mechanism, not an entitlement for explorers. The critic has identified why government must price and select investments carefully—not why Australians should categorically forgo ownership in commercially sound developments.

## [4] CRITIC

## A potentially profitable deal does not establish a case for this programme

The advocate’s strongest argument is that **some exploration stakes could earn taxpayers an adequate return even without unlocking additional investment**. That is possible. But it does not justify creating a programme specifically to fund 30% of offshore oil and gas exploration.

### 1. The investment benchmark remains too weak

The advocate proposes returns above government financing costs, with allowances for risk. The crucial question is what those allowances mean.

**Cheap government borrowing does not make exploration risk cheap.** Taxpayers still absorb losses when wells fail or projects become uneconomic. A proper test must compare the investment with alternatives offering comparable risk—not merely with the interest paid on government debt.

“Independently valued” is not itself an answer. A valuation still depends on assumptions about discovery rates, development costs, future prices and the return taxpayers should demand. The advocate has supplied a screening process, but no evidence that this particular investment category would clear an appropriate benchmark.

### 2. Deal-level profitability can miss taxpayer-wide risk

Australia already receives petroleum-related taxes and resource revenues. Buying equity adds another public revenue stream exposed to the fortunes of the same industry.

That matters because **a stake can look reasonable in isolation while making the public finances less resilient overall**. Lower petroleum profits could reduce both tax receipts and investment returns together.

This is a financial inference, not a claim that every project would lose money. It means the investment test must consider the government’s existing exposure and the merits of diversification. If earning financial returns is the justification, why privilege new offshore exploration over a broader range of investments?

### 3. Selectivity does not justify the fixed formula

The advocate has moved from defending the proposal to defending permission to make worthwhile investments. Those are different propositions.

Even if a particular public investment were justified:

- Why should government pay exactly **30%** of exploration costs?
- Why should its ownership be capped at **30%**, rather than negotiated around the risks and rights involved?
- Why enter during exploration rather than purchase an interest later, when geological uncertainty is lower?

Later entry is not automatically better; it purchases greater certainty at a different price. The point is that the proposed formula has not been shown to beat those alternatives.

### Bottom line

The burden is not on critics to prove that **every conceivable investment would fail**. Proponents must establish why this targeted programme is preferable to other uses of public money.

The advocate has made a plausible case for evaluating individual opportunities—not for adopting a 30%-funded offshore exploration scheme. **Australia should not establish that scheme on the strength of hypothetical qualifying deals.**

## [5] ADVOCATE

## The case is for a limited co-investment programme—not a government bet on the whole industry

The critic’s strongest objection is fair: **a potentially profitable project does not automatically justify a sector-specific public programme.** My answer is that the proposed structure offers a useful bargain worth testing at limited scale: government enters alongside private investors, pays a minority share of exploration costs, and obtains a corresponding ownership interest before the discovery’s value is known.

That supports establishing a capped programme—not guaranteeing explorers public money.

### 1. Use the right benchmark, including taxpayers’ existing exposure

I accept the correction on financing costs. Government borrowing rates are not an adequate investment hurdle.

An eligible deal should have to compare favourably with investments carrying similar risks, **after allowing for Australia’s existing petroleum-related revenues**. That concentration risk can justify demanding a higher return or limiting the programme’s size.

But correlated revenues do not establish that every additional investment is undesirable. The relevant questions are **how much exposure is added and what compensation taxpayers receive for it**. The critic identifies a cost to price, not necessarily a reason to prohibit investment.

### 2. Exploration-stage entry has a specific purpose

Why not buy into a proven field later? Because later entry avoids dry-hole risk but pays for the discovery.

Under this proposal, the public negotiates ownership **before success is known**. It bears exploration losses in exchange for sharing any increase in value created by a commercial discovery. That is the investment case—not the mistaken idea that government deserves a cheap stake after private investors have established its value.

This is commercial reasoning, not evidence that exploration-stage investment always outperforms later entry. Both options should be compared. However, later entry is not a substitute that preserves the same potential upside at lower cost.

### 3. Thirty per cent is a workable sharing rule, not a magic optimum

There is a straightforward rationale for matching a 30% contribution with a 30% interest: each side bears a proportional share of agreed costs and receives a proportional share of ownership, with subsequent obligations explicitly priced.

It leaves private investors funding the substantial majority while giving taxpayers meaningful participation. **I cannot establish that 30% is uniquely optimal.** But a policy need not demonstrate that its chosen percentage beats every neighbouring percentage to be reasonable.

The critic is right about the ceiling: a smaller public stake must not become an invitation to overpay. Uncompensated reductions below 30% should be rejected.

### 4. Resolve uncertainty through a bounded first round

Australia should establish a small, competitive initial programme, with publication of investment rationales and independent review before expansion. No qualifying deals should mean no investments—not relaxed standards.

**The unresolved issue is whether suitable deals exist.** A disciplined first round can test that without committing taxpayers to an open-ended scheme. That is a stronger response to uncertainty than either assuming attractive returns or ruling out public ownership before testing the terms available.

## [6] CRITIC

## Testing the market does not require funding the wells

The advocate’s strongest proposal is a **small, competitive trial with no obligation to invest**. That reduces the potential loss. But it still leaves a missing step: why should discovering whether attractive deals exist require first establishing a publicly funded exploration programme?

### 1. Seek evidence before authorising investment

Government could invite non-binding proposals, commission independent assessments and compare the offered terms with alternative investments—**without committing to cover 30% of exploration costs**.

That would test whether companies actually offer credible opportunities, rather than merely whether public ownership is theoretically possible.

The distinction matters: **investigating a policy is not the same as adopting it**. The advocate’s admission that suitable deals may not exist supports preliminary assessment, not yet a funded programme.

### 2. Buying before discovery offers risk—not an established advantage

The advocate correctly says that buying into a proven field costs more because someone else has borne the exploration risk. But that higher price buys something valuable: greater confidence that there is a commercially useful resource.

Getting in earlier is therefore not, by itself, a better bargain. The relevant comparison includes failed exploration investments, not just the increase in value following successful discoveries.

There is also an unresolved information problem. Private operators may understand their prospects better than the public investor assessing them. **Competitive applications show which risks companies want to share; they do not automatically identify the best risks for taxpayers to buy.**

This is a potential selection problem, not evidence that companies would necessarily offer bad projects. But independent assessment must demonstrably overcome it. Matching 30% of costs with 30% ownership does not do that on its own.

### 3. A small trial may give a misleading answer

The advocate presents a bounded first round as a way to resolve uncertainty. Yet a small portfolio of exploration outcomes could be difficult to interpret:

- A discovery might reflect luck rather than sound selection.
- Dry wells might occur despite reasonable initial assessments.
- A valuable discovery still would not establish the final return after development and closure costs.
- Successful initial deals would not show that equally attractive opportunities remain available for expansion.

These are features of the proposed experiment, not predictions that it will fail. **The programme needs a credible explanation of what it would teach, when, and why that knowledge is worth the public exposure.** None has been supplied.

### Bottom line

A capped programme is less risky than an unlimited one, but **limiting the size of an unsupported investment case does not establish it**.

Australia should assess actual offers before adopting the funding commitment. Unless that assessment demonstrates a public advantage over private financing and alternative investments, the government should leave exploration costs with investors—not promise a 30% contribution in exchange for uncertain future equity.

## [7] ADVOCATE

## Assess first, fund second—but retain a clear route to public ownership

The critic’s strongest point is that **taxpayers should not finance wells merely to discover whether financing wells is a good idea**. I agree. My earlier argument put too much weight on what a small trial could teach. Exploration outcomes would be a noisy, slow test of investment quality.

The stronger case is for a **conditional funding programme whose investments must be justified before drilling**, not an experiment justified by hoped-for lessons.

### 1. Preliminary assessment should be the first stage—not an alternative to the policy

Government should solicit offers and independently assess them before committing exploration money. But that is compatible with adopting the proposed funding rule:

> For approved projects, government supplies 30% of exploration funding in exchange for a fairly valued public interest of up to 30%, with subsequent obligations specified in advance.

This separates **authorising a mechanism** from **approving an investment**. The critic is right to oppose an unconditional promise; it does not follow that government should reject a conditional programme.

An executable offer also answers a question that general research cannot settle: whether operators will actually accept the public’s required ownership rights, disclosure obligations and commercial terms. That is a reason to establish a negotiating framework—not to spend before assessment.

### 2. Information asymmetry requires contractual protection, not just another valuation

The critic correctly identifies a risk: operators may offer taxpayers prospects they understand better than government does.

Independent assessment alone cannot eliminate that disadvantage. The programme should therefore require:

- Access to underlying geological data and independent technical review.
- Operators retaining their substantial exposure through the exploration decision, with restrictions on transferring it away.
- Audited, pre-agreed exploration budgets, excluding inflated related-party charges.
- Public approval before cost overruns receive additional funding.

These protections do not guarantee good prospects. They make it harder for an operator to profit from the government’s contribution while leaving taxpayers with disproportionately poor risks. **A nominal 30% stake without those protections would not be enough.**

### 3. Judge investments prospectively—not by whether the first well succeeds

A first round should establish whether acceptable contracts can be secured and administered. It should **not** be treated as statistical proof of superior exploration returns.

Each investment must stand on its expected commercial value, including failed wells, future expenditure, closure liabilities and the public’s existing industry exposure. Expansion would require fresh assessments—not extrapolation from one lucky discovery.

### Bottom line

The critic has established a sound sequence: **obtain offers, assess them, then commit money**. That is how this policy should operate.

The remaining disagreement is whether Australia should have a defined route to invest when that assessment supports it. I argue yes: a conditional 30% co-investment programme can turn carefully selected public expenditure into enforceable public ownership. Uncertainty warrants demanding terms and the freedom to reject every offer—not ruling out the mechanism itself.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

The Australian government should fund **30% of oil and gas exploration costs in Commonwealth waters** in exchange for **public equity stakes of up to 30% in resulting production licences**.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Public funding should secure public ownership.** Equity would give taxpayers a share of successful projects’ profits, access to information and a voice in major decisions, rather than merely subsidising private companies. Norway’s State’s Direct Financial Interest demonstrates that public participation is commercially workable, though not that Australia would achieve equivalent returns.
- **Sharing exploration risk could enable worthwhile investment.** Private investors would supply the remaining 70%, preserving substantial incentives to select sound prospects and control costs. The Advocate acknowledged that actual financing constraints had not been established.
- **Equity could offer value even without additional exploration.** A fairly priced stake might earn an adequate return in a project that would proceed anyway. The relevant question is whether the investment is worth its cost.
- **The programme should be conditional and selective.** Independent assessment, competitive selection, a capped budget and the freedom to reject every offer would distinguish it from an automatic subsidy.
- **Ownership must include clearly specified obligations.** The Advocate proposed a normally 30% interest carrying corresponding development, operating and closure costs, with agreed sale or dilution arrangements if government declines further investment. A smaller stake would require independently demonstrated equivalent value.
- **Exploration-stage entry exchanges risk for potential upside.** Investing before discovery would expose taxpayers to dry wells while allowing them to share in value created by successful discoveries. The Advocate described 30% as a workable minority-sharing rule, not a uniquely optimal percentage.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **Fair equity terms do not establish that government should invest.** The proper comparison is with privately funded exploration and alternative uses of public money, not merely with subsidies that provide no ownership.
- **The original formula leaves major financial terms unresolved.** Funding exploration does not cover the full cost of production. Future funding obligations, dilution and closure liabilities matter, while “up to 30%” guarantees no particular stake.
- **Private participation does not prove public value.** Projects proceeding without support may not need it; projects seeking support require an explanation of why public exposure is justified. No demonstrated market failure or evidence of attractive qualifying deals was supplied.
- **Returns require an appropriate benchmark.** Government borrowing costs are insufficient: investments must be compared with alternatives carrying comparable risk. Petroleum equity could also compound existing exposure through petroleum-related taxes and resource revenues.
- **The sector and formula need justification.** Potentially profitable individual deals do not establish why government should privilege offshore exploration, contribute exactly 30%, cap ownership at 30%, or invest before rather than after discovery.
- **Selection and evaluation remain difficult.** Operators may know more about prospects than public assessors. A small trial could produce misleading results because discoveries and dry wells are noisy indicators, final returns depend on later costs, and initial opportunities may not support expansion.
- **Actual offers should be assessed before adopting a funding commitment.** Non-binding proposals and independent assessments could establish whether credible opportunities exist without first financing exploration.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Equity versus the decision to spend:** The Critic argued that ownership is preferable to a giveaway but does not justify expenditure. The Advocate accepted this distinction and made investment conditional on demonstrating value relative to alternatives.
- **Financing-cost benchmark:** The Critic challenged reliance on returns above government financing costs. The Advocate accepted that comparable-risk investments and taxpayers’ existing petroleum exposure must inform the hurdle.
- **Private financing dilemma:** The Advocate replied that a commercially worthwhile project might still face investors’ financing constraints, and that additional exploration is not the only possible benefit. The Critic maintained that these possibilities did not establish a programme-level case.
- **Early versus later entry:** The Advocate emphasised acquiring ownership before discovery creates value. The Critic responded that later entry buys reduced geological uncertainty and that any comparison must include failed exploration. The Advocate acknowledged that neither timing is automatically superior.
- **Learning through a trial:** The Critic questioned whether a small programme would generate reliable evidence. The Advocate conceded that this argument had been overstated and shifted to requiring justification before drilling, rather than funding wells for their hoped-for lessons.
- **Information asymmetry:** In response to the Critic, the Advocate proposed geological-data access, independent technical review, retained operator exposure, audited budgets and approval of additional funding for overruns.
- **Assessment versus authorisation:** The Critic favoured assessing actual offers before adopting the scheme. The Advocate argued that a conditional negotiating and investment framework could be authorised while withholding all exploration funding until individual offers passed assessment.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides accepted that:

- Taxpayers investing in projects should receive fairly valued rights to the upside.
- Equity alone does not justify public spending.
- Exploration losses, future expenditure and closure liabilities must be included in assessments.
- Public ownership does not guarantee profits, cleaner fossil fuels or cheaper domestic energy.
- Appropriate risk-adjusted comparisons and existing public petroleum exposure matter.
- Offers should be obtained and assessed before exploration money is committed.
- A successful first discovery would not establish the programme’s quality or justify automatic expansion.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **Whether to authorise a conditional programme now:** The Advocate supported a defined route to investment; the Critic considered the programme unjustified without prior evidence from actual offers.
- **Whether a sector-specific scheme is warranted:** The Advocate saw a potentially useful co-investment mechanism; the Critic sought a demonstrated advantage over private financing and alternative public investments.
- **Whether the 30% formula is sufficiently justified:** The Advocate defended it as reasonable proportional participation, while the Critic questioned the contribution rate, ownership ceiling and entry stage.
- **Whether safeguards adequately address selection risks:** The Advocate offered contractual protections, but their effectiveness in securing suitable investments remained unestablished.
- **Whether suitable deals exist:** Neither side supplied evidence resolving that question.

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate moved from a broad argument for public ownership toward a narrower discussion of conditional investment authority. The Advocate progressively clarified financial obligations, strengthened risk and contractual safeguards, and accepted that investments must be justified before drilling rather than through experimental results.

The Critic consistently distinguished a potentially fair or profitable transaction from a demonstrated case for a dedicated offshore exploration programme. By the end, both supported obtaining and assessing offers before spending. Their central disagreement remained whether that process should operate within an already authorised conditional 30% co-investment programme or precede any decision to adopt it.
