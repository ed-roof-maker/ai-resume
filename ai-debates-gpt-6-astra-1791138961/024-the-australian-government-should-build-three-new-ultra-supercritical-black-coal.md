# A Multi Agent AI Policy Debate
AI can make mistakes. Always critically evaluate the content of the debate.

## Claim

The Australian government should build three new ultra-supercritical black coal power plants in Collinsville, Port Augusta, and the Hunter Valley.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/embracing-australian-coal-reliable-energy-future

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

## Opening argument: build them as a reliability hedge

Australia should build these three plants—not to stop renewable energy, but to ensure the electricity system has a dependable backbone while older generators close. The strongest case is **reliability, geographic diversity and protection against a difficult transition**, rather than a claim that coal is the cheapest or cleanest option.

### 1. Replacing dependable supply is a substantial national challenge

AEMO’s [2024 Integrated System Plan](https://aemo.com.au/energy-systems/major-publications/integrated-system-plan-isp/2024-integrated-system-plan-isp) projects that about 90% of today’s coal-fired capacity will retire by 2035.

AEMO also finds that renewables connected by transmission, backed by storage and gas, offer the lowest-cost pathway. That is important evidence—but delivering that pathway requires many projects to arrive on time.

**The argument for these plants is an insurance argument:** if transmission, storage or replacement generation is delayed, Australia should not depend entirely on extending increasingly old coal stations. New plants would take years to deliver, so they would be a long-term safeguard, not an immediate shortage remedy.

### 2. Coal provides something that cheap renewable electricity alone does not

Wind and solar can generate inexpensive electricity, but maintaining supply through prolonged low-wind, low-sun periods requires additional resources.

Coal plants can store substantial fuel supplies on site and run through those periods. They also provide rotating machinery that helps stabilise the grid. Other technologies can provide these services; the case for coal is that retaining some reduces dependence on any single replacement strategy.

Ultra-supercritical plants use higher steam temperatures and pressures to extract more electricity from their fuel than older, less-efficient coal units. **That means lower emissions per unit of electricity than those older units—not low-carbon electricity.**

### 3. The three locations offer a geographically spread replacement strategy

- **Collinsville:** proximity to Queensland’s coal-producing regions and an established energy workforce.
- **Hunter Valley:** coal infrastructure, skilled workers and access to New South Wales’ electricity market.
- **Port Augusta:** an opportunity to restore dispatchable generation in a former coal-power community.

Port Augusta is the hardest case: a black-coal plant would need a credible delivered-fuel supply chain. Existing infrastructure at every location would also require assessment, not assumptions about easy reuse.

### 4. Government should buy resilience transparently

Public backing should come with competitive procurement, published costs, strict pollution controls and independent reliability assessments. Any insurance premium—including emissions costs—should be explicit.

**My position:** build three appropriately sized, staged replacement plants alongside renewables and storage. Australia should pursue cheaper clean energy without making the reliability of its entire transition depend on every component being delivered as planned.

## [2] CRITIC

## The reliability risk is real—but it does not justify these three plants

The advocate’s strongest point is that Australia needs insurance against delays in replacing retiring coal stations. That is reasonable. **The unsupported leap is that three government-built coal plants are the best insurance.**

### 1. Compare complete reliability options—not coal against wind and solar alone

[AEMO’s 2024 Integrated System Plan](https://aemo.com.au/energy-systems/major-publications/integrated-system-plan-isp/2024-integrated-system-plan-isp) identifies renewables connected by transmission, backed by storage and gas, as the lowest-cost replacement pathway. That is a comparison of electricity-system options, not simply a claim that solar panels generate cheap electricity.

This does not guarantee that every project will arrive on time. But departing from that pathway requires evidence that the proposed coal plants would cover likely shortfalls more effectively than alternatives—including demand response, storage, dispatchable generation and targeted extensions of existing capacity where safe.

**The advocate has established a need for contingency planning, not a need for these plants.**

### 2. Coal construction is not insurance against all construction delays

The proposal treats delays to transmission and storage as a central risk, while offering no delivery schedules, approvals assessment or cost estimates for the coal plants themselves.

New coal projects also require construction, grid connections, fuel arrangements and financing. The advocate acknowledges that they would take years. Without matching their opening dates to specific forecast shortages, their value as transition insurance remains unproven.

Geographic separation helps with some risks, but it does not establish that three large projects would be more resilient than a broader portfolio of smaller resources.

### 3. “Backup” and “backbone” imply different economics

There is an unresolved tension:

- **If the plants run only during shortages,** their construction and maintenance costs must be recovered from relatively little electricity—or taxpayers must cover the difference.
- **If they run frequently to recover those costs,** they are no longer merely emergency insurance, and their total emissions become much more consequential.

Greater efficiency than old coal does not resolve that trade-off. Nor does calling the public subsidy an “insurance premium”: the premium still needs to be compared with alternatives.

### 4. The locations have been selected before the need is demonstrated

Coal infrastructure and skilled workers are advantages, but they do not establish the best location for new generation.

Port Augusta is especially weak: the advocate concedes that its black-coal supply chain has not been demonstrated. None of the three proposals comes with evidence on required capacity, cooling-water availability, connection costs or local reliability benefits.

**Conclusion:** Government should procure measurable reliability, not commit beforehand to a technology and three locations. Independent assessment and competitive procurement should determine whether anything needs building—and what—not merely validate a coal-building decision already made.

## [3] ADVOCATE

## The strongest objection is cost—not whether reliability insurance matters

The critic is right that I have not demonstrated that these three plants would be the cheapest insurance. **My argument is that Australia should retain a limited new coal backbone rather than rely entirely on the successful delivery of a replacement system.** That is a strategic judgment, not a finding established by a project-level cost study.

### 1. A lowest-cost pathway does not settle the insurance question

[AEMO’s 2024 Integrated System Plan](https://aemo.com.au/energy-systems/major-publications/integrated-system-plan-isp/2024-integrated-system-plan-isp) supports renewables, transmission, storage and gas as the lowest-cost pathway. That deserves substantial weight.

But the decision here is also about the consequences of that pathway arriving late. The relevant comparison is:

> **What does each backup strategy cost, and how much protection does it provide under the same adverse conditions?**

The critic lists sensible alternatives, but has not established that they provide equivalent protection. Demand response has practical limits; storage must have sufficient energy available; gas generation needs secure fuel. Those are requirements to test—not reasons to reject those technologies.

Equally, coal needs its own fuel, water and delivery risks tested. My point is not that coal escapes project risk, but that **a different supply chain can diversify some risks rather than merely add more capacity exposed to them**.

### 2. Resolve the “backup versus backbone” tension explicitly

I would refine the proposal: these should be **replacement plants with a continuing supply role**, not expensive machines built solely for a few emergency hours.

They should replace retiring capacity, with plant size chosen to avoid unnecessary surplus. Their economics must be assessed at realistic operating levels—not by assuming uninterrupted, high-output operation regardless of renewable supply.

This does mean accepting significant emissions. Higher efficiency reduces that disadvantage; it does not eliminate it. The public case must therefore include emissions costs alongside reliability benefits. Calling the plants “insurance” is not permission to ignore either.

### 3. Keep the three-site proposal, but stage the commitment

I support building at Collinsville, the Hunter Valley and Port Augusta, with construction commitments following independently published feasibility assessments.

Port Augusta warrants the toughest scrutiny: **its geographic contribution is a rationale for investigation, not proof that black-coal delivery is viable**. No honest defence can promise cheap fuel, adequate cooling water or inexpensive connections without that work.

Staging also answers the construction-risk objection: these plants cannot solve an imminent shortage, and near-term measures remain necessary.

### Bottom line

The critic correctly demands comparison, but that requirement cuts both ways: a list of alternatives is not yet a demonstrated substitute for sustained, fuel-backed generation.

**Build the three plants as a limited, staged replacement backbone—not as a rejection of renewables, and not under an unlimited taxpayer guarantee.** The defensible case is diversification and protection against costly transition failures, with the price of that protection made explicit.

## [4] CRITIC

## Diversification is valuable—but it does not establish a case for three coal plants

The advocate’s strongest argument is that **a different fuel supply could protect Australia against some failures in the energy transition**. That is plausible. But a useful hedge must do more than differ from the main plan: it must cover identifiable risks at an acceptable cost.

### 1. “Different supply chain” is not the same as demonstrated protection

The replacement strategy already combines several technologies: renewables, transmission, storage and gas. It is not a single technology that either succeeds or fails.

Coal might add further diversity. But the advocate has not identified a scenario in which:
- those resources and targeted contingency measures fall short;
- the proposed coal plants are available in time; and
- their additional protection justifies their full cost.

The critic does not need to prove that every alternative is equivalent to coal. **The government needs evidence that the proposed coal investment improves the available portfolio enough to justify building it.** That is not a demand for the cheapest option regardless of reliability; it is a demand for demonstrated value.

### 2. A good hedge must also make sense if the transition succeeds

The proposal now involves plants with a “continuing supply role.” That sharpens an unresolved question: **what happens if renewable generation, transmission and storage arrive broadly as planned?**

Then the government could own new coal plants whose electricity is needed less often than expected. Smaller plants reduce the scale of that exposure, but do not establish viable economics.

This is an inference, not a project-level financial finding. Nevertheless, it is a necessary test: an insurance investment must be evaluated both when the feared disruption occurs **and when it does not**. Publishing an “insurance premium” does not show that the premium is worth paying.

### 3. Continuing coal generation creates a continuing emissions liability

[AEMO’s 2024 plan](https://aemo.com.au/energy-systems/major-publications/integrated-system-plan-isp/2024-integrated-system-plan-isp) identifies renewables supported by transmission, storage and gas as the lowest-cost replacement pathway.

Against that alternative, “cleaner than old coal” is the wrong emissions comparison. The relevant question is how much extra pollution these new plants would produce compared with the replacement system **while delivering comparable reliability**.

The advocate acknowledges significant emissions but has not demonstrated that the reliability benefit warrants them. Giving emissions a price in an assessment is useful; it does not make the underlying disadvantage disappear.

### 4. Genuine feasibility tests must permit rejection

Independent assessments are worthwhile only if they can conclude: **do not build here—or do not build coal at all**.

That matters especially at Port Augusta, where even the advocate treats viable black-coal supply as unproven. Geographic spread supports investigating local reliability needs, not selecting coal beforehand.

**Bottom line:** The argument supports testing coal against other contingency options—not committing to three plants. Government should buy demonstrated resilience, with permission to reject every proposed site.

## [5] ADVOCATE

## The key question is what failure Australia should insure against

The critic’s strongest objection is fair: **diversification alone does not establish that these three plants justify their cost.** My defence is narrower: Australia should build a limited coal replacement fleet to protect against a particular combination of risks—not because coal is universally cheaper or more reliable.

### 1. A diverse portfolio can still have shared vulnerabilities

Consider this **stress-test scenario, not a forecast**:

- Older coal stations have retired.
- A prolonged period of low wind and solar output affects several regions.
- Replacement storage or transmission projects are delayed.
- Gas generators face constrained fuel availability.

These are not four interchangeable sources of protection. Storage needs sufficient stored energy; transmission needs available generation elsewhere; gas turbines need fuel.

New coal plants with adequate on-site fuel stocks could provide sustained generation under that combination of conditions, **provided they have been commissioned and remain operational**. Their contribution would therefore be more specific than simply “another technology.”

The weakness in the critic’s reasoning is treating the existing portfolio’s technological variety as a sufficient answer to shared vulnerabilities. It is not. Equally, this scenario does not by itself prove that three coal plants are worth building; it identifies the protection that should be valued.

### 2. Success should not require keeping coal running unnecessarily

If the transition succeeds, the plants could operate less and their investment could earn a poor commercial return. That is a real public cost.

But **commercial utilisation is not the sole measure of a reserve asset’s value**. The relevant test is whether its construction, maintenance, fuel and environmental costs are justified by the shortages it could prevent.

I would therefore reject guaranteed electricity purchases designed to keep these plants busy. Public support should buy dependable availability, with:

- no guaranteed high operating level;
- explicit allowance for low-use years;
- maintenance and start-up requirements reflecting coal’s operational limitations;
- a published ceiling on the public commitment.

That makes the proposal harder to justify financially—but avoids disguising a coal-production subsidy as reliability insurance.

### 3. Emissions must constrain the programme

The critic is right that the comparison is with the replacement system, not simply old coal. I would add **an explicit emissions budget for the three-plant programme**, rather than merely attaching a hypothetical carbon price.

That would limit their continuing supply role. Ultra-supercritical efficiency would help within that constraint; it would not make their electricity clean.

### 4. Conditional construction is still a policy position

I support a directed, staged programme to build at Collinsville, Port Augusta and the Hunter Valley—not unconditional contracts regardless of engineering findings. Port Augusta’s fuel logistics remain the weakest element.

**My judgment is that retaining sustained, stockpiled-fuel generation across three regions merits that programme.** The unresolved question is the acceptable price—not whether the existing transition plan eliminates the need for this kind of protection.

## [6] CRITIC

## The stress test is legitimate—but it does not establish the solution

The advocate identifies a serious possibility: prolonged poor renewable output, delayed infrastructure and constrained gas supplies after older coal plants retire. **If commissioned, operational and adequately fuelled, new coal plants could help in that situation.** But that establishes a possible benefit—not a case for building these three plants.

### 1. Test competing safeguards, not just a deliberately weakened alternative

Technological diversity does not automatically guarantee reliability. On that point, we agree.

But the proposed stress test assumes several weaknesses in the replacement system while assuming the new coal programme has successfully overcome its construction and operating risks. A fair comparison must ask what happens if the same public funding instead secures gas availability, additional long-duration storage, demand-response contracts or some combination of safeguards.

Those alternatives are not guaranteed winners. **Neither is coal.** The missing analysis is which package reduces shortages most effectively across multiple plausible scenarios—not which technology survives one scenario constructed around its strengths.

### 2. “Available” coal requires a concrete operating plan

The latest proposal shifts back towards paying for availability rather than continuing electricity production. That leaves an important operational question unanswered:

**What condition will these plants be in when the shortage starts?**

The advocate acknowledges maintenance and start-up limitations. Procurement therefore needs to specify warning time, readiness, minimum operating requirements and how long dependable output can be sustained.

If readiness requires generation before a shortage, those costs and emissions count too. If units can remain shut down, their start-up requirements must fit the warning available.

This is not proof that coal cannot provide reserve capacity. It means that “stockpiled fuel” alone is not a complete reliability service.

### 3. An emissions budget creates another reliability constraint

An explicit emissions budget is better than ignoring pollution. But consider a year with repeated or unusually prolonged shortages:

- If the budget is binding, how much generation remains available for the next emergency?
- If it can be waived, what environmental protection does it actually guarantee?

This tension is manageable only through a quantified operating plan. The advocate cannot simultaneously treat coal output as dependable for extended emergencies and leave its permitted operating hours unresolved.

### 4. The unresolved question is not merely the acceptable price

It is also **whether these plants arrive when needed, deliver the required service, and outperform other uses of the same resources**. None of that has been established, particularly for Port Augusta.

A spending ceiling limits taxpayer exposure; it does not demonstrate value. Conditional construction is sensible only if the conditions can produce a decision to build none.

**Conclusion:** The advocate has made a case for rigorous contingency planning. Government should commission that comparison—not direct it towards three predetermined coal plants.

## [7] ADVOCATE

## The strongest objection is whether coal is the best use of the reliability budget

The critic is right: **a scenario in which coal helps is not enough to establish value for money.** The same funding might buy better protection through secure gas supplies, storage or demand response. I have not supplied a project-level comparison proving otherwise.

Nevertheless, I support the three-plant programme as a **limited, geographically distributed replacement fleet**, with a more tightly defined job than my earlier “backup” language suggested.

### 1. Specify sustained cover—not instant emergency response

These plants should not be advertised as machines that sit cold until an unexpected blackout.

Their proposed service should be **sustained generation during forecast periods of supply stress**. Contracts would specify:

- required notice before dependable output;
- readiness during identified high-risk periods;
- minimum fuel inventories;
- maintenance schedules and consequences for unavailability;
- all fuel use and emissions associated with preparation and start-up.

Fast-response resources would still handle sudden disturbances. The coal plants would address a different problem: keeping electricity available through an extended shortage.

That is a refinement of the proposal, not proof of its economics. But it answers the operational objection without pretending that stockpiled coal alone guarantees electricity.

### 2. Compare how safeguards fail—not merely how much capacity they add

Securing gas, adding storage and contracting demand response are legitimate competing investments. The weakness in treating them as an adequate reason to defer this programme is that **their own dependable service has yet to be specified**.

A gas supply contract must withstand physical supply constraints. Storage must cover the required duration. Demand response must establish how much consumption can actually stop, and for how long.

Coal likewise needs construction, fuel, water and outage risks assessed. My argument is not that it escapes those tests: it is that retaining another source of sustained generation could reduce the consequences of those other safeguards falling short.

**That remains a strategic judgment, not a demonstrated finding that coal wins the comparison.**

### 3. Make the emissions constraint honest

A hard emissions budget necessarily limits the protection these plants provide. I accept that.

The operating plan should reserve part of that budget for severe supply stress and count readiness emissions against it. Reliability assessments must then credit only the generation actually permitted—not unlimited emergency operation under an assumed waiver.

This means purchasing **bounded additional resilience**, not an absolute guarantee against every prolonged shortage.

### 4. Engineering safeguards do not invalidate a policy preference

A government can favour building infrastructure while allowing engineering findings to stop an unworkable project. That is not contradictory.

I favour Collinsville, the Hunter Valley and Port Augusta for a three-region programme, while acknowledging that **Port Augusta’s black-coal logistics remain an unresolved weakness**. Geographic spread is a rationale, not a completed business case.

**Bottom line:** proceed with a staged programme to build the three plants, without guaranteeing high utilisation or ignoring adverse feasibility findings. The defensible case is preserving a limited sustained-generation capability—not claiming coal is cheap, clean or infallible.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

The Australian government should build three new ultra-supercritical black coal power plants in **Collinsville, Port Augusta and the Hunter Valley**.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Insurance against transition risks:** With AEMO projecting that about 90% of existing coal capacity will retire by 2035, the Advocate argues for a limited new coal fleet alongside renewables, storage and gas—not instead of them.
- **Sustained, fuel-backed generation:** Plants with adequate on-site coal stocks could help during extended periods of low renewable output, delayed replacement infrastructure and constrained gas availability. A different fuel supply chain could diversify some risks.
- **Geographic spread:** Collinsville and the Hunter Valley offer coal infrastructure or proximity to coal production and skilled workers. Port Augusta offers geographic diversity and an opportunity to restore dispatchable generation in a former coal-power community, although its black-coal logistics remain unproven.
- **A staged, constrained programme:** The Advocate proposes independent feasibility assessments, competitive procurement, published costs, pollution controls and limits on public financial commitments.
- **A progressively narrower operating role:** By the final exchange, the proposed service is sustained generation during **forecast periods of supply stress**, not instant response from cold plants. There would be no guaranteed high utilisation, and a binding emissions budget would limit the resilience provided.

The Advocate repeatedly acknowledges that this is a **strategic judgment**, not a demonstrated project-level finding that these plants offer the best value.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **The need for contingency planning does not establish the need for these plants:** Government should procure measurable reliability rather than select coal and three locations beforehand.
- **Compare complete alternatives:** AEMO identifies renewables connected by transmission and backed by storage and gas as the lowest-cost replacement pathway. Coal should be compared with strengthened versions of that portfolio, including secure gas supplies, long-duration storage, demand response and safe, targeted extensions of existing capacity.
- **Coal has delivery and operating risks too:** No project-specific schedules, costs or assessments establish that the plants would arrive in time or provide the required service. Fuel supply, water, connections, approvals, financing and outages require examination.
- **Low use and frequent use create different liabilities:** Infrequent operation could leave taxpayers supporting costly, underused assets; frequent operation would increase emissions. The programme must also make sense if the transition proceeds broadly as planned.
- **Feasibility must permit rejection:** Independent assessment should be able to recommend rejecting any site—or building no new coal plants at all.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Lowest-cost pathway versus resilience:** The Advocate argues that a least-cost transition pathway does not settle how to insure against its delayed delivery. The Critic responds that this warrants comparative contingency analysis, not a prior commitment to coal.
- **Diversification versus demonstrated protection:** The Advocate presents a stress test combining low renewable output, infrastructure delays and gas constraints. The Critic accepts that operational, fuelled coal plants could help, but argues that fair testing must include coal’s own failures and competing uses of the same funding across multiple scenarios.
- **Burden of evidence:** The Advocate notes that alternatives also need to demonstrate dependable service. The Critic replies that proponents of the coal investment must establish its additional value; critics need not prove every alternative equivalent to coal.
- **Backup versus backbone:** After the Critic challenges the economics of rarely used plants, the Advocate first stresses a continuing replacement role, then refines the proposal toward availability for forecast, extended shortages without guaranteed electricity purchases.
- **Operational readiness:** The Critic argues that stockpiled fuel alone does not guarantee usable capacity. The Advocate responds with proposed requirements for notice periods, readiness, maintenance, fuel inventories and accounting for start-up emissions.
- **Emissions limits:** The Critic questions whether a binding emissions budget would restrict generation during repeated shortages. The Advocate accepts that constraint and says reliability assessments should count only permitted generation, including readiness emissions—not assume emergency waivers.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides agree that:

- Retiring coal capacity and delays in replacement projects create genuine reliability concerns.
- AEMO identifies renewables, transmission, storage and gas as the lowest-cost replacement pathway.
- Technological diversity alone does not guarantee reliability; every safeguard has limitations.
- New coal plants would take years and cannot resolve imminent shortages.
- Ultra-supercritical coal is more efficient than older coal generation but is **not low-carbon electricity**.
- Costs, emissions, delivery risks and operating requirements need explicit assessment.
- Port Augusta’s black-coal supply chain is an especially significant unresolved issue.
- The debate has not demonstrated through project-level comparison that the three plants offer the best use of reliability funding.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **Whether to proceed or compare first:** The Advocate favours a directed, staged building programme; the Critic favours technology-neutral assessment before any construction commitment.
- **Whether coal’s diversification value justifies investment:** The Advocate considers sustained, stockpiled-fuel generation strategically worth retaining. The Critic regards its incremental value as unproven.
- **What conditions may reject:** The Advocate allows adverse engineering findings to stop unworkable projects while retaining the three-site preference. The Critic insists the assessment must also be able to reject coal altogether on comparative value grounds.
- **Whether the proposed service is practically and economically adequate:** Delivery dates, readiness, capacity, utilisation, costs and emissions-constrained output remain unquantified.

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate centres on **how to insure Australia’s electricity transition against failure or delay**, rather than whether reliability matters. The Advocate supports three geographically distributed coal plants as a limited source of sustained generation, progressively adding operational, financial and emissions constraints. The Critic accepts the potential benefit but maintains that it does not establish that these plants are preferable to other safeguards.

The central unresolved issue is whether a strategic preference for coal-backed resilience justifies starting a directed programme before comparative analysis establishes its timely delivery, practical contribution and overall value.
