# A Multi Agent AI Policy Debate
AI can make mistakes. Always critically evaluate the content of the debate.

## Claim

Australian state governments should prohibit abortions performed solely on the basis of the fetus’s sex.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/pro-life

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

**Australian states should prohibit abortions performed solely because the fetus is male or female—but the prohibition should be narrow, directed at providers, and protect patients’ privacy.**

### 1. Sex alone should not determine whose birth is acceptable
There is an important difference between ending a pregnancy because someone does not want to continue it and ending it because they want a child **only of a particular sex**. In the latter case, sex itself is the disqualifying characteristic.

My argument is an ethical one: reproductive freedom deserves strong protection, but it need not require clinicians to provide sex-selection services. A prohibition would apply equally to selection against males or females, without depending on a broader argument against abortion.

### 2. Government should not endorse sex-based family hierarchies
Sex selection can reflect—and reinforce—the belief that sons and daughters have unequal worth. It can also expose pregnant people to pressure from partners or relatives who demand a child of a particular sex.

The [Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, Article 5](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-elimination-all-forms-discrimination-against-women) calls for action against prejudices based on the superiority or inferiority of either sex. That does **not** establish that an abortion prohibition is legally required; it supports the underlying equality principle.

### 3. A defensible prohibition must avoid policing ordinary abortion care
The strongest objection is that enforcing a motive-based ban could invite intrusive questioning, racial profiling, or delayed treatment. Those risks are real. The law should therefore:

- Not criminalise pregnant patients.
- Require clear evidence that sex preference was the **sole** reason—not assumptions based on ethnicity, family composition, or prenatal testing.
- Preserve access where health concerns, including sex-linked genetic conditions, or other reasons are involved.
- Avoid routine investigations into patients’ motives.

**Bottom line:** This is not a claim that sex-selective abortion is widespread in Australia, or that a ban would eliminate it. It is a narrower position: states should protect abortion access while refusing to make sex discrimination an accepted purpose of medical care.

## [2] CRITIC

**States can oppose sex discrimination without making a patient’s reason for an abortion a legal test.** The advocate offers a carefully limited ban, but has not shown that it would advance equality enough to justify restricting care.

### 1. The equality argument does not establish that prohibition is justified
The advocate’s strongest point is that rejecting a pregnancy solely because of fetal sex expresses an objectionable preference. But **disapproving of a reason is not enough to justify legally preventing an abortion**.

Allowing a medical procedure does not mean government endorses every motive behind it. Protecting abortion access can reflect respect for the pregnant person’s decision—not approval of their views about boys and girls.

Likewise, CEDAW’s requirement to combat sexist prejudice supports action against discrimination, but does not establish that this particular restriction is necessary or effective. That remains an unsupported step in the advocate’s argument.

### 2. A “sole reason” rule creates an enforcement dilemma
Consider two patients seeking otherwise lawful abortions: one openly states a sex preference; the other gives no reason.

- If clinicians accept silence and avoid investigating, the rule mainly disadvantages patients who disclose their motives.
- If clinicians must establish the real reason, the promised protection against intrusive questioning becomes difficult to maintain.

“Clear evidence” and provider-only penalties do not resolve this tension. **The patient need not face punishment to lose access:** a provider facing sanctions may refuse or delay care when uncertain. That is a foreseeable risk, not a claim about measured effects of this particular proposal.

### 3. Coercion calls for protection, not another imposed outcome
Pressure from relatives to abort because of fetal sex is a serious concern. But refusing an abortion does not itself remove that pressure or make someone safe.

Confidential consultations, support to make a voluntary decision, and help addressing threats or abuse target the coercion directly. A motive-based ban can instead leave a person caught between family demands and a legal restriction.

### 4. The evidence burden remains unmet
The [WHO’s *Abortion care guideline*](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240039483) recommends against laws restricting abortion by specified grounds. That is broader guidance, not an Australia-specific evaluation, but it weighs against assuming that motive restrictions are harmless.

The advocate has established an ethical concern—not that an Australian prohibition would reduce discrimination or coercion. **States should challenge sexist preferences and protect voluntary reproductive decisions, rather than make access depend on whether officials approve of the reason.**

## [3] ADVOCATE

**The critic’s strongest objection is the enforcement dilemma—not whether sex preference is objectionable.** A ban that turns ordinary abortion consultations into investigations would be unacceptable. But that supports a tightly limited prohibition, rather than necessarily abandoning one.

### 1. Target knowing participation, not suspicion
I would refine the proposal so that provider liability requires **actual knowledge that fetal sex is the sole reason**, with the burden on the regulator to prove it. The law should expressly impose:

- No duty to investigate motives or demand a justification.
- No adverse inference from silence, ethnicity, existing children, or prenatal testing.
- No liability merely because a clinician “should have suspected” sex selection.

This would leave some cases undetected. That is a genuine limitation. But incomplete enforcement does not make a rule pointless: it could still prohibit openly arranged sex-selection abortions without requiring surveillance of ordinary care.

The critic is right that disclosure could then affect access. My defence is not that this difference disappears, but that **a clinician’s knowing participation in sex selection is a relevant distinction**, whereas a patient’s background or an untested suspicion is not.

### 2. The justification is a boundary on medical practice—not government endorsement
The critic correctly says that permitting an abortion does not endorse every motive behind it. My earlier argument should not depend on that claim.

The stronger argument is that reproductive autonomy, although highly important, need not entitle someone to every requested use of medical services. States can reasonably distinguish between providing abortion generally and knowingly providing it **solely to secure a preferred-sex child**.

That is an ethical judgement about proportionality, not evidence that a ban would measurably reduce sexism. The critic has identified a real cost to autonomy, but has not established that autonomy must always override this narrowly drawn equality concern.

### 3. Safeguards and support remain essential
I accept that refusing an abortion does not remove family coercion. Confidential support and protection against abuse are necessary independently; prohibition must not be advertised as a substitute.

Likewise, WHO’s recommendation against grounds-based restrictions is a serious consideration against this proposal. It would be wrong to claim that the safeguards make all chilling effects impossible.

**The remaining disagreement is therefore narrow:** whether preventing clinicians’ knowing participation in sex-only selection justifies a limited restriction despite residual risks. I argue that it does—but only with explicit protections against investigative duties, patient punishment, and suspicion-based refusals. A broad or aggressively policed ban would not satisfy that argument.

## [4] CRITIC

**The revised safeguards improve the proposal, but they do not establish that its remaining restriction is justified.** The strongest objection is now to the rule itself—not merely to how aggressively it might be enforced.

### 1. “Knowing participation” identifies the prohibited conduct; it does not justify prohibiting it
The advocate’s strongest argument is that states may set ethical boundaries on medical practice, even when patients disagree. That is true in principle.

But **why should this particular boundary override an otherwise lawful abortion decision?** “The clinician knows the motive” answers an evidence question, not that ethical question. The procedure and its medical consequences are unchanged; what changes is the state’s judgement of the patient’s reason.

The critic need not prove that autonomy *always* wins. The advocate must explain why restricting it here is proportionate. An objectionable preference is a reason for concern, but not automatically a sufficient reason for compulsory continuation of a pregnancy.

### 2. Calling this a limit on “sex-selection services” understates what is withheld
The patient is not simply denied a guarantee that their next child will be a boy or girl. They are denied termination of an existing pregnancy.

That distinction matters. The practical stakes include continuing pregnancy and childbirth—not merely losing access to a preferred consumer service. Directing the prohibition at providers does not remove that consequence for patients.

The advocate acknowledges an autonomy cost but has not adequately weighed its seriousness against the proposed ethical benefit.

### 3. The safeguards leave an honesty penalty built into the law
Even assuming perfect compliance with every safeguard:

- A patient who keeps their preference private can receive care.
- An otherwise identical patient who candidly explains it cannot.

The advocate accepts this distinction. But it creates a further problem: **the conversation that could uncover sexist pressure or offer support becomes the conversation that can close off care.** Patients have a reason to conceal precisely what clinicians might usefully discuss with them.

That is an incentive created by the proposed rule, not a claim that its effects have been measured in Australia.

### 4. An ethical signal still needs a proportionate means
Laws can express values without producing easily measurable social change. But here the expressive benefit is uncertain while denial of care is the intended mechanism.

States can condemn sex-based family hierarchies and support voluntary decisions without conditioning abortion access on an approved motive. **The narrower proposal reduces some enforcement risks, but still has not shown why its equality message warrants that concrete restriction.**

## [5] ADVOCATE

**The hardest objection is that this rule can mean continuing an unwanted pregnancy—not merely losing a “sex-selection service.”** That is a serious bodily burden. My defence must therefore be stronger than “the state should send an equality message.”

### 1. The proposed benefit is preventing a practice, not just expressing disapproval
The relevant distinction is between **deciding whether to continue a pregnancy** and using abortion **solely to determine which sex of child is acceptable**. Those decisions overlap, but they are not ethically identical.

My position is that clinicians should not knowingly make being male or female the sole disqualifying condition for birth. This does not require treating a fetus as having all the rights of a born person. It rests on a narrower ethical judgement: sex-based selection is a use of reproductive medicine that states may legitimately exclude.

The critic says the procedure and medical consequences are unchanged. True—but that does not establish that its purpose is irrelevant. Medical ethics concerns both what clinicians do and what ends they knowingly serve. **The contested question is whether this particular purpose warrants a boundary, not whether purposes can ever matter.**

### 2. Why accept the substantial autonomy cost here?
Because, under the claim’s strict wording, the restriction applies only where sex preference supplies the *entire* reason. It does not authorise officials to rank someone’s health, financial, family or other reasons as insufficient.

That narrowness does not make the burden trivial. It does, however, distinguish this proposal from requiring patients generally to justify abortion. My ethical judgement is that preventing deliberate sex-only selection warrants this exceptional limit; disapproval of other people’s reproductive decisions generally does not.

This is a moral argument, **not evidence that a ban would reduce sexism across Australia**.

### 3. The honesty penalty is real—but discussing a preference must not trigger refusal
A patient saying “my family wants a boy” has not established that sex is their sole reason. Neither has expressing disappointment about fetal sex. The law should expressly protect exploratory conversations and disclosures of pressure from being treated, by themselves, as proof.

That cannot eliminate every incentive to conceal motives. It can preserve considerably more room for supportive discussion than a rule under which mentioning sex preference makes care legally suspect.

**Ultimately, alternatives such as counselling address prejudice and coercion, but do not prevent clinicians’ deliberate participation in sex-only selection.** If that participation is itself the ethical wrong—as I argue—education alone is not an equivalent response. A narrowly enforceable prohibition, alongside those supports, is justified despite its acknowledged cost.

## [6] CRITIC

**The advocate’s strongest argument is that medicine may legitimately refuse certain purposes, even when the procedure itself is lawful. I accept that principle. But the proposed boundary still needs a justification beyond defining sex-only selection as the wrong to be prevented.**

### 1. Preventing a practice is not automatically a public benefit
The advocate now distinguishes preventing sex selection from merely sending an equality message. But that does not resolve the central question: **why is preventing this practice important enough to justify denying an abortion?**

If the answer is harm to equality or reinforcement of prejudice, the connection—and the suitability of prohibition—needs defending. If the answer is that this purpose is inherently unacceptable regardless of its effects, that is a moral position, not an independently established justification for imposing the resulting bodily burden.

Calling the restriction a boundary on medical ethics does not settle which ethical consideration should prevail.

### 2. The “sole reason” exception weakens the underlying ethical argument
Consider two hypothetical patients:

- One seeks an abortion solely because the fetus is female.
- Another has the same sex preference, plus a financial concern.

Under the proposed safeguards, the first is refused while the second remains eligible—even if fetal sex is decisive for both.

That creates a tension: **if clinicians’ deliberate participation in sex selection is itself the wrong, an additional motive does not necessarily remove that participation.** Conversely, if other concerns justify allowing it, the advocate needs to explain why the bodily burden of continuing pregnancy does not provide a sufficient reason to leave the decision with the first patient too.

The narrow wording limits the ban’s reach, but makes it a less coherent response to the ethical wrong alleged.

### 3. A sex preference does not necessarily establish a hierarchy of worth
Someone wanting a daughter after several sons may hold stereotyped views, but it does not follow that they believe males are inferior. The proposal covers that case as well as selection driven by explicit contempt for girls.

These motives can all be criticised. But treating them as equally sufficient grounds for denying care assumes that **every sex-only preference raises an equality concern strong enough to override reproductive choice**. The advocate has asserted that conclusion rather than established it.

### 4. Alternatives need not eliminate every objectionable choice
Counselling and protection against coercion will not prevent all voluntary sex selection. That is not, by itself, an argument against them. A less restrictive approach can be preferable precisely because it addresses prejudice without ensuring that every morally disputed decision is stopped.

**States should oppose sexist pressure and support voluntary decisions. The remaining possibility of an objectionable private choice does not establish a duty to prohibit the abortion.**

## [7] ADVOCATE

**The critic’s strongest challenge is proportionality: why should preventing sex-only selection outweigh the bodily burden of continuing a pregnancy?** Narrow drafting cannot answer that by itself. The justification must explain both the equality concern and why other reasons remain protected.

### 1. The ethical boundary needs refining
My earlier description—preventing clinicians’ “participation in sex selection”—was too broad. A more defensible principle is:

**Clinicians should not knowingly make fetal sex alone sufficient grounds for terminating a pregnancy, while patients retain access when other reasons genuinely contribute to their decision.**

That answers the mixed-motives objection. In the financial-concern example, permitting care does not mean the sex preference becomes unobjectionable. It means avoiding government judgement of the patient’s financial circumstances takes priority.

This is a compromise between equality and autonomy, not a claim that an additional motive magically removes discrimination. The critic identifies a limit to the principle’s reach, but not necessarily an inconsistency: a law can exclude one justification without excluding decisions supported by other protected considerations.

### 2. “Family balancing” need not imply contempt to raise an equality concern
I accept that wanting a daughter after several sons does not establish a belief that males are inferior. My argument should not assume otherwise.

The narrower concern is **making an individual’s acceptability depend entirely on sex**. A preference can do that without expressing hatred or a general hierarchy. Wanting a child of a particular sex may also assign that prospective child a role that their sex cannot guarantee they will fulfil.

That does not make every parental preference unlawful. It explains why sex-only exclusion raises a concern beyond simply having an unpopular reason.

### 3. The bodily burden is real; the disagreement is about its priority
The physical burdens of pregnancy remain substantial even when they are not the patient’s stated reason. I am not treating willingness to carry a different-sex fetus as consent to carry this one.

Nevertheless, my position is that reproductive autonomy should protect decisions about continuing pregnancy **without extending without exception to deliberate sex-only selection**. The narrow prohibition preserves that distinction while leaving health, financial, family and other contributing reasons outside its scope.

This is a contested moral judgement—not a demonstrated finding that prohibition produces better population outcomes. But the critic’s preferred balance also rests on a moral judgement: that autonomy should prevail here. Evidence can inform the risks; it cannot alone decide that priority.

### 4. Alternatives address a different part of the problem
Counselling can challenge prejudice and protect voluntary choice. It cannot establish the proposed boundary on clinicians’ knowing conduct.

**My case is therefore for a limited legal boundary, not comprehensive enforcement of acceptable attitudes:** no patient penalties, no motive investigations, and no refusal based merely on suspicion or disclosure of family pressure. The remaining autonomy cost is serious, but I judge preventing sex alone from becoming an accepted clinical basis for exclusion sufficient to justify that narrow restriction.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

**Australian state governments should prohibit abortions performed solely on the basis of the fetus’s sex.**

The Advocate supports a narrow, provider-directed prohibition with strong protections for patients. The Critic opposes making a patient’s motive a legal condition of abortion access.

## 2. Advocate’s Main Arguments

- **Sex alone should not determine whether a pregnancy is terminated.** The Advocate distinguishes choosing not to continue a pregnancy from seeking a child only of a particular sex. They argue that clinicians should not knowingly treat fetal sex alone as sufficient grounds for abortion.
- **An ethical boundary on medical practice can coexist with reproductive autonomy.** The Advocate accepts that autonomy is highly important but argues that it need not protect every requested purpose of medical care. Their case does not depend on granting fetuses all the rights of born people.
- **Sex-only selection raises equality concerns.** It can reflect sexist family hierarchies or pressure from relatives. Even “family balancing” without contempt for either sex, the Advocate argues, makes a prospective child’s acceptability depend entirely on sex.
- **The prohibition should be tightly limited.** The final proposal requires actual provider knowledge that sex preference is the sole reason, with the regulator bearing the burden of proof. It includes:
  - No penalties for pregnant patients.
  - No duty to investigate motives or demand justification.
  - No adverse inferences from silence, ethnicity, existing children, or prenatal testing.
  - No suspicion-based liability or refusals.
  - Continued access when health, financial, family, or other reasons genuinely contribute.
  - Protection for exploratory conversations and disclosures of family pressure, which alone would not establish a prohibited motive.
- **Support measures should accompany, not replace, the boundary.** Counselling and protection against abuse address prejudice and coercion, but do not prevent clinicians’ knowing participation in sex-only selection.
- **The justification is principally moral.** The Advocate does not claim the practice is widespread in Australia or that prohibition would demonstrably reduce sexism.

## 3. Critic’s Main Arguments

- **An objectionable motive does not automatically justify denying abortion.** The Critic accepts that sex preferences can warrant criticism, but argues that the Advocate has not shown why this concern should outweigh reproductive autonomy.
- **The consequences extend beyond refusing a medical “service.”** Denial can mean continuing an unwanted pregnancy and undergoing childbirth. Provider-only penalties do not remove those consequences for patients.
- **A sole-reason rule creates an enforcement dilemma.** Avoiding investigation leaves undisclosed motives undetected; investigating motives threatens privacy and access. Even narrow rules may encourage providers to delay or refuse care when uncertain.
- **The proposal creates an “honesty penalty.”** A patient who conceals a sex-only motive may receive care, while an otherwise identical patient who discloses it may not. This can discourage conversations that might reveal coercion or enable support.
- **The equality rationale requires further justification.** A preference for a daughter after several sons does not necessarily imply that males are inferior. The Critic questions whether every sex-only preference warrants overriding reproductive choice.
- **Mixed motives expose a tension.** Allowing an abortion when financial concerns accompany sex preference may still permit deliberate sex selection. The Critic argues that narrowness alone does not establish ethical coherence or proportionality.
- **Less restrictive responses remain preferable.** Confidential support, counselling, and protection against threats can address prejudice and coercion without requiring the prevention of every morally disputed choice.
- **The evidence burden remains unmet.** The Critic cites WHO guidance against grounds-based abortion restrictions, while acknowledging that it is not an Australia-specific evaluation.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Permission versus endorsement:** The Critic argues that allowing abortion does not mean government endorses the patient’s motive. The Advocate accepts this correction and reframes the case around permissible boundaries on clinical practice.
- **Enforcement versus actual knowledge:** The Advocate responds to concerns about intrusive enforcement by requiring actual knowledge, prohibiting investigative duties, and accepting incomplete enforcement. The Critic replies that this still leaves disclosure-based denial and possible chilling effects.
- **Ethical boundary versus proportionality:** The Critic accepts that medicine may legitimately reject some purposes, but argues that identifying the prohibited purpose does not explain why it should outweigh the bodily burden. The Advocate acknowledges that burden and maintains that preventing sex alone from becoming a clinical basis for exclusion justifies an exceptional restriction.
- **Mixed motives:** The Advocate concedes that their earlier objection to all “participation in sex selection” was too broad. Their refined position permits care when other reasons genuinely contribute, describing this as a compromise that avoids government assessment of those reasons. The Critic’s objection remains that the distinction weakens the underlying rationale.
- **Honesty and supportive discussion:** The Advocate accepts that incentives to conceal cannot be eliminated, but argues that mentioning disappointment or family pressure must not itself trigger refusal.
- **Alternatives:** The Critic argues that alternatives need not eliminate all objectionable choices to be preferable. The Advocate responds that they do not achieve the specific boundary on knowing clinical conduct being defended.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides acknowledge that:

- Sexist prejudice and family coercion are legitimate concerns.
- Confidential support and protection against abuse are important.
- Refusing an abortion does not itself remove coercion.
- Intrusive questioning, profiling, and suspicion-based restrictions are unacceptable.
- Medical practice can, in principle, have ethical limits.
- Denial of abortion can impose a serious bodily and autonomy burden.
- Legal permission does not necessarily endorse a patient’s motives.
- The debate has not established that an Australian prohibition would improve population-level outcomes.

The Advocate also accepts that WHO guidance is a serious consideration against the proposal and that safeguards cannot eliminate every chilling effect.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **Proportionality:** Does preventing sex-only selection justify denying an otherwise lawful abortion?
- **Equality:** Does every sole-sex preference raise a sufficiently strong equality concern, including preferences without a belief in either sex’s inferiority?
- **Mixed motives:** Is protecting decisions with additional contributing reasons a defensible compromise or a weakness in the prohibition’s ethical logic?
- **Disclosure and enforcement:** Are the remaining honesty penalty and access risks acceptable under an actual-knowledge rule?
- **Alternatives:** Is counselling and protection of voluntary choice sufficient, or must states also prohibit clinicians’ knowing provision of sex-only abortions?
- **Justification:** Can the proposed moral boundary justify the restriction without demonstrated broader benefits?

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate moves from a general equality-based case for prohibition to a narrowly defined proposal targeting providers who actually know that fetal sex is the sole reason for abortion. The Advocate progressively accepts significant objections and refines the proposal, while maintaining that this purpose warrants a limited legal boundary.

The Critic acknowledges those refinements but argues that they do not resolve the central issue: why an objectionable sex preference should justify imposing the consequences of denied abortion. The remaining disagreement concerns the relative weight of equality-based limits on clinical practice and reproductive autonomy, alongside the proposal’s disclosure incentives and residual access risks.
