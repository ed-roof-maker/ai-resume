## Claim

Australia should amend its Constitution through a referendum to explicitly protect freedom of speech.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/free-speech-policy

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

## Australia should protect free speech explicitly—not leave it to implication

**The strongest case is simple: governments should not have the final say over the speech used to criticise them.** A constitutional guarantee would establish a lasting safeguard while allowing carefully justified limits.

### 1. Australia’s current protection leaves a real gap
The High Court recognises an *implied freedom of political communication*. But it is a restriction on government power needed for representative democracy—not a general personal right to free speech. Its coverage turns on the connection to political communication, rather than protecting expression as such. See [*Lange v ABC* (1997)](https://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/cases/cth/HCA/1997/25.html).

An explicit guarantee could protect a broader range of expression, including artistic, scientific, religious and personal speech, without requiring that political connection.

### 2. Ordinary laws cannot provide the same security
Parliament can strengthen speech protections today—and a future parliament can weaken them. Constitutional protection would instead give people a durable basis to challenge unjustified federal, state and territory restrictions.

That matters most for unpopular speakers. Popular opinions rarely need legal protection; whistleblowers, dissenters and minority groups do. **This is insurance against abuses of power, not an accusation that every current government is censorious.**

### 3. Protecting speech does not mean permitting every harm
Australia need not copy an absolutist model. A proposed amendment should expressly permit restrictions that are lawful, necessary and proportionate—for example, to address credible threats, incitement to violence or serious invasions of privacy.

The [International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, Article 19](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-civil-and-political-rights) offers a useful starting point: protect expression while requiring restrictions to meet defined conditions.

The practical change would be **requiring governments to justify restrictions**, rather than treating discomfort or offence alone as sufficient.

### 4. A referendum gives the safeguard democratic authority
Courts would still interpret the provision, but voters would approve its words and boundaries. That is a stronger foundation than relying entirely on judicial inference.

**The choice is not free speech versus public safety. It is whether Australians should have an explicit, enforceable guarantee that governments may restrict expression only for sufficiently strong reasons. They should.**

## [2] CRITIC

## Protect speech—but constitutional entrenchment is not necessarily the best protection

The advocate’s strongest point is durability: **a future parliament could repeal ordinary speech protections.** That is true. But making a rule harder to change also makes its flaws harder to fix. The question is whether a constitutional guarantee would produce better outcomes—not simply whether it would last longer.

### 1. The protection gap does not establish the remedy
The advocate correctly explains that Australia’s implied freedom protects political communication rather than creating a general personal right. But that distinction alone does not demonstrate that a broad constitutional amendment is necessary.

Indeed, the existing freedom already targets the advocate’s central concern: government interference with communication needed for democratic accountability. See [*Lange v ABC*](https://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/cases/cth/HCA/1997/25.html).

Extending constitutional protection to other expression might be beneficial. But the advocate has not identified which unjustified restrictions require constitutional—not legislative—reform.

### 2. “Necessary and proportionate” does not settle the hard questions
Allowing justified restrictions is much more reasonable than protecting speech absolutely. Nevertheless, someone must decide what counts as justified.

Consider misleading advertising, publication of private information, or speech that intimidates without explicitly threatening violence. A proportionality clause would leave courts to decide how much protection each deserves.

**The proposal would not remove contested value judgments; it would give judges greater authority to settle them.** That may sometimes protect minorities, but it is not automatically better than accountable, revisable lawmaking.

### 3. Entrenching speech alone risks an uneven balance
Privacy, reputation and equality also matter. If speech receives an explicit constitutional guarantee while competing interests remain protected mainly through ordinary legislation, challenges would centre on whether those interests justify restricting the constitutional right.

That does not guarantee bad judgments. It does mean the amendment’s wording and its relationship to other rights are crucial—not details to resolve after endorsing the proposal.

The advocate’s [ICCPR reference](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/international-covenant-civil-and-political-rights) supports protecting expression, but does **not** establish that constitutional entrenchment is required. Article 2 expressly contemplates legislative or other implementation measures.

### 4. Democratic approval requires an actual proposal
A referendum can authorise a constitutional rule. It cannot eliminate ambiguity about its future application. Without proposed wording, Australians cannot assess its scope, exceptions or consequences.

**The better course is to identify concrete speech restrictions needing reform and strengthen statutory safeguards first.** Constitutional permanence is a powerful tool, but the advocate has not shown that its additional benefits outweigh its risks.

## [3] ADVOCATE

## Entrench the safeguard—not every answer to a speech dispute

**The critic’s strongest objection is that a poorly drafted guarantee could freeze mistakes into the Constitution and give courts excessive discretion.** That is a reason to define the proposal carefully, not to leave speech entirely dependent on protections Parliament can withdraw.

### 1. Protect a principle while leaving ordinary laws adaptable

I would support an amendment along these lines—not as final legal wording:

> Everyone has freedom of expression. Commonwealth, state and territory laws and government actions may restrict that freedom only through lawful, necessary and proportionate measures serving specified legitimate purposes, including protecting others’ rights and reputations, public safety and national security.

The text should expressly recognise privacy and equality among those competing rights.

This would **not constitutionalise the details of defamation, advertising or privacy law**. Parliament could keep revising those laws as circumstances change. What it could not readily remove is the requirement to justify restricting expression.

The critic treats durability chiefly as a risk. But the relevant distinction is between entrenching a particular policy and entrenching a safeguard against unjustified policies.

### 2. Judicial judgment is a cost—but legislative judgment has risks too

“Necessary and proportionate” does not eliminate disagreement. Agreed. The choice, however, is not between imperfect judges and neutral lawmakers.

Governments can have a direct interest in suppressing embarrassing information or unpopular criticism. **Independent review matters precisely because the institution restricting speech should not always be its own final judge.** Parliament would still make policy; courts would assess whether its restrictions meet the voter-approved standard.

That arrangement is workable in principle: Canada’s constitutional framework combines freedom of expression with “reasonable limits” that must be “demonstrably justified.” See [Canadian Charter, sections 1–2](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/const/page-12.html). This establishes a drafting precedent, not proof that every Canadian outcome should be copied.

### 3. Statutory reform does not answer the central vulnerability

The critic rightly asks for concrete problems. But requiring a catalogue of current abuses sets too narrow a test for a constitutional safeguard.

Consider a **hypothetical** future law banning non-political artwork solely because its viewpoint offends the governing majority. The existing political-communication protection would not necessarily cover it. A statutory protection could be overridden or repealed by the same legislature.

That illustrates the distinct benefit: broader protection that the restricting government cannot simply switch off. Statutory reform remains worthwhile, but it cannot supply that feature.

### 4. Support the reform, scrutinise the wording

The ICCPR does not require constitutional entrenchment; my argument is that entrenchment offers additional security, not that international law mandates it.

Nor should Australians approve an unspecified amendment. Drafting, consultation and scrutiny must precede a referendum. **The case is for putting a carefully bounded guarantee to voters—not for accepting any proposal labelled “free speech.”**

## [4] CRITIC

## A safeguard can be valuable without belonging in the Constitution

**The advocate’s strongest argument is genuine: a statutory right cannot stop a future parliament determined to repeal it.** But that establishes a benefit of entrenchment—not that the benefit outweighs the consequences of giving courts a broad constitutional veto over speech-related laws.

### 1. Calling it a “safeguard” does not avoid entrenching policy choices

The proposed wording would leave Parliament free to revise laws **only within boundaries ultimately determined by courts**.

For example, must a privacy law require proof of serious harm before stopping publication? How much evidence must Parliament produce before restricting harmful advertising? Under a “necessary and proportionate” test, these could become constitutional questions rather than ordinary policy disputes.

The unsupported assumption is that courts would enforce an agreed minimum while leaving disputed details to Parliament. **The minimum and the details cannot always be separated.** A constitutional judgment could rule out a legislative approach, leaving voters unable to restore it through an ordinary election.

### 2. Canada illustrates a trade-off, not a ready-made solution

The Canadian example needs an important qualification. Its Charter includes **section 33**, which allows legislatures expressly to override certain Charter rights—including freedom of expression—for renewable periods of up to five years. See the [Canadian Charter, sections 2 and 33](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/const/page-12.html).

Australia would therefore face a choice the advocate has not resolved:

- **Include a comparable override:** a determined government could still bypass the speech guarantee.
- **Exclude it:** give courts stronger final authority than this feature of the Canadian model allows.

Neither choice is automatically unacceptable. But citing Canada does not settle which balance Australia should adopt.

### 3. The hypothetical proves possibility, not priority

A future ban on non-political artwork illustrates something an amendment *might* prevent. It does not establish how much additional protection Australians would gain in practice, or whether this particular wording would deliver it.

The draft also permits restrictions for national security, public safety and others’ rights. A government seeking to suppress expression could invoke those purposes. Courts might reject its justification—or accept it.

That is not an argument that constitutional rights are useless. It means **their value depends on judicial interpretation, not merely on their permanence**. The advocate has demonstrated stronger legal machinery, not reliably better outcomes.

### 4. Prefer reforms whose operation can be tested and corrected

Targeted speech protections and stronger scrutiny of restrictive laws can address identifiable problems while remaining revisable. Their vulnerability to repeal is real; so is their capacity for correction.

**Australia should not constitutionalise a broad judicial balancing power merely because legislation cannot provide permanent insurance.** On the case presented, the additional security remains too uncertain to justify that institutional transfer.

## [5] ADVOCATE

## The judicial trade-off is real—and worth making within clear limits

**The critic’s strongest objection is that courts would sometimes prevent Parliament from adopting a speech-related policy, even when voters support it. I accept that consequence.** A constitutional right that never constrains an elected majority offers little protection against majority-backed censorship.

The question is how broad that constraint should be—not whether it can be avoided altogether.

### 1. Refine the guarantee to protect legislative choice

The critic correctly says that “principle” and “policy detail” cannot always be separated. My earlier distinction was too neat.

The amendment should therefore make clear that:
- Protecting privacy, reputation, equality and public safety can justify restrictions.
- Courts must assess whether restrictions are justified, **not whether judges would have chosen a different policy**.
- Parliament retains a reasonable range of policy choices; it need not prove that its approach is the only imaginable solution.

Those principles would require careful legal drafting. They would not eliminate difficult judgments. But they identify the intended boundary: **exclude unjustified restrictions without requiring judges to redesign every advertising or privacy law.**

### 2. Resolve the override question: no ordinary parliamentary bypass

Canada’s override is an important qualification. My reference established that express speech protection can coexist with justified limits—not that Australia should copy the whole Canadian arrangement.

**I favour a guarantee without an ordinary legislative override.** Otherwise, a government could bypass the safeguard precisely when protecting unpopular expression mattered most.

That gives courts consequential authority. But the authority would concern restrictions on expression under a defined constitutional standard—not a general power to reject policies they dislike. Its boundaries would come from the amendment Australians approved.

### 3. Invoking “national security” would not settle the case

The critic argues that governments could invoke the permitted exceptions. Certainly—but **offering a justification and establishing it are different things**.

Under the proposed guarantee, a government defending a restriction would need to explain its connection to the claimed harm and why a substantially less restrictive, reasonably available approach would not suffice. Courts could still reach mistaken conclusions. Nevertheless, requiring a defensible justification is a meaningful protection compared with allowing a restriction merely because Parliament enacted it.

This is an institutional argument, not a claim that constitutional litigation guarantees good outcomes.

### 4. Revisability cuts both ways

The critic recommends statutory protections because mistakes can be corrected. But the same flexibility allows sound protections to be dismantled. “Try legislation first” supplies no answer to **when the protection should cease to depend on the goodwill of the institution it constrains**.

The additional security cannot be precisely quantified in advance. Neither can the future benefits of leaving Parliament unrestricted. Uncertainty alone does not favour the status quo.

**Australia should preserve legislative flexibility over how to address harms, while constitutionally removing the option of restricting expression without sufficient justification.** That is a limited but valuable transfer of authority—and a sound reason to put a carefully drafted guarantee to a referendum.

## [6] CRITIC

## A reasonable aim—but still too broad a constitutional commitment

**The advocate’s strongest point is that protection against censorship should not disappear whenever a government finds it inconvenient.** But the latest proposal goes further: it gives courts final authority over restrictions on *all expression*, without a parliamentary override. The case for that breadth remains unproven.

### 1. The proposed restraint on judges does not resolve the central tension

The advocate would let Parliament choose among reasonable policies, while requiring it to explain why a substantially less restrictive alternative would not suffice.

Those principles can coexist—but they pull in different directions. Suppose Parliament limits targeted advertising to children. Challengers argue that warnings or parental controls would be sufficient. Who decides whether those alternatives work well enough, and how much additional protection children deserve?

Under this proposal, courts would ultimately resolve that dispute. Telling judges not to substitute their policy preferences does not settle **how closely they should second-guess Parliament’s evidence and predictions**.

The unsupported assumption is that drafting can reliably preserve broad legislative choice while delivering strong judicial intervention whenever intervention is desirable.

### 2. Protection for dissent also creates opportunities to challenge protective laws

The advocate’s examples centre on unpopular artists and government critics. But “everyone has freedom of expression” is not confined to those cases.

Depending on the wording, businesses could also invoke it against advertising restrictions or mandatory disclosures. That would not mean they would win. It means the amendment could create a constitutional battleground over ordinary consumer regulation.

**This is a foreseeable implication, not a prediction of inevitable abuse.** The advocate must justify that wider reach—not merely the compelling cases of censorship used to sell the proposal.

### 3. A constitutional defence is not a complete protection system

An enforceable right matters only if people can use it. The proposal does not itself provide affordable representation, quick remedies or protection against retaliation by private employers.

For example, constitutional protection against government restrictions would not automatically protect a worker dismissed by a private employer for speaking out. That depends on the amendment’s reach and accompanying legislation.

This matters because the advocate invokes vulnerable speakers as the main beneficiaries. **Legal assistance, whistleblower protections and accessible remedies may help them more directly.** Constitutional entrenchment does not substitute for those measures.

### 4. There is a sensible threshold for reconsidering entrenchment

The advocate asks when protection should stop depending on Parliament. A reasonable answer is: when there is a demonstrated pattern of serious failures that narrower safeguards cannot adequately address, and a sufficiently settled proposal showing how constitutional protection would improve matters.

That is not demanding certainty or waiting for catastrophe. It is demanding evidence proportionate to a difficult-to-reverse institutional change.

**Australia should strengthen practical speech protections now. The argument presented does not yet justify turning this broad range of disputes into constitutional questions.**

## [7] ADVOCATE

## Protect expression without constitutionalising every advertising dispute

**The critic’s strongest objection is about scope:** a guarantee promoted as protection for dissent could also turn routine consumer regulation into constitutional litigation. That deserves a concrete drafting response—not another assurance that judges will exercise restraint.

### 1. Narrow the proposal where the objection is strongest

I would refine the proposed guarantee to **exclude purely commercial advertising**, with an accompanying clarification preserving ordinary requirements for factual consumer disclosures.

That would leave Parliament responsible for decisions such as restricting advertisements aimed at children. But a business’s criticism of government policy would not lose protection simply because the speaker is a company.

The boundary would sometimes require interpretation; no constitutional wording eliminates that. Nevertheless, this substantially reduces the particular risk the critic identifies while retaining protection for artistic, scientific, religious and personal expression.

**An explicit freedom need not cover every category of communication equally—or copy the broadest overseas model.**

### 2. Judicial review is a real constraint, not necessarily judicial policymaking

For expression within the guarantee, courts would still decide whether restrictions are justified. I am not claiming drafting can reliably produce intervention only when desirable.

But consider the distinction between two questions:

- **Policy choice:** Which measure would best protect people?
- **Constitutional review:** Has government adequately justified this restriction on protected expression?

The second can respect reasonable legislative predictions without accepting unsupported assertions. Where evidence is genuinely uncertain, courts should allow a reasonable range of responses—not require proof beyond doubt.

Australia already has experience with courts reviewing speech restrictions. In [*Brown v Tasmania* (2017)](https://www.austlii.edu.au/cgi-bin/viewdoc/au/cases/cth/HCA/2017/43.html), the High Court held provisions of Tasmania’s anti-protest legislation invalid under the implied freedom.

**That establishes that constitutional review can provide a concrete remedy.** It does not prove that every broader guarantee would work well. My inference is narrower: an explicit guarantee would build on an existing institutional practice, rather than introduce an entirely foreign judicial role.

### 3. Practical protection and constitutional protection solve different problems

The critic is right that a constitutional right would not automatically provide affordable lawyers or protect employees against private retaliation.

Those are reasons to accompany reform with legal assistance, accessible remedies and whistleblower legislation. They are not reasons to reject the constitutional foundation.

A worker may need statutory protection against dismissal; an artist may need constitutional protection against a government ban. **Neither safeguard substitutes for the other.** My proposal concerns government restrictions, not a general right to speak without private consequences.

### 4. The critic’s threshold undervalues prevention

Requiring a demonstrated pattern of serious failures before entrenchment makes repeated harm a prerequisite for preventive protection. The critic has not established why that is the right threshold.

A more appropriate test is whether the vulnerability is identifiable, the safeguard addresses it, and its institutional costs can be reasonably bounded. Here, the vulnerability is clear: expression outside political communication lacks that constitutional protection, and ordinary protections remain removable by the government they constrain.

**The defensible proposal is therefore a bounded constitutional guarantee, supported by practical legislation—not constitutional litigation as the answer to every speech problem.** That is a worthwhile reform to develop carefully and put to voters.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

**Australia should amend its Constitution through a referendum to explicitly protect freedom of speech.**

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Existing protection is limited.** The implied freedom of political communication constrains government power but is not a general personal right. An explicit guarantee could also protect artistic, scientific, religious and personal expression.
- **Constitutional protection offers durability.** Ordinary statutory safeguards can be repealed by the government they constrain. Entrenchment would provide lasting protection, particularly for unpopular speakers, dissenters and minorities.
- **Speech protection can accommodate justified limits.** The Advocate proposes allowing lawful, necessary and proportionate restrictions for specified purposes, including privacy, reputation, equality, public safety and national security.
- **Independent review addresses government conflicts of interest.** Governments may have incentives to suppress criticism. Courts should therefore be able to require adequate justification for restrictions, even where those restrictions have majority support.
- **Referendum approval would provide democratic authority.** Voters would approve the guarantee’s boundaries following drafting, consultation and scrutiny.
- **Constitutional and practical protections are complementary.** Legal assistance, accessible remedies and whistleblower legislation would still be needed; the amendment would address government restrictions rather than guarantee freedom from private consequences.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **A protection gap does not establish the need for constitutional reform.** The existing implied freedom already addresses democratic accountability. The Advocate has not demonstrated which additional problems require constitutional rather than legislative remedies.
- **Durability also makes mistakes harder to correct.** Constitutional judgments could exclude legislative approaches that voters could not restore through an ordinary election.
- **Proportionality transfers consequential decisions to courts.** Judges would ultimately assess evidence, competing rights and alternative policies. Instructions to respect legislative choice would not eliminate uncertainty over how closely courts should scrutinise Parliament.
- **A broad guarantee could reach beyond dissent.** Businesses might challenge advertising restrictions or mandatory disclosures, creating constitutional disputes over ordinary consumer regulation.
- **Practical benefits are uncertain.** Constitutional rights depend on judicial interpretation and do not themselves provide affordable representation, quick remedies or protection against private retaliation.
- **Narrower, revisable reforms should come first.** The Critic favours targeted safeguards and stronger scrutiny of restrictive laws, with entrenchment reconsidered after demonstrated serious failures and development of a sufficiently settled proposal.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Safeguard versus entrenched policy:** The Advocate initially distinguished entrenching a justification requirement from freezing detailed policies. The Critic argued that constitutional principles and policy details cannot always be separated. The Advocate accepted that qualification and proposed preserving a reasonable range of legislative choices.
- **Canada and parliamentary override:** The Advocate cited Canada as a precedent for combining expression rights with justified limits. The Critic noted Canada’s legislative override mechanism. The Advocate then explicitly favoured **no ordinary parliamentary override**, accepting the resulting authority for courts.
- **Permitted exceptions:** The Critic argued that governments could invoke national security or other exceptions and courts might accept them. The Advocate responded that invoking a purpose would not establish justification: governments would need to explain the harm and why reasonably available, substantially less restrictive measures would not suffice.
- **Commercial expression:** In response to concerns about consumer regulation, the Advocate narrowed the proposal to **exclude purely commercial advertising and preserve ordinary factual consumer-disclosure requirements**, while retaining protection for companies’ criticism of government policy.
- **Evidence versus prevention:** The Critic treated the hypothetical ban on offensive non-political artwork as demonstrating possibility, not sufficient practical benefit. The Advocate argued that preventive safeguards should not require a prior pattern of serious failures.
- **Existing judicial practice:** The Advocate cited *Brown v Tasmania* as an example of constitutional review providing a remedy against speech restrictions, while acknowledging that it did not prove a broader guarantee would work well.
- **Practical accessibility:** The Advocate accepted that constitutional protection would not automatically solve litigation costs or private retaliation, but argued that accompanying legislation could address those separate problems.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides accept that:

- Freedom of expression deserves protection, but need not be absolute.
- The implied freedom is not a general personal speech right.
- Statutory safeguards can be repealed.
- Privacy, reputation, equality and public safety are legitimate competing interests.
- Constitutional wording, scope and exceptions require careful scrutiny.
- Judicial interpretation involves difficult judgments and cannot guarantee good outcomes.
- The ICCPR does not require constitutional entrenchment.
- Practical protections remain important alongside any constitutional framework.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **Whether additional security outweighs institutional costs:** The Advocate sees durable independent review as worthwhile; the Critic considers its benefits insufficiently demonstrated.
- **How much authority courts should have:** The Advocate accepts judicial constraints on elected majorities without an ordinary override. The Critic questions that transfer across a broad range of expression.
- **Whether drafting can adequately bound the guarantee:** The Advocate proposes exclusions and legislative latitude; the Critic doubts that general standards can reliably prevent extensive judicial involvement in policy.
- **What evidence should precede reform:** The Advocate prioritises identifiable vulnerability and prevention. The Critic seeks demonstrated failures that narrower safeguards cannot adequately address.
- **Whether legislation should supplement or precede entrenchment:** The Advocate supports both together; the Critic favours testing and correcting targeted reforms first.

The transcript ends with the Advocate’s narrowed proposal, without a further Critic response to those final refinements.

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate centres on how best to protect expression while balancing legislative flexibility, competing rights and judicial authority. The Advocate argues for a bounded, voter-approved constitutional guarantee that governments cannot readily withdraw, supplemented by practical legislation. The Critic supports stronger speech protection but argues that the case for broad constitutional entrenchment remains insufficient, favouring targeted and revisable safeguards first. The proposal becomes narrower during the debate, but disagreement remains over its necessity, likely benefits and allocation of final decision-making power.
