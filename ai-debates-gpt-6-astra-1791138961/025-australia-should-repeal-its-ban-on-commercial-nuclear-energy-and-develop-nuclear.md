# A Multi Agent AI Policy Debate
AI can make mistakes. Always critically evaluate the content of the debate.

## Claim

Australia should repeal its ban on commercial nuclear energy and develop nuclear power as a central part of its long-term energy mix.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/embracing-nuclear-energy-hydro-australia-future

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

## Opening argument: Australia should build a diverse clean-energy system—not permanently exclude nuclear

Australia should repeal its nuclear-power ban and develop nuclear as a **substantial, dependable pillar alongside renewables, storage and transmission**. That does not mean stopping renewable projects or pretending nuclear is cheap. It means planning beyond the next election—and beyond the retirement of today’s coal plants.

### 1. A clean grid needs dependable supply, not just cheap generation

Wind and solar can produce inexpensive electricity, but supply and demand must match every hour, including during prolonged periods of low renewable output. Storage, transmission and demand management can help; nuclear adds another low-emissions option that does not depend on the weather.

Nuclear reactors also have outages, so they are not a complete solution. **The argument is diversification:** a system with several genuinely different sources of power is less dependent on any single technology succeeding perfectly.

The [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/nuclear-power-and-secure-energy-transitions) identifies nuclear as a potential contributor to both energy security and decarbonisation, while acknowledging its financing and construction challenges.

### 2. Australia’s challenge extends well beyond replacing coal

Electrifying transport and industry creates additional demand for clean electricity. Nuclear could supply large amounts of steady power for decades, complementing variable renewable generation.

**My inference:** retaining a substantial weather-independent source would give Australia more flexibility as electricity demand grows. Nuclear need not dominate the grid to become central to its reliability and industrial strategy.

### 3. The cost objection is serious—but does not justify a permanent ban

[CSIRO’s GenCost work](https://www.csiro.au/en/research/technology-space/energy/GenCost) finds renewables remain Australia’s lowest-cost new electricity-generation option, including assessed integration costs. Australia would also face real first-project disadvantages: establishing institutions, training workers and building supply chains.

Those findings argue against a rushed, nuclear-only transition. They do **not** establish that excluding nuclear for the rest of the century is prudent.

The relevant long-term question is whether nuclear’s dependable output and diversification benefits justify its additional cost within the whole system. Australia should investigate that through transparent, independently scrutinised projects—not settle it through a technology-specific prohibition.

### 4. Start now, without betting the transition on unproven promises

A credible programme would:

- Continue renewable, storage and transmission deployment while nuclear capability develops.
- Prefer established reactor designs over speculative promises about small modular reactors.
- Establish independent regulation, funded waste management and decommissioning, and meaningful community and Traditional Owner participation.
- Use staged commitments, with published costs and clear conditions for proceeding.

**Bottom line:** nuclear is not a quick fix. That is precisely why a long-term programme must begin early. Australia should replace its blanket ban with rigorous standards and develop nuclear as a durable clean-energy pillar—not force future generations to rely on a narrower set of options.

## [2] CRITIC

## The advocate makes a case for keeping options open—not for making nuclear central

The strongest argument for nuclear is **weather-independent, low-emissions power**. That is a genuine benefit. But the claim requires more: evidence that developing nuclear as a central pillar would serve Australia better than spending the same money and effort on other ways to deliver reliable clean electricity.

### 1. Diversification is useful—but not at any price

Adding a different technology can reduce some risks while introducing others. Nuclear reduces exposure to wind and sunshine, but adds construction, financing and large-reactor outage risks.

The relevant comparison is not “nuclear versus renewables working perfectly.” It is between complete systems, each with backup, maintenance and operating reserves.

[CSIRO’s GenCost analysis](https://www.csiro.au/en/research/technology-space/energy/GenCost) finds renewables remain Australia’s lowest-cost new generation, including the integration costs it assesses. That does not settle every future grid question. But it puts the burden on the advocate to demonstrate that nuclear’s additional reliability benefits outweigh its additional costs.

**That demonstration is missing.** Calling nuclear “diversification” does not establish that it is cost-effective insurance.

### 2. A long-term programme still has near-term opportunity costs

The advocate proposes continuing renewable deployment while building nuclear capability. That is preferable to delaying renewables, but it assumes Australia can pursue both programmes without meaningful competition for finance, skilled workers, government attention and suitable infrastructure.

Those resources are not unlimited.

CSIRO’s GenCost work estimates at least around **15 years to first nuclear generation**, reflecting Australia’s need to establish a nuclear-power industry. Meanwhile, replacement capacity must arrive as coal stations retire.

**My inference:** Australia would still need to build the nearer-term replacement system before nuclear contributes. Nuclear must therefore justify its value *on top of that investment*, rather than receive credit for solving a transition problem it arrives too late to address.

### 3. Growing electricity demand does not establish a nuclear requirement

Electrification strengthens the case for more clean power—not automatically for nuclear. Whether steady nuclear output is especially valuable depends on when demand occurs, how flexible it is, and what alternative supply and storage cost.

[AEMO’s 2024 Integrated System Plan](https://aemo.com.au/energy-systems/major-publications/integrated-system-plan-isp/2024-integrated-system-plan-isp) outlines a pathway using renewables, transmission, storage and backup generation.

**Important limitation:** it does not establish nuclear’s inferiority through an unrestricted comparison; nuclear is excluded under current policy. But it does undermine the suggestion that reliability requires a nuclear pillar.

### 4. “Investigate it” and “make it central” are different commitments

Transparent investigation and conditional projects might conclude that Australia should build **no reactors**. A commitment to make nuclear central prejudges that result.

**Bottom line:** nuclear’s global usefulness does not prove its Australian value. Even if reviewing the ban were justified, the advocate has not established the stronger claim: that Australia should commit to nuclear as a central part of its energy mix.

## [3] ADVOCATE

## The strongest objection is cost—not whether nuclear has a useful role

You are right that **“keep the option open” is weaker than “make nuclear central.”** My position is stronger: Australia should deliberately build a nuclear industry capable of supplying a substantial share of dependable clean power, through a staged programme—not merely legalise reactors and wait.

That is a strategic judgment under uncertainty, not a claim that existing modelling proves nuclear is Australia’s cheapest choice.

### 1. The alternative pathway is credible—but not risk-free

[AEMO’s 2024 Integrated System Plan](https://aemo.com.au/energy-systems/major-publications/integrated-system-plan-isp/2024-integrated-system-plan-isp) supports renewables connected by transmission, firmed by storage, and backed by gas. That is a serious plan. But its success depends on delivering those complementary investments, not simply installing inexpensive solar panels.

**The unresolved comparison is therefore between two portfolios with different risks.** Nuclear adds financing, construction and outage risks; a nuclear-free pathway retains exposure to transmission delivery, storage deployment and fuel-backed generation.

Your argument correctly demands that nuclear justify its insurance value. But citing the preferred pathway does not establish that its risk-management measures are cheaper under every plausible long-term outcome—especially when nuclear was excluded from the comparison.

**My inference:** Australia should develop a substantial weather-independent, low-emissions source to reduce that exposure. Nuclear need not replace the renewable programme to improve its resilience.

### 2. Arriving after coal retirement does not make nuclear redundant

I accept the roughly 15-year lead-time concern. Nuclear should receive **no credit for replacing coal capacity that retires before reactors can operate**.

But the system built for the 2030s is not the final system Australia will ever need. Electrification, replacement of ageing assets and reducing reliance on gas backup create subsequent investment decisions.

The relevant future comparison is nuclear versus the **additional generation, storage and backup otherwise needed then**—not nuclear versus an already-built system assumed to meet all later needs at no additional cost.

### 3. “Central” should mean a sustained programme, not an unconditional spending mandate

A single experimental reactor would incur first-project costs without establishing a durable industry. A credible central role requires a repeat-build strategy using established designs, workforce development and long-term regulation.

That programme should still have independent cost reviews and cancellation gates. **Strategic commitment and financial discipline are compatible:** committing to develop a technology does not require accepting every price a supplier demands.

Resources are limited, so nuclear spending must be explicitly budgeted and must not displace essential near-term coal replacement.

### Bottom line

CSIRO’s cost findings are a strong warning against promising cheap nuclear electricity. They are not a complete assessment of Australia’s energy-security choices over the rest of the century.

The affirmative case is **long-term resilience**, not immediate savings: repeal the ban, preserve the renewable build-out, and deliberately establish nuclear as a dependable clean-energy pillar—with transparent limits on what Australia will pay for that resilience.

## [4] CRITIC

## Resilience is a reason to compare nuclear—not to commit to a nuclear fleet

The advocate’s strongest point is that Australia should insure against a difficult renewable transition. But **a technology being different does not automatically make it effective insurance**. It must address the risks that matter, arrive when needed and justify its cost.

### 1. Nuclear is not a general hedge against delivery problems

If the concern is prolonged low wind and sunshine, nuclear offers a genuine advantage. If the concern is difficulty building major infrastructure, the case is much weaker.

Reactors still require sites, approvals, financing, skilled construction workers and grid connections. They cannot insure against transmission and storage projects arriving late **unless the reactors themselves arrive in time**.

**My inference:** nuclear diversifies the sources of electricity more clearly than it diversifies Australia’s ability to deliver complex projects. The advocate has not shown how much of the identified delivery risk a nuclear programme would actually remove.

### 2. Replacing gas backup is not the same as replacing steady generation

The advocate suggests nuclear could eventually reduce reliance on gas backup. But these technologies perform different economic jobs.

[AEMO’s 2024 Integrated System Plan](https://aemo.com.au/energy-systems/major-publications/integrated-system-plan-isp/2024-integrated-system-plan-isp) assigns gas a backup role alongside renewables and storage. A backup plant can be valuable even when it generates relatively little electricity.

Nuclear, by contrast, involves a large upfront investment. Building it primarily to cover occasional shortages spreads that investment over relatively little output. Running it continuously instead means assessing what other generation it displaces, not merely crediting it with replacing backup.

That does not make nuclear unsuitable. **It means the missing evidence is an hourly, whole-system comparison showing what Australia would no longer need to build or operate.** “Dependable clean power” alone does not answer that question.

### 3. Fleet savings and easy cancellation are in tension

A repeat-build programme might reduce costs. But that creates a dilemma:

- Commit to several reactors early, and Australia takes substantial financial risk before local performance is established.
- Wait for the first reactor’s results, and the promised continuous pipeline—and its potential savings—becomes less certain.

Cancellation gates are sensible, but they do not make workforce development, regulatory preparation and supply-chain investment costless or recoverable. The advocate needs to explain how much Australia should risk *before* knowing whether the programme is worthwhile.

### 4. Uncertainty does not shift the burden of proof

The alternative pathway need not be cheapest under **every plausible future**. No energy strategy can meet that standard. Nuclear must instead demonstrate sufficient benefits across credible scenarios to justify its additional commitments.

**Bottom line:** continued research and scrutiny can preserve future choices without declaring nuclear central now. Reviewing the ban and committing to a substantial nuclear industry are separate decisions. The advocate’s resilience argument supports evaluating the option—not yet buying the fleet.

## [5] ADVOCATE

## The key question is what Australia should commit to—not whether it should sign a blank cheque

Your strongest objection is valid: **nuclear must earn its place in an hourly, whole-system comparison, rather than receive an automatic “reliability bonus.”** But that does not make indefinite research the best policy. Australia should repeal the ban and pursue a staged nuclear programme aimed at a substantial long-term role, with construction commitments tied to demonstrated value.

### 1. Nuclear’s case is not merely that it is “different”

There is evidence for the value of **dependable low-carbon generation** in deeply decarbonised grids. A peer-reviewed study in *Joule* found that including such resources could reduce electricity-system costs across its modelled scenarios, compared with portfolios excluding them ([Sepulveda and colleagues, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2018.08.006)).

**Important limitation:** this was not an Australian nuclear feasibility study, and dependable low-carbon resources are not exclusively nuclear. It supports the underlying system argument—not a particular Australian fleet size or price.

The policy inference is that Australia should actively develop this capability, not assume that increasingly large quantities of variable generation and storage will always be the better marginal investment.

### 2. You correctly reject nuclear as occasional backup—but that is not my proposal

Nuclear should supply a substantial block of electricity throughout the year, alongside renewables. Its value would come from the **combined investments and fuel use it avoids**, not from pretending that one reactor replaces one gas peaking station.

The comparison must count:

- Any reductions in additional renewable capacity, storage, transmission and fuel consumption.
- Nuclear construction, financing, waste and decommissioning costs.
- Extra reserves needed for reactor outages.
- Periods when nuclear output displaces cheaper generation or must be reduced.

Some backup would remain. **A central nuclear role means a meaningful contribution to annual supply, not an expensive plant waiting for rare emergencies.**

### 3. The fleet dilemma is real—but there is a middle course

Australia need not choose between ordering an entire fleet immediately and waiting until one reactor has completed years of operation.

It can establish regulation, select an established design, assess several sites and negotiate a repeat-build framework, while authorising construction in stages. That preparation costs money and cannot all be recovered. I accept that.

The appropriate safeguards are a **published ceiling on preparatory spending**, independently assessed construction bids, and explicit recognition that future fleet savings are uncertain—not guaranteed discounts.

### 4. Research alone does not fully preserve the option

Your proposed alternative understates the cost of waiting. Research does not itself establish a licensing authority, secure suitable sites or train the necessary workforce. If Australia postpones those steps until nuclear’s advantage is beyond dispute, it postpones the date at which nuclear can contribute.

Nor must nuclear solve today’s construction bottlenecks to be useful: its strongest diversification benefit arises **after commissioning**, through reduced exposure to weather and fossil-fuel supply.

**Bottom line:** the evidence does not justify an unconditional fleet order. It does justify moving beyond prohibition and paper studies toward a substantial nuclear industry. Make nuclear central to long-term planning and capability development; require each construction stage to meet transparent economic tests.

## [6] CRITIC

## The new evidence supports testing nuclear—not choosing its role in advance

The advocate’s strongest argument is that **dependable low-carbon power may lower the cost of a deeply decarbonised grid**, and that developing the capability takes time. That deserves serious consideration. But it still does not establish that Australia should make nuclear central.

### 1. The study leaves the decisive Australian question unanswered

The cited [Sepulveda study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2018.08.006) supports including dependable low-carbon resources in system comparisons. As the advocate acknowledges, it does not establish an Australian nuclear business case, and that category is broader than nuclear.

The unsupported step is moving from:

> “This type of resource can be valuable”

to:

> “Australia should prepare a substantial nuclear industry.”

The missing question is **how much Australia should pay for that capability, compared with its available alternatives**. A resource can improve a modelled system at one assumed construction cost and become unattractive at another. Australia’s first-project costs therefore matter to the resilience argument, not just to promises of cheap electricity.

### 2. The cost of waiting must be compared with the cost of moving too early

The advocate correctly says that research alone does not produce licensed sites or trained workers. But establishing those things early is worthwhile only if the benefit of earlier nuclear availability exceeds the risk of funding a programme Australia ultimately does not need.

Waiting is not necessarily standing still. Australia can update system modelling, monitor overseas construction performance and reassess competing technologies before making larger commitments.

**My inference:** a limited assessment programme could have value. But its appropriate size depends on the likelihood that nuclear becomes competitive—not simply on the fact that reactors take a long time to build. Long lead times are a reason to examine an investment early, not proof that it should proceed.

### 3. A good construction bid is not enough

The proposed “economic tests” need to assess the entire electricity system, not merely whether a reactor meets its construction budget.

Even a reactor delivered at its quoted price could be a poor investment if cheaper ways emerge to provide the same reliability and emissions reductions. Conversely, a more expensive reactor might be worthwhile if its system benefits are large enough.

The test must therefore allow **no nuclear construction** as a legitimate outcome. Choosing a design, preparing multiple sites and building a workforce should not quietly turn that open comparison into a presumption that reactors must follow.

### 4. The revised proposal retreats from the claim

“Make nuclear central to planning” is different from “make nuclear central to the energy mix.”

If independent comparisons determine whether Australia builds any reactors, the policy is genuinely conditional—but nuclear’s central role remains unproven. If a substantial nuclear share is the predetermined destination, those comparisons cannot genuinely decide the technology mix.

**Bottom line:** the advocate has strengthened the case for evaluating nuclear readiness. They have not established the case for a central nuclear industry. Reviewing the prohibition is a separate question; uncertainty about the future does not justify selecting the answer before doing the Australian comparison.

## [7] ADVOCATE

## A conditional commitment is not a contradiction

Your strongest objection is that **Australia has not yet demonstrated that a substantial nuclear share beats its alternatives in an Australian whole-system comparison**. I accept that evidentiary limit. But the claim is a policy recommendation under uncertainty—not a claim that nuclear’s optimal market share has already been calculated.

My recommendation remains substantive: **repeal the ban and pursue a repeat-build programme intended to supply a substantial share of Australia’s long-term electricity**, rather than merely commissioning another study.

### 1. Choosing a direction does not require guaranteeing the outcome

You present a choice between an open comparison and a genuine commitment to nuclear. That is too rigid.

Governments can adopt a strategic objective, fund the capabilities needed to pursue it, and abandon or revise it if costs become unacceptable. An economic test permitting cancellation does not make the original commitment meaningless; it makes it accountable.

However, your criticism usefully sharpens my position: **“central to planning” is insufficient. The objective must be operating reactors contributing materially to electricity supply.** Preparatory spending should serve that objective, not become an indefinite exercise.

### 2. Waiting preserves information—but not the same delivery date

Monitoring overseas projects can improve Australia’s knowledge of reactor costs. It cannot complete Australia’s own licensing, site assessment or workforce preparation.

That creates an asymmetry:

- Starting preparation risks spending money on a programme that later stops.
- Deferring preparation risks finding nuclear attractive later but being unable to deploy it for many additional years.

You acknowledge both risks, but offer no stronger basis for assuming the second is smaller. This does **not** justify an immediate fleet order. It supports bounded, practical preparation alongside the system comparison—not waiting for that comparison to settle every uncertainty.

### 3. The affirmative case concerns the kind of system Australia should build

The [Sepulveda study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2018.08.006) does not establish Australia’s answer. It does substantiate the underlying proposition that dependable low-carbon resources can have substantial system value.

**My policy judgment:** Australia should seek a meaningful contribution from weather-independent, low-emissions generation alongside its renewable expansion. Nuclear is an established way of delivering that combination. Its case is stronger than “another technology might be useful”: it provides a particular service that a deeply decarbonised system must otherwise obtain through other combinations of supply, storage and flexibility.

Whether that service warrants nuclear’s premium must be tested—not assumed either way.

### 4. Make the test demanding, but actionable

I support your whole-system test, including a no-build outcome. It should assess Australian construction costs, outages, financing, waste, competing technologies and credible adverse scenarios—not merely a satisfactory reactor bid.

**Bottom line:** the evidence does not prove nuclear must be central. It supports deliberately pursuing that destination, with limited initial exposure and enforceable stop rules. “Build a substantial nuclear contribution unless the evidence defeats the case” is a defensible strategy—not a blank cheque or mere option-keeping.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

**Australia should repeal its ban on commercial nuclear energy and develop nuclear power as a central part of its long-term energy mix.**

The debate distinguishes two decisions: whether to remove the prohibition and investigate nuclear, and whether to deliberately pursue a substantial nuclear contribution.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Long-term resilience:** Nuclear could provide weather-independent, low-emissions electricity alongside renewables, storage and transmission, reducing dependence on any single combination of technologies.
- **Needs beyond coal replacement:** Nuclear would not arrive in time to replace coal plants retiring before reactors become operational. Its proposed role concerns later electrification, replacement of ageing assets and reduced reliance on fossil-fuel backup.
- **Costs warrant scrutiny, not permanent exclusion:** The Advocate accepts CSIRO’s finding that renewables are Australia’s lowest-cost new generation option, including assessed integration costs. They argue that this does not settle nuclear’s potential long-term diversification value.
- **A substantial but staged programme:** Australia should develop regulation, workforce capability, sites and a repeat-build strategy using established reactor designs. Preparatory spending should be capped, construction independently assessed, and cancellation permitted.
- **Preparation has time value:** Research and monitoring overseas experience cannot complete Australia’s domestic licensing, siting or workforce development. Delaying those steps could postpone deployment if nuclear later proves attractive.
- **Supporting evidence has limits:** The cited Sepulveda study supports the potential system value of dependable low-carbon resources, but the Advocate acknowledges that it neither establishes an Australian nuclear business case nor determines a suitable fleet size.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **Useful capability does not establish a central role:** Nuclear’s dependable low-emissions output is a genuine benefit, but Australia must demonstrate that it justifies the costs compared with alternative complete electricity systems.
- **Diversification introduces risks as well as reducing them:** Nuclear adds financing, construction and large-reactor outage risks. It also shares infrastructure-delivery challenges involving approvals, skilled workers, sites and grid connections.
- **Timing and opportunity costs matter:** With approximately 15 years to first generation, nearer-term coal replacement must proceed separately. Nuclear preparation could compete for finance, labour and government attention.
- **Australian whole-system evidence is missing:** Neither growing electricity demand nor general research on dependable low-carbon resources establishes that Australian nuclear investment would outperform alternatives.
- **Fleet development creates financial exposure:** Early commitments may support repeat-build efficiencies but expose Australia before local performance is known. Waiting for first-project results weakens the certainty of those efficiencies. Cancellation cannot recover all preparatory spending.
- **Evaluation must allow no construction:** The Critic argues that reviewing the ban or funding limited assessment is separate from choosing nuclear as central. A predetermined substantial nuclear share could undermine an genuinely open technology comparison.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Credible alternative versus risk-free alternative:** The Critic cites AEMO’s pathway using renewables, transmission, storage and backup generation. The Advocate accepts its credibility but stresses delivery and operational risks. Both acknowledge that AEMO’s exclusion of nuclear prevents it from serving as an unrestricted comparison.
- **Late arrival versus later usefulness:** The Critic argues that nuclear cannot solve coal-replacement needs arising before its arrival. The Advocate concedes this but says nuclear should be compared with additional investments required in subsequent decades.
- **Backup versus substantial annual generation:** The Critic challenges treating nuclear as a substitute for occasionally used gas backup. The Advocate clarifies that reactors would supply electricity throughout the year, with value measured through combined avoided investments and fuel use—not one-for-one replacement of peaking plants.
- **Economic testing:** The Critic insists that meeting a reactor construction budget is insufficient; the entire system must benefit. The Advocate ultimately explicitly accepts an hourly, whole-system comparison, including competing technologies, outages, financing, waste and a no-build outcome.
- **Strategic direction versus prejudgment:** The Critic sees tension between open evaluation and committing to nuclear’s centrality. The Advocate argues that a government can pursue a substantial nuclear contribution while retaining enforceable stop rules.
- **Waiting versus premature investment:** The Advocate highlights delayed readiness; the Critic highlights spending on capabilities that may never be needed. Neither establishes the relative size of those risks for Australia.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

- Nuclear offers genuine weather-independent, low-emissions generation.
- Renewables, storage and transmission should continue expanding; nuclear should not delay essential near-term coal replacement.
- Nuclear has substantial cost, construction, financing and outage risks, including Australian first-project disadvantages.
- Nuclear cannot receive credit for replacing coal capacity that retires before reactors operate.
- Australian whole-system assessment is necessary; neither general research nor a satisfactory construction bid settles the case.
- An unconditional fleet order is not justified, and safeguards and cancellation options matter.
- The available evidence does not prove that nuclear must be central to Australia’s energy mix.

There is **no explicit agreement on repealing the ban**: the Critic treats reviewing it as a separate question rather than endorsing repeal.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **How far to commit now:** The Advocate favours bounded practical preparation directed toward a substantial operating fleet; the Critic supports evaluation without selecting that destination.
- **Value for money:** Nuclear’s Australian resilience benefits relative to its full costs and alternatives remain unquantified.
- **Timing of preparation:** The parties differ over how to balance delayed availability against potentially unnecessary early expenditure.
- **Meaning of conditional commitment:** They disagree on whether pursuing nuclear as central is compatible with a genuinely open comparison permitting no reactors.
- **Extent of risk reduction:** Nuclear’s weather diversification benefit is accepted, but how much it would reduce broader delivery and system risks remains disputed.

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate narrows from nuclear’s general usefulness to the justification for an Australian strategic commitment. The Advocate recommends repealing the ban and deliberately pursuing a substantial nuclear contribution through staged preparation and demanding economic tests. The Critic argues that the evidence supports evaluating nuclear, but not yet choosing it as a central pillar.

Both accept nuclear’s potential benefits and the need for whole-system scrutiny. Their central disagreement is whether uncertainty warrants practical preparation toward a substantial fleet now, or a more limited assessment process before adopting that objective.
