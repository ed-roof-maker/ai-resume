## Claim

The Australian government should expand domestic timber plantations to make Australia self-sufficient in timber.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/forestry

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

**Australia should expand domestic timber plantations so it can meet its timber needs at home, rather than depend on overseas supply for an essential building material.** The strongest case is for a targeted, long-term expansion—not planting trees everywhere or banning imports.

### 1. Timber security is worth planning for
Australia produces substantial timber but also imports sawnwood and other wood products, as documented by [ABARES forest-sector statistics](https://www.agriculture.gov.au/abares/research-topics/forests). Imports are useful, but dependence leaves Australian builders exposed to overseas shortages, shipping disruption and exchange-rate movements.

More domestic production would not guarantee cheap timber, but it would give Australia greater control over an important input into housing and infrastructure.

### 2. The long growing cycle is a reason to start now
Construction timber takes decades to grow. Plantations cannot solve today’s housing shortage—but delaying planting leaves future Australians facing the same supply problem.

Government should help establish plantations suited to anticipated demand, especially construction-grade softwoods, while supporting the nurseries, transport and processing capacity needed to turn those trees into usable timber. **Self-sufficiency requires the right timber and mills, not simply more hectares of trees.**

### 3. Well-planned plantations offer a better environmental pathway
Producing more timber on suitable, already-cleared land can reduce pressure to obtain additional wood from native forests, here or overseas. Growing trees stores carbon, and some remains stored in long-lived timber products.

Those benefits are conditional: plantations should not replace native ecosystems, undermine water supplies or displace highly productive farmland unnecessarily. Farm forestry, careful siting and fire management should be central to expansion.

### The policy choice
Self-sufficiency should mean **having the capacity to supply Australia’s needs**, not forcing consumers to reject every imported product. Government support should be targeted, transparent and tested against environmental impacts and commercial viability.

The objection that “imports may be cheaper” overlooks the value of dependable supply. Australia should retain trade while building the domestic capacity to avoid being reliant on it. Planting now is an investment in future housing supply, regional industry and national resilience.

## [2] CRITIC

**Australia should pursue reliable timber supply—not self-sufficiency as an end in itself.** Some plantation expansion may be worthwhile, but the advocate has not shown that enough expansion to meet all domestic needs is the best use of public money or land.

### 1. Domestic supply is not necessarily dependable supply
The advocate’s strongest point is that imports expose builders to shipping disruptions and overseas shortages. That is a genuine risk—but concentrating production in Australia creates different risks.

The [Bureau of Meteorology and CSIRO’s State of the Climate](https://www.bom.gov.au/state-of-the-climate/) reports increasing extreme fire weather and longer fire seasons across large parts of Australia.

**The policy inference:** domestic plantations should be one part of a diversified supply system, not its security benchmark. Overseas suppliers can provide backup when Australian forests or mills suffer disruption. The advocate needs to demonstrate why self-sufficient capacity offers better protection than a smaller domestic expansion combined with diversified imports.

### 2. “Suitable land” is an unresolved economic constraint
The proposal assumes enough land can be found that is productive for timber, environmentally acceptable, accessible to mills and not better used for farming or conservation.

Those conditions cannot simply be written into policy and assumed to hold. Restricting plantations to genuinely suitable sites may limit expansion or increase costs. Before committing to self-sufficiency, government needs a credible assessment of land availability, water impacts, processing costs and the subsidy required.

### 3. The long growing cycle cuts both ways
Planting now creates future supply—but it also commits land and money decades before the final market is known. Future construction methods, timber specifications and demand may differ from today’s forecasts.

That supports staged investment tested against demand, not planting toward a national self-sufficiency target regardless of cost.

### 4. Environmental benefits do not establish the target
Well-sited plantations can store carbon and supply useful wood. But those benefits support **particular projects**, not necessarily self-sufficiency. Reduced pressure on native forests also depends on plantation timber actually replacing native-forest products.

**The better policy:** support demonstrably worthwhile plantations, processing efficiency and supply diversification. The advocate has made a case for considering more plantations—not for making domestic capacity to meet every timber need the government’s objective.

## [3] ADVOCATE

**The critic’s strongest objection is fair: why aim for self-sufficiency rather than a smaller expansion backed by imports?** My answer is that Australia should plan for the ability to meet domestic demand—not merely hope that individually attractive plantation projects add up to adequate supply. That is a strategic objective, not permission to subsidise trees at any cost.

### 1. Domestic capacity and diversified imports are compatible
The critic rightly identifies bushfire and climate risks. But the proposal does not require Australia to stop importing timber or concentrate plantations in one region.

There is an important difference between **using imports by choice and needing imports to cover a structural shortfall**. Domestic capacity can cover disrupted overseas supplies, while overseas suppliers remain available after Australian disruptions.

That does not prove that complete domestic capacity is automatically the cheapest option. It does show why fire risk is an argument for geographic diversification and continued trade—not, by itself, against self-sufficiency.

### 2. A national target addresses a coordination problem
Plantations, mills and transport infrastructure need to develop together. A grower needs confidence that processing capacity will exist when trees mature; a processor needs confidence that sufficient trees will be available.

**The policy inference:** assessing plantations only as isolated projects can miss the value of a coordinated supply system. A national self-sufficiency objective gives government and industry a demand benchmark against which to plan the whole chain.

The critic’s alternative—support worthwhile projects and diversify imports—does not explain how it would ensure that the remaining import dependence is acceptably small.

### 3. Test feasibility, then expand in stages
I accept that suitable land cannot simply be assumed into existence. Government should:

- Identify land with acceptable farming, water and biodiversity trade-offs.
- Match planting to forecast demand and realistic processing capacity.
- Compare costs with diversified imports, including disruption risks.
- Release support in stages and revise plans as evidence changes.

**The burden is to establish a credible pathway to self-sufficiency, not to promise it regardless of cost.** The critic has identified necessary tests, but has not shown that Australia would fail them.

### 4. Uncertainty also makes inaction risky
Future timber demand is uncertain. Yet choosing not to establish plantations also commits Australia to a future: one in which domestic supply cannot quickly increase if demand rises. Staging investment manages uncertainty without treating delay as costless.

**Australia should therefore adopt self-sufficiency as a long-term capacity goal, supported by disciplined, staged plantation expansion.** Environmental benefits strengthen eligible projects; supply security and coordinated investment justify the national objective. Trade should remain a useful option—not an unavoidable dependency.

## [4] CRITIC

**The advocate makes a good case for coordinated timber planning—but still does not establish why the target should be self-sufficiency.** The unresolved issue is whether capacity to meet *all* domestic demand provides enough extra security to justify its extra cost.

### 1. Domestic capacity is not the same as backup supply
The advocate’s strongest argument is that domestic production could replace imports during overseas disruptions, while imports remain available after Australian disruptions.

But that assumes Australian producers have **usable extra supply when needed**. If plantations and mills are already supplying customers, their output cannot simply be counted again as emergency replacement timber. Government would need a credible arrangement for spare capacity, inventories or redirecting exports—and timber of the right specifications.

This is a practical inference, not a claim that domestic backup is impossible. It means that “annual production capacity equals annual consumption” is an incomplete security measure. **The relevant question is how much additional timber can reach builders during a disruption, and how quickly.**

### 2. Coordination does not require a self-sufficiency target
Growers, mills and transport providers do need to plan together. But government can coordinate regional planting and processing around demand forecasts without deciding that imports must become unnecessary.

The advocate conflates two different objectives:

- Ensuring investments across the supply chain fit together.
- Ensuring domestic capacity covers every unit of national demand.

The first does not establish the second. A coordinated system with some continuing import dependence could be cheaper and sufficiently resilient.

### 3. The proposal reverses the burden of proof
The advocate says the critic has not shown Australia would fail the feasibility tests. But **a policy’s opponents do not have to prove it impossible before questioning its adoption**.

Even technical feasibility would not settle the issue. The proposed pathway must outperform alternatives: more modest plantation expansion, diversified suppliers, inventories where practical, and better use of existing timber. The relevant test is comparative value, not simply whether self-sufficiency can be achieved.

### 4. Staging only helps if the target can be rejected
Suppose the assessment finds that substantial expansion is worthwhile, but eliminating the remaining import dependence is disproportionately expensive. Would government stop?

If yes, the governing objective is cost-effective resilience—not self-sufficiency. If no, the promise of disciplined investment is undermined.

**Australia should plan and expand worthwhile timber capacity, but judge success by reliable supply through realistic disruptions—not by whether imports are theoretically optional.**

## [5] ADVOCATE

**The critic is right that matching annual production to annual consumption is not enough. But that calls for a better-designed self-sufficiency policy—not abandoning the objective.** Australia should expand plantations toward meeting domestic timber needs, with processing and supply arrangements that make that capacity usable.

### 1. The main benefit is reduced exposure—not counting timber twice
Suppose, purely as an illustration, Australia needs 100 units of construction timber and produces 70. Expanding domestic production to supply the remaining 30 **before a disruption** reduces the amount exposed to an overseas supply interruption. Those additional units are ordinary supply, not emergency stock counted twice.

The critic’s spare-capacity objection does apply if Australia continues importing those 30 units while treating fully committed domestic production as backup. I agree that would be misleading.

The refined objective should therefore measure **deliverable domestic supply of the products Australia needs**, not just standing trees or theoretical mill capacity. Inventories and arrangements to redirect suitable export products could supplement that supply; they cannot simply be assumed.

### 2. Diversified imports do not eliminate every shared risk
The critic reasonably proposes buying from several countries. That helps when one supplier suffers a local disruption. But suppliers can still face shared pressures—for example, competing international demand or transport disruption.

This is a risk argument, not evidence that imports always fail together. Its significance is that **several foreign suppliers are not necessarily several independent safeguards**.

Domestic production also has shared risks, especially fire and drought. Hence the case for geographically spread plantations *and* continued access to trade. Self-sufficiency would reduce structural overseas dependence without pretending Australia is disaster-proof.

### 3. The final increment must earn its place
I accept the correction on burden of proof: advocates must justify the investment, not demand that opponents prove it impossible.

But the critic presents too sharp a choice between an unconditional target and no self-sufficiency target. Government can adopt a long-term destination while requiring successive investments to pass economic and environmental tests.

**If eliminating the final shortfall proves disproportionately costly, government should pause that step—not subsidise it blindly.** That qualification does not make the goal meaningless: the goal directs planning toward closing identified supply gaps, while the tests constrain how it is pursued.

### Bottom line
Coordination alone does not prove that self-sufficiency is optimal. Equally, the possibility that a smaller expansion might be sufficient does not establish that alternative.

**The positive case is for building domestic capacity that reduces exposure before disruption occurs, over the decades needed to grow timber.** Australia should pursue self-sufficiency through staged plantation expansion, while testing actual delivery capability and the additional cost of each step—not treating either imports or domestic trees as automatic guarantees of security.

## [6] CRITIC

**The advocate’s revised proposal supports selective expansion, but still does not justify self-sufficiency as the destination.** Its strongest argument is that replacing imports *before* a disruption reduces exposure to overseas interruptions. That is correct—but it is only half the security calculation.

### 1. Replacing imports transfers risk; it does not necessarily reduce it
The advocate’s hypothetical increase from 70 to 100 units of domestic supply avoids counting timber twice. But those additional 30 units move from overseas supply chains into Australian growing, processing and transport networks.

The relevant comparison is therefore not simply **“How much do we import?”** It is **“Under each supply mix, how severe and prolonged could a shortage become?”**

Diversified imports can share risks, as the advocate notes. But acknowledging shared risks on both sides does not establish which mix is safer. That requires assessing actual suppliers, regions, products and disruption scenarios. **The unsupported assumption is that moving progressively toward entirely domestic supply keeps improving resilience.**

### 2. A self-sufficiency target can direct investment toward the wrong gaps
Not every imported timber product presents the same security problem. An easily replaceable product available from several suppliers may deserve less attention than a bottleneck affecting essential construction.

Yet closing the national supply gap encourages attention to replacing imports, rather than fixing the vulnerabilities with the greatest consequences.

A better planning instruction is: **identify which shortages would seriously disrupt building, then compare ways to prevent or manage them.** Plantation expansion might win that comparison in some cases; inventories, processing upgrades or alternative suppliers might win in others. This is coordinated planning—not passive reliance on isolated commercial projects.

### 3. “Pause if too expensive” leaves the destination unjustified
I accept that a conditional goal is not meaningless. Goals influence which projects receive attention, infrastructure and funding.

That is precisely why the destination needs justification *before* it guides investment. Otherwise, self-sufficiency gains preferred status while alternatives must repeatedly prove themselves against it.

The advocate argues that a smaller expansion has not been proved sufficient. Fair—but that uncertainty supports comparing options, not choosing the most domestically oriented option in advance.

**Bottom line:** reducing a particular import vulnerability can be worthwhile without making imports unnecessary. Australia should expand plantations where they demonstrably improve supply security and value—not organise timber policy around an unproven self-sufficiency endpoint.

## [7] ADVOCATE

**The critic’s strongest point is correct: replacing imports with Australian timber does not automatically make supply safer.** My case for self-sufficiency rests instead on a different consideration: **Australia can retain access to overseas suppliers while building domestic productive capacity—but it cannot quickly create mature plantations when overseas supply becomes unreliable.**

### 1. The options are not equally reversible
The critic proposes choosing the supply mix that performs best across realistic disruptions. That is sensible, but the assessment must extend beyond today’s suppliers and prices.

Plantations take decades to mature. Changing overseas purchasing arrangements can also be difficult, but it does not necessarily require waiting for an entirely new forest to grow.

**My policy inference:** domestic growing capacity has long-term insurance value. An assessment that counts its establishment costs but overlooks the difficulty of rebuilding missing capacity would favour imports too readily.

This does not prove that every additional plantation is worthwhile. It explains why government should deliberately pursue the capacity to meet domestic needs rather than accept a permanent shortfall as the default.

### 2. Prioritising bottlenecks is compatible with the destination
The critic rightly distinguishes essential construction timber from easily substituted imports. **Self-sufficiency should not become a crude “replace the most imports” funding rule.**

Government should first identify the timber specifications essential to construction, then work backwards through processing requirements, growing conditions and planting needs. Inventories and mill upgrades can address nearer-term weaknesses; plantation expansion addresses whether sufficient domestic feedstock will exist later.

The weakness in the critic’s alternative is not that it ignores planning. It is that **managing current vulnerabilities does not, by itself, establish how much long-term growing capacity Australia should preserve or develop**. A domestic-needs benchmark makes that question explicit.

### 3. The endpoint needs a justification—not a guarantee
The justification is a policy judgement: for an essential material with a decades-long production cycle, the ability to supply ourselves deserves weight beyond the cheapest expected supply mix.

That preference must be transparent. Government should publish the additional costs, land and water trade-offs, and performance under disruption scenarios. A self-sufficiency target should guide investigation and staged investment—not override adverse findings.

**The unresolved issue is how much that independence is worth, not whether imports are inherently bad.** I favour expanding plantations toward self-sufficiency because productive capability takes decades to secure, while retaining trade preserves diversification. The critic’s cost and risk tests should discipline that ambition, not replace it.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

**The Australian government should expand domestic timber plantations to make Australia self-sufficient in timber.**

The Advocate defines self-sufficiency as the capacity to supply domestic timber needs, while retaining trade—not banning imports or pursuing expansion regardless of cost.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Supply security:** Greater domestic production could reduce exposure to overseas shortages, shipping disruption and exchange-rate movements affecting an essential building material.
- **Long-term insurance value:** Plantations take decades to mature. Australia cannot quickly create domestic growing capacity when overseas supply becomes unreliable, so delaying investment carries risks.
- **Coordinated planning:** Plantations, mills and transport infrastructure must develop together. A domestic-needs target would provide a benchmark for planning the whole supply chain.
- **Conditional environmental benefits:** Well-sited plantations on suitable, already-cleared land could store carbon and reduce pressure on native forests. Expansion should respect water supplies, biodiversity and productive farmland.
- **Disciplined implementation:** Government should assess land availability, demand, processing requirements, costs and disruption risks, then invest in stages. Essential construction products should take priority, and disproportionately expensive steps should be paused.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **Resilience, not self-sufficiency, should be the objective:** The Advocate has not established that meeting all domestic needs provides sufficient additional security to justify its costs.
- **Domestic production also faces risks:** Fire, drought and disruptions to Australian processing and transport can affect supply. Replacing imports transfers exposure between supply systems rather than automatically reducing it.
- **Feasibility and opportunity costs remain unresolved:** Suitable land must meet timber-growing, environmental, farming and processing requirements. Decades-long investment also faces uncertain future demand and specifications.
- **Capacity is not necessarily usable supply:** Matching annual production capacity to consumption does not show how much suitable timber can reach builders during a disruption.
- **Coordination does not require a self-sufficiency endpoint:** Government can plan plantations and processing around long-term demand while retaining some import dependence.
- **Alternatives need comparison:** More modest expansion, diversified suppliers, inventories, processing improvements and better use of existing timber may address vulnerabilities more effectively than eliminating the domestic supply gap.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Domestic risks and continued trade:** The Advocate argues that geographically diversified plantations and continued imports are compatible with self-sufficient capacity. The Critic responds that compatibility does not establish which supply mix performs best under actual disruptions.
- **The backup-capacity objection:** The Critic warns against counting already-committed domestic output as emergency replacement supply. The Advocate accepts this and clarifies that replacing imports *before* disruption reduces overseas exposure without double-counting. Both recognise the need to measure deliverable products rather than theoretical capacity.
- **Coordination versus the target:** The Advocate says a national benchmark helps align growers, processors and infrastructure. The Critic argues that coordinated planning can occur without making imports unnecessary.
- **Burden of proof:** The Advocate initially notes that the Critic has not shown self-sufficiency to be infeasible, but later accepts that proponents must justify the investment. The Critic maintains that feasibility alone is insufficient: the policy must offer comparative value.
- **Conditional targets:** The Advocate would pause the final step if eliminating the remaining shortfall proved disproportionately costly. The Critic accepts that conditional goals have meaning but argues that their influence on investment makes prior justification essential.
- **Long-term planning:** The Advocate’s final response emphasises the difficulty of rebuilding missing plantation capacity and its insurance value. The Critic’s position is that long-term resilience should be assessed through competing options rather than presumed to favour self-sufficiency.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides agree that:

- Some plantation expansion may be worthwhile.
- Timber supply needs coordinated, long-term planning.
- Neither imports nor domestic production automatically guarantees security.
- Continued trade and geographic diversification have value.
- Investment should account for land, water, biodiversity, processing capacity and commercial viability.
- Environmental benefits are conditional and do not alone establish the appropriate national target.
- Actual product specifications and deliverable supply matter more than plantation hectares or nominal capacity.
- Public investment must be justified, with staged decisions responsive to evidence.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **The policy endpoint:** Should self-sufficiency guide policy, or should government pursue reliable supply without a preferred domestic-production target?
- **The value of independence:** How much additional weight should domestic capacity receive because plantations take decades to establish?
- **The safest supply mix:** Would progressively replacing imports improve overall resilience, or could a mixed system provide better protection?
- **The effect of a national target:** Would it improve coordination and preserve future options, or bias investment toward import replacement rather than the most consequential vulnerabilities?
- **The justification for full capacity:** The debate does not establish the comparative costs, suitable land availability or disruption performance needed to settle whether capacity to meet all domestic needs is worthwhile.

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate narrows from a broad proposal for timber self-sufficiency to a conditional programme of staged plantation expansion, coordinated processing investment and continued trade. The Advocate sees self-sufficiency as a valuable long-term planning goal because domestic growing capacity cannot be created quickly. The Critic supports worthwhile expansion but argues that this does not justify self-sufficiency as the destination.

Their central disagreement is therefore not whether Australia should invest in timber capacity, but whether domestic independence should guide that investment or emerge only if comparative assessments show it offers the best resilience and value.
