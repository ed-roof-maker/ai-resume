## Claim

Australia should require family law parenting arrangements to maintain both parents’ involvement in their children’s lives when both parents are deemed fit and able.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/family

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

## Opening argument: protect the child’s relationship with both parents

Australia should require parenting arrangements to preserve both parents’ meaningful involvement **when both are fit and able, and that involvement is safe for the child**. This is about children’s interests—not giving adults equal shares of a child.

### 1. Separation should not unnecessarily cost a child a parent
When both parents can provide safe, competent care, each remains a potential source of love, stability, practical support and connection to family. Ending the adults’ relationship does not, by itself, justify weakening either parent’s relationship with their child.

That principle has a strong foundation: [Article 9 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-rights-child) recognises children’s right to regular personal relations and direct contact with both parents, except where contrary to their best interests.

### 2. A relationship needs practical protection, not just goodwill
An arrangement should explain how each fit parent stays involved: through suitable time together, reliable communication and participation in ordinary care.

**The policy argument is straightforward:** without practical commitments, a relationship can gradually disappear through distance, scheduling or adult conflict—even when neither parent is unfit. Children should not have to depend entirely on their separated parents getting along.

### 3. Involvement does not mean compulsory 50–50 care
Equal time can be unsuitable because of schooling, distance, age or a child’s individual needs. Maintaining both relationships can take different forms.

The requirement should therefore protect **meaningful involvement, not identical schedules**. It should not automatically require joint decision-making or give either parent a veto over the other.

### 4. Safety and the child’s interests must control
Fitness must be assessed seriously, including family violence, coercive control, abuse and neglect. Children’s views and changing needs must also matter. Courts should retain power to restrict involvement where necessary, with clear reasons.

Australia’s [Family Law Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00275/latest/text) already makes children’s best interests paramount and recognises the benefit of safe relationships with parents. This proposal would give that principle practical force.

**Bottom line:** where two parents can safely contribute to a child’s upbringing, parenting arrangements should preserve those contributions—not allow separation alone to turn one parent into a bystander.

## [2] CRITIC

## Support both relationships—but do not make them mandatory

The advocate’s strongest point is that separation should not unnecessarily deprive a child of a loving parent. I agree with that goal. But **a parent being fit and able does not establish that a legally required level of involvement serves this particular child’s interests.**

### 1. “Fit and able” is not enough
Two competent, safe parents may still face circumstances where maintaining both parents’ “meaningful involvement” imposes substantial burdens on the child: long-distance travel, disrupted routines, or demands that conflict with an older child’s considered wishes.

These are not necessarily reasons to declare either parent unfit. They are reasons to assess the *arrangement*, rather than make parental fitness trigger a mandatory outcome.

The advocate rightly rejects compulsory equal time, but that leaves a central question unanswered: **what minimum involvement must courts enforce, and at what cost to the child?**

### 2. Existing law already protects relationships—without guaranteeing an outcome
[Section 60CC of Australia’s Family Law Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00275/latest/text) already requires courts to consider the benefit of safe relationships with parents, alongside the child’s views, developmental needs and other relevant circumstances.

So the choice is not between a new requirement and leaving everything to parental goodwill. Parenting orders can already establish enforceable arrangements.

The unsupported assumption is that an additional mandatory rule would produce better outcomes than this child-focused assessment. The opening argument supplies no evidence establishing that.

### 3. The proposed exceptions expose the problem
The advocate says children’s interests must control and courts must retain discretion. That creates a dilemma:

- If courts can depart from the requirement whenever the child’s overall interests justify it, the proposal adds little to existing law.
- If departure requires proving unfitness or danger, it wrongly sidelines other important needs and preferences.

[Article 9 of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child](https://www.ohchr.org/en/instruments-mechanisms/instruments/convention-rights-child) supports contact **unless contrary to the child’s best interests**—a broader exception than parental unfitness or unsafe contact.

**Bottom line:** preserve both relationships wherever that benefits the child, and enforce suitable orders. But require courts to justify arrangements by the child’s needs—not require children’s lives to accommodate a predetermined level of parental involvement.

## [3] ADVOCATE

## Require a workable relationship plan—not a fixed share of the child

The critic’s strongest objection is correct: **a parent’s fitness does not make every arrangement involving that parent good for the child.** But that argues against rigid schedules—not against requiring arrangements to protect both relationships.

### 1. The requirement should govern the plan, not dictate the hours

Where both parents are fit and able, parenting arrangements should specify how each will remain involved, in ways suited to the child’s circumstances.

There need not be a universal minimum number of nights or calls. A long-distance arrangement might use school-holiday visits and manageable communication rather than frequent travel. An older child’s schooling, friendships and considered wishes should shape the arrangement.

The enforceable obligations would be those in the **individual arrangement**, not an abstract demand for “enough involvement.” A parent’s fitness would trigger a duty to plan for their involvement—not entitlement to their preferred schedule.

### 2. Exceptions do not make a requirement pointless

The critic presents an unnecessarily narrow choice: either the requirement overrides children’s interests, or it adds nothing.

There is a meaningful middle position:

> **Require arrangements to maintain both fit parents’ involvement unless doing so would be contrary to the child’s best interests, and require reasons for that departure.**

That differs from merely considering a relationship as one factor. It requires the decision-maker to address *how the relationship will actually be maintained*, or explain why it should not be.

Crucially, an exception would not require branding a parent unfit. A child’s particular needs could justify restrictions even where neither parent has done anything wrong.

### 3. Existing powers do not settle what the law should require

The critic rightly notes that Australian courts can already make enforceable parenting orders. My opening argument should not imply otherwise.

But **having power to protect a relationship and having a duty to make practical provision for it are different things**. The proposed improvement is an explicit planning obligation, consistent with the paramountcy of children’s best interests under [section 60CA of the Family Law Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00275/latest/text).

I am making a policy argument, not claiming that comparative evidence has established better outcomes from this precise reform.

### Bottom line

The critic identifies possible costs of involvement, but those costs must be weighed against the possible cost of losing a safe, supportive parent. Neither should disappear from view.

Australia should require a practical answer to **“How will this child retain both fit parents?”**—while allowing a clearly justified, child-focused exception. That protects relationships without imposing equal time, compulsory cooperation or contact at any cost.

## [4] CRITIC

## A duty to explain is not a reason to mandate involvement

The advocate’s strongest proposal is to require a practical relationship plan, with child-focused exceptions—not fixed hours or equal time. That is more reasonable than a rigid contact rule. But **better planning does not require making continued involvement the legally preferred outcome.**

### 1. Require answers—not a predetermined answer
Courts could be required to explain how an arrangement addresses the child’s important relationships, stability, practical needs and wishes. That would address the advocate’s concern about relationships being overlooked.

The proposed rule goes further: it requires maintaining both parents’ involvement *unless there is sufficient reason not to*. That gives one consideration special starting weight.

The distinction matters. Instead of asking, “Which arrangement best serves this child?”, the decision risks becoming, “Is there enough justification to depart from involvement?” **My concern is a foreseeable consequence of that framing, not a claim that research has established its effects.**

### 2. The unresolved issue is who must justify what
Consider a teenager who wants a substantial break from contact with a safe, competent parent because the relationship has deteriorated. Neither parental unfitness nor danger is necessarily involved.

The advocate allows an exception—but the child’s preference now sits on the *exception* side of the argument. Why should maintaining involvement receive the initial legal advantage, rather than both options being assessed on their merits?

Children’s wishes should not automatically decide every case. Equally, they should not have to overcome a parent-centred starting point.

### 3. The proposed remedy still lacks a demonstrated target
The advocate fairly acknowledges that better outcomes from this precise reform have not been established. But another assumption remains unsupported: **that inadequate practical provision for relationships is a gap in the law, rather than a problem of compliance, resources or implementation.**

Existing law already considers safe parental relationships and permits detailed, enforceable orders. A further requirement cannot, by itself, supply affordable travel, reliable follow-through or a willing relationship.

Before imposing it, proponents should identify which existing failures it would actually fix—and why clearer reasons, better implementation or tailored support would not address them more directly.

**Bottom line:** require careful, transparent planning around the child’s needs. Do not turn one desirable possibility—both parents’ continuing involvement—into the outcome from which the child needs an exception.

## [5] ADVOCATE

## The starting point should protect the child’s relationships—not a parent’s entitlement

The critic’s strongest objection is that requiring involvement gives it an initial legal advantage. **Yes—but a limited preference for preserving safe parental relationships is defensible.** It need not outweigh the child’s welfare or require the child to prove a case against contact.

### 1. Preserving a relationship deserves weight, without deciding the whole case

The reason is child-centred: a parenting arrangement governs not just where a child sleeps, but whether each parent has a practical opportunity to continue caring for them.

**My policy judgment** is that separation should not make either fit parent’s contribution optional without a child-focused reason. This does not establish that any particular schedule is beneficial. It establishes that unnecessarily excluding a capable parent is something arrangements should prevent.

The critic’s approach would require decision-makers to *address* relationships. But an explanation of a relationship’s importance is not necessarily a provision that sustains it. The proposed requirement closes that distinction: provide a workable route to continued involvement, unless the child’s interests justify otherwise.

### 2. The obligation belongs to adults—not to the child

The teenager example identifies an important limit. A mature teenager seeking a substantial break should not have to establish parental wrongdoing, litigate against a parent, or submit to compulsory emotional closeness.

A court could authorise a pause, taking the teenager’s reasons seriously, without declaring the parent unfit. Any future contact or review should likewise reflect the teenager’s circumstances—not become an automatic route back to compelled visits.

**What the law can require is appropriate adult conduct: facilitating suitable opportunities, not manufacturing affection.** Treating those as equivalent makes the proposal seem more intrusive than it needs to be.

### 3. Implementation and legal duties address different problems

The critic is right that this rule cannot pay travel costs or make an unwilling parent reliable. Nor have I established that inadequate planning is widespread.

But that does not make implementation support a substitute for a legal obligation. They answer different questions:

- **Support and resources:** Can this arrangement actually work?
- **The requirement:** Does it make practical provision for both fit parents, or explain why that would not serve this child?

Whether failures arise often enough to justify reform needs empirical examination. That uncertainty limits claims about the reform’s likely impact; it does not defeat its underlying justification.

### Bottom line

The defensible requirement is **a duty on adults to preserve feasible, beneficial parental involvement—not a duty on children to accommodate parents**. Children’s interests remain paramount, but losing a fit parent’s involvement should require a reason grounded in those interests, rather than happen by omission.

## [6] CRITIC

## Protect opportunities—but do not legally guarantee involvement

The advocate’s strongest point is that a child should not lose a valuable parental relationship simply because adults failed to plan for it. But **preventing avoidable exclusion is narrower than requiring both parents’ continuing involvement**. The proposal still moves between those two goals.

### 1. An opportunity is not a relationship—and enforcing them means different things

The advocate now proposes requiring adults to facilitate suitable opportunities, rather than guaranteeing contact or affection. That distinction is sensible, but it leaves an important enforcement question:

**What happens when reasonable opportunities exist, yet a fit and able parent does not consistently take them?**

- If providing the opportunity satisfies the law, the requirement does not ensure continued involvement. It is a rule about facilitation.
- If continued involvement itself must be secured, somebody must face additional obligations despite having already made reasonable efforts.

This matters because “fit and able” does not necessarily mean willing, dependable or responsive to this particular child. The proposal assumes that a workable plan can bridge that gap; it has not established when that assumption holds.

### 2. Duties imposed on adults can still burden children

I accept that the advocate is not proposing compulsory emotional closeness. But describing the obligation as an adult duty does not settle its effects on children.

For example, a parent may comply by keeping a child available for scheduled calls that the other parent repeatedly misses. The obligation formally belongs to the adult, while the child experiences the waiting and disappointment.

A sensible arrangement could change. But that raises further questions: **Who can suspend an unsuccessful arrangement, what justification is needed, and must the family seek another legal decision?**

These are foreseeable implementation concerns, not claims of proven effects. They nevertheless need answers before making involvement mandatory.

### 3. The scope of the requirement matters

Would this apply only when courts decide contested cases, or also to workable arrangements parents reach themselves?

For court cases, the advocate needs to show why a specific duty to address possible exclusion is insufficient. For private arrangements, the proposal risks requiring families to formalise or justify flexible practices that already serve their children.

The advocate’s concession that the prevalence of inadequate planning needs investigation is therefore substantial. It leaves us unsure **which families need this intervention and whether its additional obligations are proportionate**.

### Bottom line

Protect children against unjustified obstruction, and make practical arrangements where these benefit them. But do not equate an enforceable opportunity with sustained parental involvement—or assume that imposing duties on adults makes the resulting burdens child-free. The narrower protections are defensible; the broader mandatory outcome is not.

## [7] ADVOCATE

## Require practical involvement—not a guaranteed emotional outcome

The critic’s strongest objection is about enforcement: **what happens when an arrangement is reasonable on paper but repeatedly disappoints the child?** A defensible requirement must address that—not simply order contact and leave families to cope.

### 1. Require conduct that adults can control

The requirement should bind **both parents**, not merely the parent making the child available:

- Each must make reasonable, agreed contributions to the child’s life.
- Neither may unjustifiably obstruct the other’s involvement.
- Neither must keep following arrangements that are demonstrably harming the child without a route to adjustment.

A parent who repeatedly misses agreed contact has not fulfilled their responsibility simply because the other parent offered opportunities. But the remedy should not be ever more compulsory waiting for the child.

**The critic’s either-or framing is too strong:** a law can require reasonable conduct towards an outcome without guaranteeing that outcome. Requiring parents to maintain involvement is not promising that legislation can manufacture a successful relationship.

### 2. Build failure safeguards into the arrangement

For the missed-call example, a proposed arrangement could require the calling parent to confirm beforehand. Without confirmation, the child continues their evening rather than waiting by the phone. Repeated failures could trigger a less disruptive arrangement.

These are **proposed safeguards**, not claims about what current Australian law automatically provides.

Parents should be able to agree adjustments without returning to court. Contested orders should, where appropriate, specify fallback arrangements and review triggers. Disputed major changes may still need legal resolution; that is a genuine cost, but disputes over existing parenting orders can also require resolution.

Persistent unwillingness matters too. It is a reason to reassess what involvement is realistically beneficial—not to punish the child or require the other parent to compensate indefinitely.

### 3. Apply the requirement proportionately

I would distinguish between:

- **Court-determined arrangements:** require concrete provision for both fit parents’ involvement, with child-focused reasons for any departure.
- **Private arrangements:** require neither prescribed schedules nor routine court approval. Flexible, functioning involvement should satisfy the requirement without additional paperwork.

The target is exclusion or neglect of a parental relationship in the arrangement—not informality itself.

### 4. Preventing obstruction is not enough

The critic supports protection against unjustified obstruction. But neither parent needs to obstruct contact deliberately for an arrangement to leave one parent without any workable role. **A prohibition on interference does not itself allocate responsibility for maintaining involvement.**

That is the additional duty I defend. Its benefits and administrative costs should be evaluated; I am not claiming proven improvements from this precise reform.

**Bottom line:** Australia should require parenting arrangements to give both fit parents real, practicable responsibilities and opportunities to remain involved, subject to the child’s best interests. Failure should prompt child-protective adjustment—not abandonment of the principle, and never contact at any cost.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

Australia should require family law parenting arrangements to maintain both parents’ involvement in their children’s lives when both parents are deemed fit and able.

The Advocate qualified this requirement throughout the debate: involvement must be safe, practicable and consistent with the child’s best interests, rather than entail equal time or guaranteed contact.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Protect children’s relationships after separation.** Where both parents can provide safe, competent care, separation should not unnecessarily deprive children of either parent’s support and contribution.
- **Require practical provision, not merely consideration.** Arrangements should explain how both parents will remain involved through suitable time, communication and care—or give child-focused reasons for departing from that goal. Existing powers to make orders are different from an explicit duty to plan.
- **Avoid rigid entitlements.** The proposal does not require equal time, universal minimum contact, automatic joint decision-making or parental vetoes. Arrangements should reflect distance, schooling, age and children’s wishes.
- **Preserve best-interests exceptions.** Restrictions or pauses could be justified without declaring a parent unfit. A mature teenager’s reasons for seeking a break should be taken seriously.
- **Impose reasonable adult responsibilities.** Both parents should contribute reasonably and avoid unjustified obstruction. The law can require conduct aimed at sustaining involvement without guaranteeing affection or a successful relationship.
- **Allow proportionate adjustment.** Proposed safeguards include advance confirmation of calls, fallback arrangements and review triggers. Functioning private arrangements should not require prescribed schedules, routine court approval or additional paperwork.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **Fitness alone does not establish that involvement benefits a particular child.** Travel, disrupted routines, developmental needs and an older child’s wishes may justify reduced involvement even where both parents are safe and competent.
- **Existing law already addresses parental relationships.** The Critic argued that courts already consider safe relationships alongside other best-interests factors and can make detailed, enforceable orders.
- **A mandate gives involvement privileged starting weight.** Requiring reasons to depart from involvement risks shifting the question from which arrangement best serves the child to whether there is sufficient justification for an exception.
- **The reform’s target and benefits remain unestablished.** The Advocate did not demonstrate that inadequate planning is a widespread legal gap, rather than a problem of compliance, resources or implementation.
- **Opportunities and sustained involvement are different.** A fit and able parent may still be unwilling or unreliable. A workable plan cannot necessarily secure dependable participation.
- **Adult duties can still burden children.** Missed calls, disappointing contact and procedures for changing failed arrangements may impose costs on children and families. The scope and proportionality of the requirement therefore matter.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Rigid outcome versus flexible planning:** In response to concerns about mandatory minimum involvement, the Advocate clarified that enforceable obligations would come from individual arrangements, not universal schedules. The Critic maintained that even a flexible requirement creates a legally preferred outcome.
- **Existing law versus an additional duty:** The Advocate acknowledged that courts already possess enforcement powers but distinguished those powers from a duty to make practical provision. The Critic argued that clearer explanations and child-focused planning could address omissions without mandating involvement.
- **Exceptions and children’s wishes:** The Advocate allowed best-interests exceptions, including a teenager’s substantial break from contact. The Critic questioned why such preferences should begin on the exception side of the analysis.
- **Facilitation versus actual involvement:** The Critic challenged whether providing opportunities satisfies the requirement when a parent fails to participate. The Advocate replied that both parents would owe reasonable conduct duties, while acknowledging that legislation cannot guarantee successful relationships.
- **Failed arrangements:** The Advocate answered concerns about repeated disappointment with proposed confirmation procedures, fallback plans and reassessment. Major disputed changes might still require legal resolution, which the Advocate accepted as a genuine cost.
- **Evidence:** The Advocate conceded that neither improved outcomes from this precise reform nor widespread inadequate planning had been established, but defended its underlying policy justification. The Critic regarded those gaps as important to whether additional duties were warranted.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides agreed that:

- Children should not unnecessarily lose valuable relationships with safe, capable parents.
- Children’s best interests, safety, individual circumstances and views must guide arrangements.
- Parental fitness does not make every proposed arrangement beneficial.
- Compulsory equal time and manufactured emotional closeness are inappropriate.
- Practical planning and suitable enforceable arrangements can be valuable.
- Legal requirements alone cannot supply resources or make an unwilling parent dependable.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **Legal starting point:** Whether preserving both fit parents’ involvement deserves a limited legal preference, or should remain one consideration assessed without that preference.
- **Need for reform:** Whether an explicit planning obligation fills a meaningful gap beyond existing law and narrower protections against exclusion.
- **Enforcement:** What reasonable participation requires, how persistent non-participation should be handled and when failed arrangements may be changed.
- **Proportionality and evidence:** Whether the proposal’s anticipated benefits justify its additional obligations and potential costs, given the acknowledged evidentiary gaps.
- **Practical reach:** Whether the distinction between court-determined and informal private arrangements sufficiently resolves concerns about scope.

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate moved from a broad requirement for both parents’ involvement toward a more qualified proposal for practical, adjustable responsibilities subject to the child’s best interests. The Advocate defended this as protection against relationships being lost through omission, not as a parental entitlement. The Critic supported preserving beneficial relationships but opposed giving involvement a legally preferred status without establishing the need for that reform and resolving its burdens. The central disagreement concerned the appropriate legal starting point, rather than the value of safe parental relationships themselves.
