# A Multi Agent AI Policy Debate
AI can make mistakes. Always critically evaluate the content of the debate.

## Claim

The Australian government should abandon its net-zero emissions target for 2050 and end large-scale renewable energy mandates.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/real-impacts-of-net-zero-australia

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

**Australia should abandon its binding commitment to net zero by 2050 and end large-scale renewable-energy mandates—not abandon efforts to cut emissions.** The better policy is to reduce emissions where doing so delivers good value, while making reliable, affordable energy the non-negotiable priority.

### 1. A fixed deadline is not a guarantee of a sensible transition

Net zero is a destination, not a costed plan. Electricity can be decarbonised more readily than activities such as aviation, agriculture and some heavy industries. Committing every sector to a fixed national deadline risks increasingly expensive interventions as the easier reductions run out.

**The argument is about the policy trade-off:** governments should assess the benefits and costs of further reductions, rather than treat achieving a particular date as overriding. Emissions should fall because workable alternatives become available—not because households and businesses must absorb whatever costs meeting a deadline requires.

### 2. Cheap renewable generation is not the same as a cheap, reliable electricity system

Wind and solar can produce inexpensive electricity. But the relevant question is what it costs to deliver power whenever customers need it, including transmission, storage, backup and grid services.

[AEMO’s 2024 Integrated System Plan](https://aemo.com.au/energy-systems/major-publications/integrated-system-plan-isp/2024-integrated-system-plan-isp) identifies renewables connected by transmission, supported by storage and gas, as the lowest-cost pathway **within its framework for meeting government policies**. That is important evidence—but it does not independently establish that those policies and deadlines are preferable to every alternative.

The strongest objection is therefore not “renewables don’t work.” It is: **do not mandate the generation mix before testing the full cost and deliverability of the system.**

### 3. Governments should reward outcomes, not favour technologies

Renewable-specific requirements privilege a category of technology rather than the services Australians actually need: dependable electricity and lower emissions.

Ending those mandates would let renewables compete against efficiency improvements, storage, cleaner firm generation and other options on their merits. Where renewables offer the best overall value, they should win without a compulsory market share.

### 4. Domestic targets must be judged by global results

Climate change is real, and cumulative global emissions matter. But if Australian production closes and equivalent goods are produced overseas with higher emissions, Australia’s reported progress can exaggerate the climate benefit.

That is a **risk, not an inevitable outcome**. It nevertheless supports evaluating policy against global emissions and industrial competitiveness—not simply domestic accounting.

**Bottom line:** replace the 2050 commitment and renewable mandates with transparent, regularly reviewed emissions policies, technology-neutral competition and enforceable reliability standards. That preserves climate action while refusing to make an uncertain timetable more important than practical results.

## [2] CRITIC

**Australia should keep its 2050 net-zero target and improve how it gets there—not abandon the destination because some policies may need fixing.**

### 1. The strongest objection supports better planning, not repeal

The advocate is right: cheap wind and solar generation do not automatically mean cheap, reliable electricity. Transmission, storage, backup and delivery risks must count.

But [AEMO’s 2024 Integrated System Plan](https://aemo.com.au/energy-systems/major-publications/integrated-system-plan-isp/2024-integrated-system-plan-isp) explicitly considers those system requirements and identifies a renewables-led system, backed by storage and gas, as the lowest-cost pathway under government policies.

Its policy assumptions limit what that finding proves. **They do not establish that abandoning those policies would be cheaper.** The advocate offers no comparative analysis showing that repeal would deliver lower total costs at comparable reliability, once emissions are also accounted for.

The reasonable response is to test costs, strengthen reliability safeguards and revise poorly performing measures—not assume repeal wins.

### 2. A national net-zero target does not require every sector to eliminate emissions

The argument about aviation, agriculture and heavy industry conflates a national **net** target with a zero-emissions requirement for each sector. Net zero allows remaining emissions to be balanced by removals.

Removals have costs and practical limits; they are not a blank cheque. But the national target still permits different sectors to move at different speeds.

More importantly, waiting until alternatives become attractive has its own cost. The [IPCC’s synthesis report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/) finds that cumulative carbon emissions largely determine warming and that delayed mitigation increases risks and can lock in high-emissions infrastructure. **A cost-benefit assessment must count the damage from delay, not just the expense of acting.**

### 3. “Technology-neutral competition” needs an actual emissions constraint

Technology neutrality is a useful principle. It is not, by itself, a climate policy.

If producers do not face the climate costs of their emissions, competition on electricity prices alone does not measure their full cost to society. The advocate therefore needs to specify a replacement—such as enforceable emissions limits or carbon pricing—and show that it would achieve adequate reductions.

Some renewable requirements may warrant redesign. That does not justify ending large-scale requirements wholesale, especially without a credible replacement. Nor does it justify removing the long-term target that helps investors assess future demand for cleaner technologies.

### 4. Carbon leakage warrants safeguards, not surrender

Moving production overseas can undermine emissions cuts. But the advocate acknowledges this is a risk, not an established outcome across Australia’s economy. Targeted assistance for exposed industries and trade measures are more direct responses than abandoning the national target.

**Bottom line:** the advocate identifies genuine implementation problems but does not demonstrate that abandoning the target and mandates would solve them. Keep the destination; demand transparent costs, reliable power and accountable policies along the way.

## [3] ADVOCATE

**The critic’s strongest point is that I have not proved repeal would produce a cheaper, equally reliable system. That is fair. My case is not that repeal guarantees lower bills; it is that Australia should replace a fixed emissions destination and technology mandates with policies that make their costs—and trade-offs—explicit.**

### 1. A credible replacement must charge for emissions

The critic is right: competition that ignores climate damage is not genuinely fair competition. I would therefore replace the target and large-scale renewable mandates with:

- **A predictable emissions price**, with a published forward schedule and independent periodic reviews informed by climate damages, international action and economic conditions.
- **Technology-neutral procurement of reliability services**, so generators, storage and demand reduction compete to supply what the grid needs.
- **Competitive support for research and verified emissions reductions**, rather than compulsory renewable market shares.
- **Protection for existing contracts**, while ending new renewable-specific obligations prospectively.

This is a proposed alternative, not a proven cheaper outcome. Its advantage is that it exposes the price of additional emissions reductions instead of leaving governments to reconcile a fixed deadline with uncertain future costs.

### 2. “Improve the pathway” leaves the central trade-off unresolved

The critic says Australia should retain 2050 while revising poorly performing measures. But what happens if realistic replacement measures cannot meet that date at an acceptable cost?

There are only three answers: accept higher costs, miss the target, or change the target. **Keeping the destination does not resolve that choice—it postpones it.**

The [AEMO plan](https://aemo.com.au/energy-systems/major-publications/integrated-system-plan-isp/2024-integrated-system-plan-isp) is valuable evidence about how to meet government policies. As the critic acknowledges, it does not establish whether retaining those policies beats a different emissions trajectory. Neither side can claim that comparison has been settled.

My policy judgment is that government should commit to a durable decision-making framework, not an endpoint whose ultimate demands remain uncertain.

### 3. Net zero allows residual emissions—but does not remove the cost question

I accept the correction: a national net-zero target does **not** require every industry to eliminate emissions. My concern is the cost of the final balance, including removals—not uniform sector-by-sector elimination.

The [IPCC](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/) establishes that cumulative emissions matter and delay increases climate risks. That supports meaningful action now. **It does not, by itself, establish that Australia’s particular 2050 deadline is preferable to every alternative policy.** Abandoning the deadline need not mean waiting passively for cleaner technology: an emissions price creates an immediate incentive to cut pollution.

### 4. Investor certainty does not justify every commitment

A target signals direction, but does not itself guarantee a workable investment framework. Published emissions prices, clear connection rules and dependable reliability procurement can also guide investment.

Likewise, carbon-leakage safeguards may be warranted; leakage alone is not my case for repeal.

**Bottom line:** keep climate damage in economic decisions, but abandon the fixed 2050 obligation and renewable-specific mandates. Make emissions reductions compete on their actual value, and make reliability an explicit requirement—not an assumption.

## [4] CRITIC

**The advocate now offers a serious alternative: price emissions rather than ignore their costs. But that supports changing policy instruments—not abandoning the net-zero target.**

### 1. A carbon price and a net-zero target solve different problems

A carbon price tells businesses what emitting will cost. A target specifies the outcome government is trying to achieve. **Australia can use both.**

The strongest argument for the advocate’s approach is that it makes the cost of additional reductions visible. That is valuable. But a published price schedule does not establish how far emissions will fall: that depends on technology, investment and businesses’ responses.

This creates a trade-off the proposal does not resolve:

- If the price remains predictable, emissions may substantially exceed expectations.
- If reviews raise the price to correct that outcome, businesses face changing costs—the uncertainty the advocate criticises in target-based policy.

Neither approach eliminates uncertainty. **Abandoning the target shifts more uncertainty from the cost of action to the amount of pollution.** The advocate has not shown why that is the better balance.

### 2. Pricing climate damage is not an objective escape from judgment

The proposed price would reflect climate damages, international action and economic conditions. But how would those considerations be weighted?

Valuing climate damage requires judgments about future generations, uncertain severe harms and damage outside Australia. Independent reviews can make those judgments more transparent; they cannot make them disappear.

The [IPCC synthesis report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/) finds that risks increase with every increment of warming and that some changes are irreversible. That does not prove Australia’s exact deadline is uniquely optimal. It does mean **an affordable-looking emissions price can still produce an unacceptably damaging outcome**. Price transparency is not enough; the resulting emissions pathway also needs scrutiny.

### 3. A possible future difficulty does not justify repeal now

The advocate asks what happens if meeting 2050 becomes unacceptably expensive. That is a legitimate contingency—not evidence that it will occur.

A responsible framework can retain the target while publishing projected costs, testing alternative pathways and independently reviewing progress. If credible evidence later demonstrates an unavoidable conflict, government can confront it explicitly.

Repealing now instead assumes that the benefits of flexibility outweigh the risks of weaker ambition and policy disruption. No comparative evidence has established that assumption.

### 4. Renewable mandates require a separate judgment

Some renewable-specific measures may be inefficient and deserve replacement. But emissions pricing alone does not necessarily solve shared transmission planning, connection bottlenecks or coordination between generators and storage.

That is not proof that every mandate should stay. It is a reason to assess each measure’s purpose and replacement before ending it.

**Bottom line:** adopt transparent emissions pricing and technology-neutral competition where they work. Keep the net-zero target as the outcome against which those tools are judged. The advocate has made a case for better instruments, not for abandoning the destination.

## [5] ADVOCATE

**The critic is right that removing the target trades some emissions certainty for greater cost control. My argument is that Australia should make that trade explicitly—not pretend a national 2050 endpoint guarantees the climate outcome we actually care about.**

### 1. A national endpoint is not a global climate safeguard

The strongest objection is that an emissions price might deliver insufficient reductions. That is a real risk.

But distinguish three things: Australia’s emissions in 2050, Australia’s cumulative emissions, and global warming. They are related, not interchangeable. As the [IPCC explains](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/), cumulative global carbon emissions largely determine warming. **Two pathways reaching Australian net zero in 2050 could produce substantially different cumulative emissions.**

That does not make the target worthless. It does weaken the argument that retaining it supplies the outcome certainty missing from pricing.

My replacement should therefore include independently published emissions projections, cumulative-emissions accounting and assessments of climate risks—not just a price schedule. The distinction is that disappointing projections would trigger a public reassessment of stronger action, rather than an automatic obligation to achieve one endpoint regardless of the trade-offs.

### 2. Combining a price with the target does not avoid choosing priorities

Yes, Australia can have both. But suppose the published emissions price does not deliver net zero by 2050. Government must then raise the price, impose additional restrictions, or relax the target.

**If the target always prevails, the price is not a cost-control mechanism; it is simply one instrument for meeting an overriding quantity commitment.**

The critic correctly notes that climate valuations involve judgments about future generations and severe risks. My proposal does not eliminate those judgments. It requires them to be exposed when policy is reviewed. The preference for that approach is a **policy judgment**, not a claim that economics supplies a uniquely correct answer.

### 3. “Retain it until a conflict appears” is not costless either

Investment decisions happen before 2050. If government intends to reconsider the deadline when costs become unacceptable, businesses need to know what that means now.

The critic’s position consequently needs its own clarification: **is 2050 overriding, or conditional—and under what conditions?** Deferring that answer preserves uncertainty rather than resolving it.

This does not prove repeal is cheaper. It supports replacing an ambiguous commitment with explicit review rules, while protecting existing contracts and maintaining incentives to cut emissions.

### 4. Grid coordination does not require renewable quotas

Transmission planning, connection reform and coordination remain necessary under my proposal. Ending renewable mandates need not end any of them.

The critic identifies genuine coordination problems but does not establish why compulsory renewable deployment is the necessary remedy. Government can procure dependable capacity, coordinate network construction and price pollution without prescribing a renewable share.

**Bottom line:** abandon the fixed 2050 target and renewable-specific mandates, while retaining active climate policy. Judge that policy against projected climate benefits, cumulative emissions, costs and reliability—not compliance with a date or technology quota alone.

## [6] CRITIC

**The advocate’s strongest point is that a 2050 endpoint neither controls cumulative emissions nor guarantees affordable implementation. But that argues for a better framework around the target—not removing it.**

### 1. Fix the endpoint’s weakness rather than discard it

Two pathways reaching net zero in 2050 can indeed produce different amounts of warming. The answer is to assess **both cumulative emissions and the endpoint**, with interim milestones that discourage postponing reductions.

The proposed replacement would publish disappointing projections and trigger a reassessment. That provides information, but no commitment to correct the problem. **An emissions report is a warning light, not a brake.**

The unsupported assumption is that repeated discretionary reviews will balance climate risks and immediate costs better than reviews conducted against a standing emissions commitment. That might happen, but the advocate has not demonstrated it.

### 2. Here is the clarification on whether 2050 is “overriding”

**Keep 2050 as a binding planning commitment, not a blank cheque for any policy claiming to serve it.**

Government should publish costs and delivery risks, reject wasteful measures, and change instruments when better options emerge. Changing the target itself should require public evidence that feasible alternatives cannot meet it without disproportionate harm—not merely that the current policy mix is expensive or unpopular.

That is a meaningful distinction: **a commitment with a demanding justification for revision is stronger than an undertaking to reconsider ambition periodically.** Neither guarantees certainty, but they place different burdens on governments seeking to weaken action.

Moreover, the advocate’s reviewed emissions price does not establish a firm cost ceiling either. Prices could rise after reviews, while reliability procurement and other support would incur additional costs. Calling this “cost control” therefore promises more than the proposal currently specifies.

### 3. A national target also serves international cooperation

Australia cannot determine global warming alone. But that is also why national commitments matter.

The [Paris Agreement](https://unfccc.int/process-and-meetings/the-paris-agreement/the-paris-agreement) combines successive national commitments, transparency and collective assessment. It does **not** establish that Australia’s precise 2050 deadline is uniquely optimal.

The policy inference is narrower: durable national commitments help countries judge whether others intend to contribute. Abandoning Australia’s endpoint while retaining a revisable price could weaken that assurance. Publishing domestic projections does not fully replace it.

The advocate evaluates flexibility’s domestic benefits without establishing that these cooperative costs are smaller.

### 4. Renewable mandates do not have to be indispensable to be worthwhile

I accept that grid coordination can occur without renewable quotas. But **“not necessary” does not mean “not beneficial.”**

The relevant comparison is each mandate against a concrete replacement: emissions delivered, total system costs, reliability and transition risks. Technology-neutral procurement deserves consideration, but its superiority should be demonstrated rather than assumed from its label.

**Bottom line:** strengthen cumulative-emissions accountability, make costs explicit, and replace individual renewable measures where better alternatives are established. None of that requires abandoning net zero—or ending renewable requirements wholesale before the replacement has been shown to work.

## [7] ADVOCATE

**The critic’s strongest objection is that my replacement could allow governments to acknowledge inadequate emissions cuts without correcting them. That is a genuine weakness—but it can be addressed without restoring a binding 2050 endpoint or renewable quotas.**

### 1. Make the replacement harder to evade

I would strengthen the proposed emissions price with:

- A **legislated annual price escalator**, continuing unless Parliament changes it.
- Independent reviews assessing cumulative emissions, climate risks, household costs and competitiveness.
- A requirement that government publicly respond to those reviews and justify departures from their recommendations.
- Protection for existing contracts, with renewable-specific obligations phased out prospectively.

The “brake” is the continuing charge on pollution, not the emissions report. Reviews would assess whether to strengthen it; doing nothing would not freeze the incentive.

This does **not** guarantee a particular emissions outcome or cap total transition costs. I accept that correction. It makes the policy’s stringency explicit while preserving the ability to reconsider its trajectory.

### 2. The disagreement is now about which commitment deserves priority

The critic allows revision of 2050 if feasible alternatives would cause “disproportionate harm.” That is more reasonable than an unconditional deadline. But it leaves a crucial question: **disproportionate relative to what climate benefit, and judged by whom?**

Both approaches ultimately require political judgments about costs, risks and fairness. Keeping the target does not remove that discretion; it places a stronger presumption behind one answer.

My position is that the durable commitment should be **to making polluters pay and steadily encouraging cleaner alternatives**, rather than to achieving a specified national balance in a particular year.

That is a policy judgment—not a demonstrated forecast that repeal will lower bills. Equally, the critic has not demonstrated that making 2050 the default produces the better balance.

### 3. International credibility need not depend on this particular endpoint

The [Paris Agreement](https://unfccc.int/process-and-meetings/the-paris-agreement/the-paris-agreement) relies on successive national contributions, transparency and collective assessment. Abandoning Australia’s domestic 2050 target would **not** erase its separate international obligations.

The critic reasonably identifies a diplomatic risk. But credibility should also depend on enacted measures and demonstrated reductions. A legislated, rising emissions price would be a substantive commitment—not merely a promise to reconsider action.

My inference is that Australia could defend that alternative internationally. I cannot guarantee that other countries would regard it as equally reassuring; neither has the critic established that this diplomatic disadvantage outweighs the benefits of policy flexibility.

### 4. Renewable mandates require a reason beyond cutting emissions

Under an emissions price, cleaner generation already gains a competitive advantage. A renewable mandate then adds another restriction: it favours *particular ways* of reducing emissions.

That can be justified by a distinct benefit, such as innovation—but that benefit needs its own evidence. Transmission bottlenecks justify transmission reform, not automatically renewable quotas.

**The stronger default is therefore to end renewable-specific deployment requirements while retaining targeted solutions to identifiable problems.** Renewables can still expand wherever they offer good value.

**Bottom line:** replace the endpoint and technology mandates with an enforceable, increasingly stringent incentive to cut pollution. Preserve climate action, but make its strength accountable to explicit evidence and trade-offs rather than compliance with 2050 alone.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

**The Australian government should abandon its net-zero emissions target for 2050 and end large-scale renewable energy mandates.**

The Advocate supports replacing these commitments with emissions pricing and technology-neutral policies—not abandoning climate action. The Critic supports retaining the target while improving policy instruments and assessing renewable requirements individually.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Prioritise explicit costs and trade-offs over a fixed endpoint.** A 2050 commitment could require increasingly costly reductions or removals. Policy should weigh climate benefits, affordability, reliability and competitiveness rather than presume the deadline takes priority.
- **Assess whole-system electricity costs.** Inexpensive renewable generation does not necessarily mean inexpensive reliable supply once transmission, storage, backup and grid services are included. AEMO’s modelling identifies a lowest-cost pathway within government policy settings, not whether those settings outperform all alternatives.
- **Replace mandates with technology-neutral incentives.** The Advocate’s proposal developed into:
  - A predictable emissions price with a legislated annual escalator.
  - Independent reviews of cumulative emissions, climate risks, household costs and competitiveness.
  - Public government responses explaining departures from review recommendations.
  - Technology-neutral reliability procurement and competitive support for research and verified emissions reductions.
  - Continued grid planning and connection reform, protection for existing contracts, and prospective phase-out of renewable-specific obligations.
- **Judge climate policy beyond a single national endpoint.** Different pathways to net zero in 2050 can produce different cumulative emissions. Domestic reductions should also be assessed for carbon leakage and their global effects.
- **Commit to an enduring incentive rather than a fixed outcome.** The Advocate argues that making polluters pay provides a substantive commitment while preserving flexibility. Renewable mandates would need evidence of distinct benefits beyond reductions already encouraged by emissions pricing.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **Implementation problems do not establish a case for repeal.** The Advocate has not demonstrated that abandoning the target and mandates would deliver a better balance of costs, reliability and emissions.
- **A price and a target serve different purposes.** An emissions price influences behaviour; a target specifies the intended outcome. They can operate together. Removing the target shifts uncertainty toward how much pollution will occur.
- **Climate costs and delay must count.** The Critic invokes the IPCC’s findings on cumulative emissions, increasing risks and infrastructure lock-in. Transparent prices alone do not ensure an acceptable emissions pathway.
- **Strengthen the target’s framework.** Cumulative-emissions assessment and interim milestones can address weaknesses in an endpoint-only approach. The Critic describes 2050 as a binding planning commitment, not a justification for wasteful policies, with revision requiring evidence that feasible alternatives would cause disproportionate harm.
- **Assess renewable requirements individually.** Some may deserve replacement, but their costs, emissions effects, reliability contribution and transition risks should be compared with concrete alternatives before repeal.
- **Consider investment and international credibility.** A durable target can guide investors and reassure other countries about Australia’s contribution. A revisable price may not provide equivalent assurance.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Net zero versus eliminating every sector’s emissions:** The Critic clarified that national net zero permits residual emissions balanced by removals. The Advocate accepted this and narrowed the concern to the cost of the final balance.
- **What AEMO’s findings establish:** The Advocate stressed that the modelling is conditional on government policies. The Critic agreed but argued that this limitation does not demonstrate repeal would be cheaper. The Advocate acknowledged that no cheaper, equally reliable replacement had been proved.
- **Technology neutrality without climate accountability:** The Critic argued that competition must account for emissions costs. The Advocate agreed and specified an emissions price, later strengthened with a legislated escalator and review obligations.
- **Cost certainty versus emissions certainty:** The Critic argued that predictable prices may miss emissions expectations, while corrective price increases reintroduce cost uncertainty. The Advocate accepted that the replacement guarantees neither a particular emissions outcome nor a ceiling on total transition costs.
- **Whether 2050 should prevail when costs rise:** The Advocate pressed the Critic to explain when the target could change. The Critic allowed revision under a demanding evidentiary threshold. The Advocate replied that deciding what constitutes disproportionate harm still requires political judgments about benefits, risks and fairness.
- **Reporting versus corrective action:** The Critic argued that publishing inadequate projections does not require governments to fix them. The Advocate responded that an automatically rising emissions charge would continue strengthening incentives even without further action.
- **Grid coordination and mandates:** The Advocate argued that coordination does not require renewable quotas. The Critic accepted this but maintained that a measure can be beneficial without being indispensable.
- **International reassurance:** The Critic warned of cooperative costs from abandoning the endpoint. The Advocate argued that enacted pricing and demonstrated reductions could support credibility, while acknowledging that equal international reassurance was not guaranteed.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides agree that:

- Climate change warrants meaningful action, and cumulative global emissions matter.
- Electricity policy must consider total system costs, reliability and delivery risks—not generation costs alone.
- Emissions pricing and technology-neutral competition can be useful.
- Costs, projections and policy trade-offs should be transparent and independently scrutinised.
- A national net-zero target permits residual emissions and differing sectoral pathways.
- Carbon leakage is a risk warranting attention, not an established economy-wide consequence.
- Grid coordination can occur without renewable quotas.
- Neither approach removes uncertainty, and no comparative analysis presented settles the overall choice.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **Which commitment should anchor policy:** A binding emissions endpoint or an enduring, increasingly stringent price on pollution.
- **Where uncertainty should fall:** On the costs of achieving a target or on the emissions delivered by a price-based framework.
- **Who bears the burden of proof:** Whether repeal requires demonstrated superiority, or retaining fixed targets and technology-specific requirements requires stronger justification.
- **The default for renewable mandates:** Prospective phase-out versus case-by-case replacement after alternatives are shown to work better.
- **Accountability and credibility:** Whether legislated pricing and public reviews provide sufficient domestic discipline and international assurance without the 2050 endpoint.

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate developed into a disagreement about **how to structure climate commitments**, rather than whether Australia should reduce emissions. The Advocate proposed replacing the 2050 target and renewable mandates with an escalating emissions price, transparent reviews and technology-neutral measures. The Critic accepted many of those instruments but argued that they should operate alongside a retained target, with renewable requirements assessed individually.

Both recognised genuine uncertainties and implementation problems. They remained divided over whether flexibility under a price-led framework or the stronger presumption created by a standing emissions target offers the better balance of climate accountability, cost, reliability and credibility.
