## Claim

The Australian government should establish a Royal Commission to investigate federal, state, and territory governments’ management of the COVID-19 pandemic.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/covid

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

**Australia should establish a Royal Commission because decisions of extraordinary reach deserve independent scrutiny with the power to obtain the full record.** That is not a claim that every restriction was wrong—or that governments should have predicted everything. It is a case for accountability and better preparation.

### 1. The existing inquiry left a significant gap
The Commonwealth’s COVID-19 Response Inquiry examined the national response, but its terms of reference explicitly excluded actions taken unilaterally by state and territory governments.  
**Source:** [Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet—COVID-19 Response Inquiry](https://www.pmc.gov.au/covid-19-response-inquiry)

That matters because Australians experienced the pandemic through overlapping government decisions: federal responsibilities, state health orders, border restrictions, school closures and quarantine arrangements. **My argument:** we cannot fully assess that system while excluding some of its most consequential decisions.

### 2. A Royal Commission can investigate beyond what governments voluntarily disclose
Royal Commissions have statutory powers to compel evidence and witness testimony, subject to legal protections. Those powers matter when investigating how advice was weighed, why alternatives were rejected, and who was responsible.  
**Source:** [Royal Commissions Act 1902](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1902A00012/latest/text)

The inquiry should be established cooperatively across jurisdictions, with the legal authority needed to examine federal, state and territory conduct—not simply assume a Commonwealth commission has unlimited reach.

### 3. The question is not “restrictions or no restrictions”
A serious investigation would assess both the benefits of measures and their costs: illness and deaths prevented, disrupted education, delayed care, isolation, lost livelihoods and unequal burdens. It should judge decisions against **what was reasonably known at the time**, while asking whether governments adapted when evidence changed.

Australia’s successes deserve investigation too: future governments need to know what to repeat.

### 4. Cost and duplication are reasons to focus it—not abandon it
The strongest objection is that another inquiry would be expensive and backward-looking. The answer is a fixed timetable, targeted terms of reference, use of existing inquiry findings, privacy safeguards, and a requirement for public government responses.

**Bottom line:** a well-designed Royal Commission would not be a trial of pandemic restrictions. It would test whether governments used extraordinary powers competently, proportionately and transparently—and establish practical safeguards before the next emergency.

## [2] CRITIC

**The case for further scrutiny is strong; the case for a Royal Commission covering every level of government has not been established.** Accountability matters, but we should choose the investigation that fills specific gaps—not assume the most powerful instrument is necessarily the best.

### 1. A gap in the existing inquiry does not establish the need for a Royal Commission
The advocate’s strongest point is that the Commonwealth inquiry excluded actions taken unilaterally by states and territories. That is a genuine limitation.  
**Source:** [COVID-19 Response Inquiry—terms of reference and report](https://www.pmc.gov.au/covid-19-response-inquiry)

But exclusion from *that inquiry* is not proof that those decisions have escaped all scrutiny, or that only a Royal Commission can assess them. The missing step is to identify which important questions remain unanswered after existing national and state investigations—and why targeted follow-up reviews or parliamentary inquiries cannot answer them.

The appropriate response to a particular gap may be a particular investigation, not another review of the entire system.

### 2. Compulsory powers need a demonstrated purpose
Royal Commissions can compel documents and testimony.  
**Source:** [Royal Commissions Act 1902](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1902A00012/latest/text)

However, the advocate has not identified a crucial evidence gap caused by withheld documents or uncooperative witnesses. Without that connection, compulsory powers are a potential advantage, not a demonstrated necessity.

They also cannot resolve every disputed question. Obtaining every briefing document would not, by itself, establish precisely what would have happened under different border, school or lockdown policies. Those questions also require scientific and economic analysis—and some uncertainty will remain.

### 3. “Comprehensive” and “tightly targeted” pull in opposite directions
The proposal spans nine governments, numerous policy areas and several years. Yet it also promises a fixed timetable and limited duplication.

**My assessment:** that creates a practical trade-off. Narrow terms may leave out the very decisions used to justify the commission; broad terms risk lengthy proceedings and repeated evidence gathering. Cooperative establishment across jurisdictions adds another dependency—it is not something the Commonwealth can simply guarantee.

### 4. Recommendations are not implementation
A requirement for governments to respond publicly does not require them to fix anything. Another commission could produce valuable findings, but its creation is not itself preparedness.

**Better approach:** publish an audit of unresolved questions, commission targeted independent investigations where needed, and require public progress reports on reforms already identified. Escalate to compulsory investigation where evidence obstruction or serious unresolved failures justify it.

**Bottom line:** investigate demonstrated gaps first. The advocate has shown why scrutiny matters, but not why a nationwide Royal Commission is necessary.

## [3] ADVOCATE

**The critic is right that an omission does not automatically justify a Royal Commission. The stronger case is that the missing scrutiny concerns how governments worked together—and separate reviews are poorly suited to settling shared responsibility.**

### 1. Define the gap around decisions, not the entire pandemic
I would refine the proposal: establish a **jointly authorised Royal Commission into major pandemic decisions and accountability**, rather than repeat every scientific and operational review.

Its first task should be to map existing findings and identify unresolved questions, particularly:

- When federal and state responsibilities overlapped, who was responsible for failures—and did the arrangements themselves contribute?
- What evidence and criteria justified imposing, extending and lifting major restrictions?
- How were harms to children, people needing non-COVID care and disadvantaged communities weighed against infection risks?
- Were governments sufficiently transparent about uncertainty and disagreements?

These are proposed questions, **not assertions that every previous inquiry ignored them**. The Commonwealth inquiry’s explicit exclusion of unilateral state and territory actions nevertheless means its mandate could not provide a complete examination of those decisions.  
[Source: Commonwealth COVID-19 Response Inquiry](https://www.pmc.gov.au/covid-19-response-inquiry)

### 2. Compulsory powers are not merely a punishment for obstruction
The critic asks for proof that crucial evidence has been withheld. That sets the threshold too narrowly: outsiders may not know which relevant records exist until an investigation obtains them.

The purpose of compulsory powers is to make the evidence-gathering process less dependent on the cooperation of institutions whose conduct is under examination. That does **not** imply concealment or wrongdoing.

A parliamentary inquiry or voluntary review may answer many questions. But a jointly empowered Royal Commission offers a stronger framework for testing conflicting accounts across governments. Its powers have legal limits and protections.  
[Source: Royal Commissions Act 1902](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1902A00012/latest/text)

I agree that documents cannot establish precisely what an alternative policy would have achieved. They can establish **what decision-makers considered, what they overlooked and whether their stated reasoning matches the record**.

### 3. Broad jurisdiction need not mean unlimited subject matter
Covering nine governments does not require investigating every action by each. The commission could examine selected high-consequence decisions and recurring coordination failures, incorporating earlier findings rather than rehearing everything.

Cooperation is a genuine practical obstacle. It is also something governments should be asked to provide—not a reason to leave shared accountability fragmented.

### 4. Reform and investigation should proceed together
Existing recommendations should be implemented now. A commission need not delay them.

The critic’s proposed audit is useful, but it can be the commission’s opening stage rather than another preliminary process followed by further investigations.

**The choice is not scrutiny versus preparedness. A focused Royal Commission would connect the two: establish a credible cross-government record, then identify safeguards grounded in how decisions were actually made.**

## [4] CRITIC

**The advocate has narrowed the proposal, but still has not shown why a Royal Commission should come before establishing what remains unresolved.** The strongest argument—shared responsibility needs shared scrutiny—supports a cross-government investigation, not necessarily this particular form of investigation.

### 1. Shared responsibility was not wholly excluded from the existing inquiry
The Commonwealth inquiry’s terms covered Commonwealth responsibilities **including those shared with states and territories**, while excluding unilateral state and territory actions.  
[Source: COVID-19 Response Inquiry](https://www.pmc.gov.au/covid-19-response-inquiry)

That distinction matters. The advocate shifts from a demonstrated gap—unilateral state decisions—to a broader claim that coordination and shared accountability lack adequate scrutiny. The exclusion alone does not establish that broader claim.

There may be important unanswered questions. But the case needs examples of unresolved conflicts in responsibility or decision-making, checked against existing findings—not simply a list of questions a commission could investigate.

### 2. Making the audit the commission’s first task puts the decision backwards
The proposed opening audit would determine which questions remain unanswered and what further investigation they require. **Those are precisely the findings needed to decide whether to establish a Royal Commission.**

Commissioning that assessment first is not necessarily an extra bureaucratic layer: both proposals involve an audit. The difference is whether governments commit to the larger institution before learning whether it is warranted.

The advocate’s approach assumes the audit will justify a commission. It might instead identify a few discrete gaps best addressed by specialist reviews or narrower investigations.

### 3. Unknown evidence is a reason to assess investigative needs—not an automatic case for maximum powers
I accept that outsiders cannot prove in advance which documents exist, and compulsory powers need not imply wrongdoing.

But that advantage applies to almost any review of government conduct. By itself, it supplies no limiting principle for choosing a Royal Commission.

The relevant questions are: **Which conflicting accounts need testing? What records are needed? What can alternative mechanisms obtain, and where would they fall short?** Proof of concealment is unnecessary; a concrete account of why lesser mechanisms are inadequate is still needed.

### 4. A joint commission does not itself solve fragmented accountability
Joint authorisation requires governments to agree on scope and powers. That is a condition the proposal depends on, not a benefit its creation guarantees.

Likewise, examining “selected high-consequence decisions” creates a selection problem: who chooses them, and on what criteria? Without an independent gap assessment first, the commission could revisit politically prominent disputes while overlooking less visible preparedness failures. That is a risk, not an established outcome—but it deserves an answer.

**Bottom line:** shared scrutiny is justified; a Royal Commission remains unproven. First publish an independent cross-government gap assessment, pursue existing reforms, and select investigative powers to match identified problems. The advocate has explained what a commission *could* do, not why Australia should establish one now.

## [5] ADVOCATE

**The critic’s strongest objection is about sequencing: establish what is missing before choosing the investigative machinery. I accept a short, independent scoping exercise before finalising the terms of reference. But that should shape a Royal Commission—not make proven obstruction a prerequisite for one.**

### 1. The justification is broader than coordination failures
The critic correctly notes that the Commonwealth inquiry covered shared responsibilities. I should not have treated its exclusion of unilateral state decisions as proof that coordination went unexamined.

The remaining concern is **consistent scrutiny of governments’ use of extraordinary powers**. The inquiry expressly excluded unilateral state and territory actions. That is a documented boundary, although it does not prove those actions escaped every other review.  
[Source: COVID-19 Response Inquiry—terms of reference](https://www.pmc.gov.au/covid-19-response-inquiry)

Consider a concrete question: **when governments maintained different border or school restrictions, did their review processes adequately account for changing evidence, local risks and foreseeable harms?** Different outcomes would not establish wrongdoing. The value of examining them together is to distinguish justified differences from weaknesses in decision-making.

That is a proposed investigative question, not a claim that existing reports contain no answers.

### 2. There is a limiting principle for choosing a Royal Commission
The critic reasonably asks why this logic would not justify a commission into almost anything. My answer is the combination of:

- exceptional restrictions on ordinary freedoms;
- consequences across health, education and livelihoods;
- decisions distributed across nine governments;
- the need to examine decision-makers’ own conduct, not merely recommend future policy.

**My judgment is that this combination warrants an independent public accounting with compulsory evidence powers available from the outset.** Those powers are insurance for a credible investigation, not an accusation of concealment.  
[Source: Royal Commissions Act 1902](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1902A00012/latest/text)

The critic’s alternative might obtain the necessary evidence. But that possibility does not establish that separate, narrower investigations would adequately test a connected national experience.

### 3. Selection should be transparent—not politically convenient
The scoping exercise should publish an inventory of existing findings and recommend subjects using explicit criteria: seriousness of consequences, unresolved factual questions, cross-jurisdictional relevance and usefulness for future emergencies. Public submissions should help identify less visible harms.

Those criteria should shape a jointly authorised commission’s mandate. Settled matters should not be reheard without a clear reason.

### 4. The disagreement is ultimately about the standard of accountability
A Royal Commission cannot guarantee reform, settle every scientific dispute or eliminate uncertainty. Neither can the critic’s alternatives.

**The affirmative case is not that lesser reviews are worthless. It is that decisions of this exceptional reach deserve a common, independent evidentiary process capable of testing governments’ accounts—not merely collecting further lessons.** Scope it carefully, continue existing reforms, and establish the commission.

## [6] CRITIC

**The advocate now offers a reasonable way to scope an investigation—but still commits to a Royal Commission regardless of what that exercise finds.** The importance of pandemic decisions justifies demanding accountability. It does not, by itself, establish which institution should provide it.

### 1. Exceptional powers justify scrutiny—not automatically a Royal Commission
The advocate’s strongest argument is that restrictions affected freedoms, health, education and livelihoods across nine governments. That is a compelling reason for serious, independent scrutiny.

But **the seriousness of the decisions and the adequacy of existing scrutiny are different questions**. Even exceptionally consequential decisions do not require a new Royal Commission if their central failures are already documented and the remaining gaps can be investigated more directly.

The advocate acknowledges that the Commonwealth inquiry’s exclusion of unilateral state actions does not establish that those actions escaped other reviews. Yet the argument still moves from that exclusion to a preferred investigative institution without demonstrating the intervening need.

### 2. A common standard does not require a single commission
The proposed comparison of border and school restrictions is worthwhile. But it requires more than assembling governments’ accounts:

- What evidence was available at each decision date?
- How did local risks and available alternatives differ?
- What harms were anticipated, measured or overlooked?
- What criteria would distinguish a reasonable decision from an unreasonable one?

**Compulsory testimony can clarify the record; it cannot supply the evaluative standard.** Experts and the public may still reasonably disagree about how governments should have balanced competing harms.

An independent comparative review could apply a common framework across jurisdictions, publish the evidence and identify specific factual disputes requiring stronger investigation. The advocate assumes a unified Royal Commission would produce better accountability than that approach, but has not established why.

### 3. “Insurance” must be weighed against its costs
Having compulsory powers available from the outset offers a genuine benefit. But insurance is not automatically worth purchasing at any price.

**My concern is a practical inference, not a quantified finding:** preparing evidence and participating in formal proceedings would draw on some of the same public-health and government expertise needed to implement reforms. Saying reform and investigation should proceed together does not remove that competition.

The relevant comparison is therefore not “Royal Commission versus inadequate scrutiny.” It is whether the commission’s **additional evidentiary value** outweighs its additional demands compared with a focused alternative. That case remains missing.

### 4. Scoping must be allowed to change the answer
The advocate’s proposed criteria—unresolved facts, serious consequences and usefulness for future emergencies—are sensible. But if the assessment can only shape a commission, never recommend against one, the institutional choice is predetermined.

**Bottom line:** commission the independent gap assessment, publish its findings and continue reforms. Choose further investigations based on what it discovers. Australians deserve an effective accounting—not a commitment to the most powerful investigative format before its added value has been demonstrated.

## [7] ADVOCATE

**The critic is right that scoping must be capable of changing the recommendation. I would refine my position accordingly: establish a jointly authorised Royal Commission, subject to a short preliminary assessment confirming that substantial accountability gaps remain.** That is a recommendation based on the present case—not a commitment regardless of evidence.

### 1. The assessment should test the adequacy of scrutiny, not merely count reports
The key question is not whether border closures, school restrictions or quarantine have appeared in previous investigations. It is whether those investigations adequately established:

- what advice decision-makers received;
- why major measures were retained or changed;
- how foreseeable harms and less restrictive alternatives were considered;
- whether governments’ public explanations matched their internal reasoning.

The Commonwealth inquiry explicitly excluded unilateral state and territory actions. That establishes a significant limitation in the national inquiry, although **not proof that other investigations failed to fill it**.  
[Source: COVID-19 Response Inquiry](https://www.pmc.gov.au/covid-19-response-inquiry)

If the preliminary assessment finds that existing investigations collectively provide that accounting, the case for another commission would weaken substantially. But simply identifying overlapping reports would not answer the concern.

### 2. The additional value is testing explanations—not imposing one “correct” policy
The critic correctly says compulsory testimony cannot decide how society should balance competing harms. **But disagreement about values does not make the decision-making process untestable.**

For example, there is a meaningful difference between a government documenting educational harms and explaining why it nevertheless retained school restrictions, and asserting that it considered those harms without a supporting record. A commission could investigate that difference without claiming to know the perfect reopening date.

A comparative expert review can evaluate published evidence. A Royal Commission can also compel relevant records and testimony, subject to legal protections, to test the account governments present. That is a concrete additional function—not merely greater prestige.  
[Source: Royal Commissions Act 1902](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1902A00012/latest/text)

### 3. The alternative also has costs
The critic reasonably identifies staff time and distraction as risks. But an assessment followed by several specialist reviews and possible escalation also consumes resources and may require repeated evidence gathering.

**Neither approach has been shown here to be cheaper or faster.** That uncertainty supports a comparative assessment of costs and scope—not a presumption that a commission is excessive. Hearings should concentrate on material factual disputes, with existing evidence reused wherever possible.

**My position remains affirmative:** Australia should pursue a focused Royal Commission because a national accounting should be able to test governments’ explanations, including state and territory decisions excluded from the Commonwealth inquiry. Let preliminary scoping genuinely test that need—but require evidence of adequate existing accountability, not merely the possibility that another lighter review might suffice.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

**The Australian government should establish a Royal Commission to investigate federal, state and territory governments’ management of the COVID-19 pandemic.**

The Advocate ultimately qualified this proposal: a focused, jointly authorised commission should proceed **subject to a short preliminary assessment confirming substantial accountability gaps remain**.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Extraordinary decisions warrant independent accountability.** Pandemic measures affected freedoms, health, education and livelihoods across nine governments. Scrutiny should examine both successes and failures, judging decisions against what was reasonably known at the time.
- **The Commonwealth inquiry had an important boundary.** It excluded unilateral state and territory actions. The Advocate argued for assessing whether existing investigations collectively provide an adequate account of those decisions, while acknowledging that this exclusion does not prove they escaped other scrutiny.
- **Compulsory powers can test governments’ explanations.** A Royal Commission could obtain records and testimony to establish what advice governments received, how they considered harms and alternatives, and whether public explanations matched internal reasoning. These powers need not imply concealment or wrongdoing.
- **Cross-government coverage can coexist with a focused mandate.** The commission could investigate selected high-consequence decisions rather than repeat every pandemic review. Published selection criteria, public submissions, reuse of existing evidence, legal and privacy safeguards, and a fixed timetable could guide its work.
- **Investigation should accompany reform.** Existing recommendations should be implemented without waiting for a commission. The Advocate also noted that multiple narrower investigations could consume resources and duplicate evidence gathering.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **The need for scrutiny does not establish the need for a Royal Commission.** The first task should be to identify significant unanswered questions across existing investigations and determine which mechanism can best resolve them.
- **The Commonwealth inquiry’s exclusion must not be overstated.** Its remit included Commonwealth responsibilities shared with states and territories. Excluding unilateral state actions therefore did not establish that coordination or shared accountability went unexamined.
- **Compulsory powers require a concrete justification.** Proof of concealment is unnecessary, but the case should identify relevant factual disputes, needed records and the limitations of alternative mechanisms. Such powers cannot resolve all counterfactual or value-based disagreements.
- **Breadth, timeliness and limited duplication involve trade-offs.** Joint authorisation requires government cooperation, while selecting only certain decisions risks overlooking less visible failures unless guided by an independent assessment.
- **A commission’s additional value must justify its demands.** Formal proceedings could draw expertise away from implementing reforms. Public responses to recommendations do not guarantee action.
- **The assessment must genuinely determine the institutional choice.** The Critic preferred an independent cross-government gap assessment, continued reform and investigations tailored to its findings, with stronger powers where justified.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Existing inquiry coverage:** The Critic challenged the Advocate’s suggestion that the exclusion of unilateral state decisions demonstrated inadequate scrutiny of shared responsibility. The Advocate accepted the correction and refocused on consistent scrutiny of extraordinary powers.
- **Need for compulsory powers:** The Advocate argued that outsiders may not know what records exist and that compulsory powers reduce dependence on voluntary cooperation. The Critic accepted this benefit but maintained that it does not, by itself, distinguish this case from other government reviews.
- **What evidence can establish:** Both accepted that documents and testimony cannot identify a single ideal pandemic policy. The Advocate stressed that they can nevertheless test whether governments’ stated decision-making processes are supported by evidence.
- **Sequencing:** The Advocate initially proposed making an audit the commission’s first task, then accepted preliminary scoping. After further criticism, the Advocate agreed that this assessment must be capable of changing the recommendation.
- **Costs and alternatives:** The Critic raised resource competition and duplication risks. The Advocate responded that narrower reviews and later escalation also have costs, and that neither approach had been shown in the debate to be cheaper or faster.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides agreed that:

- Serious, independent scrutiny and accountability are warranted.
- The Commonwealth inquiry excluded unilateral state and territory actions, but that exclusion alone does not prove an overall absence of scrutiny.
- Existing findings should be mapped and unnecessary repetition avoided.
- Compulsory powers can help establish the record without implying wrongdoing.
- Decisions should be evaluated in their contemporary evidentiary and local context, including competing harms.
- No investigation can eliminate every scientific, counterfactual or value-based uncertainty.
- Existing reforms should continue, and an inquiry cannot guarantee implementation.
- By the end, preliminary assessment should be capable of affecting whether a Royal Commission proceeds.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **Whether a Royal Commission is presently the justified preferred option:** The Advocate favoured pursuing one conditionally; the Critic considered its necessity unproven.
- **The threshold for choosing it:** The Advocate emphasised demonstrating adequate existing accountability. The Critic emphasised identifying concrete gaps and showing why less demanding mechanisms would be insufficient.
- **Its comparative advantage:** They disagreed over whether a unified process with compulsory powers would add enough value beyond comparative reviews and targeted investigations.
- **Practical trade-offs:** The debate did not establish which approach would better balance scope, cost, speed, cooperation and demands on reform capacity.

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate centred on **the form and sequencing of accountability**, rather than whether pandemic management deserves scrutiny. The Advocate argued that decisions of exceptional reach warrant a common evidentiary process capable of testing governments’ accounts. The Critic argued that the investigative institution should be chosen only after unresolved questions and the limitations of alternatives are established.

The positions moved closer as the Advocate accepted corrections about the existing inquiry and made the proposal conditional on a meaningful preliminary assessment. The remaining disagreement concerned whether a Royal Commission should be the preferred conditional course or simply one possible outcome of that assessment.
