## Claim

The Australian government should require photo identification for Medicare cards and increase Medicare rebates for GP consultations.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/rorts

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

**Australia should adopt both reforms: make Medicare cards harder to misuse and make GP visits more affordable.** They address different problems, but serve the same goal—a public health system people can trust and actually access.

### 1. Photo Medicare cards would provide a practical identity check
A Medicare number identifies an account; it does not, by itself, establish that the person presenting it is the cardholder. Adding a photograph would give reception staff a straightforward way to detect someone using another person’s details.

That is a **limited but worthwhile safeguard**, not a cure for Medicare fraud. It would not stop a provider billing for services never delivered. Those problems still require audits and enforcement.

Implementation should be proportionate:
- Provide photo cards free, with accessible enrolment options.
- Phase them in through renewals.
- Allow sensible exceptions for children and people whose identity cannot readily be checked.
- Never make a missing card a reason to refuse urgent care.

A photograph on a card need not mean facial-recognition technology or a central biometric surveillance system.

### 2. Higher GP rebates would strengthen affordable primary care
Medicare rebates are the government’s contribution towards treatment. With bulk billing, the practitioner accepts that benefit as full payment. ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/bulk-billing))

Higher rebates therefore give practices more room to bulk bill—or reduce patients’ out-of-pocket charges—while covering the cost of providing care. The ABS’s *Patient Experiences* survey documents people delaying or going without GP care because of cost: affordability is a real barrier, not merely an inconvenience. ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/health/health-services/patient-experiences/latest-release))

A blanket increase would not guarantee lower fees. The strongest policy would prioritise **bulk-billed appointments and longer consultations for complex needs**, then track whether patient costs and access improve.

### The case for doing both
Photo cards should proceed only with proportionate costs and safeguards; their savings should not be assumed to fund higher rebates. The independent case remains strong: **verify entitlement more reliably, and invest more adequately in the everyday care Medicare exists to provide.**

## [2] CRITIC

**The advocate identifies real concerns, but has not shown that these two reforms are the best way to address them.** Making care affordable is a stronger case than requiring photo Medicare cards; neither follows automatically from the arguments presented.

### 1. A possible identity check is not yet a case for a national requirement
A photograph could help detect someone presenting another person’s card. But the advocate provides no evidence establishing:
- how much Medicare loses specifically through this kind of impersonation;
- how often staff would detect it using photographs;
- whether the benefit would outweigh issuing cards, updating photographs and resolving identity disputes.

That matters because, as the advocate acknowledges, photographs would not prevent providers billing for care they never delivered. **An anti-fraud measure should target demonstrated losses, not merely a conceivable loophole.**

Free cards would remove the purchase price—not the time and difficulty of obtaining a photograph or correcting a mismatch. Exceptions would help vulnerable patients, but require additional rules and staff judgment. Protecting urgent care also leaves unanswered whether someone could face delays accessing routine care.

The better starting point is to measure impersonation and test targeted checks before imposing a universal requirement.

### 2. Higher rebates do not necessarily buy better access
The advocate’s strongest argument is that cost prevents some people seeing a GP. The [ABS patient-experience survey](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/health/health-services/patient-experiences/latest-release) supports that concern. **It does not establish which funding reform would reduce that barrier most effectively.**

Targeting bulk-billed consultations is more convincing than an unrestricted increase: under [bulk billing](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/bulk-billing), the practitioner accepts the Medicare benefit as full payment. Nevertheless:
- Extra funding also goes to consultations that would already have been bulk billed.
- Higher payment per visit does not itself create additional appointments.
- Funding longer consultations may support complex care, but does not automatically increase appointment availability.

These are limitations of the proposal—not proof that rebates never help. Before committing substantial funding, government should compare the additional affordable care purchased with alternatives such as directly funded clinics and workforce investment in underserved areas.

### Bottom line
The advocate offers plausible mechanisms, not a demonstrated case for these requirements. Australia should prioritise **measurable improvements in access and evidence-led fraud prevention**, rather than assume that mandatory photo cards and higher consultation payments are the right combination.

## [3] ADVOCATE

**The critic’s strongest objection is fair: a useful identity check is not automatically a cost-effective national policy.** That calls for a tested rollout—not dismissal of photo identification—and a more carefully targeted rebate increase.

### 1. Require photo identification, but test the delivery model before national rollout
I have not established the scale of Medicare losses from patient impersonation, and I would not claim that photo cards would pay for themselves.

The narrower case is that **entitlement details and proof of identity do different jobs**. A photograph adds a check that possession of a Medicare number alone cannot provide. There is also a plausible patient-safety benefit: reducing the risk of treatment being attributed to the wrong person. That is an inference, not a measured benefit established here.

The government should adopt a staged requirement, testing:
- impersonation detected and administrative cost;
- mistaken identity challenges;
- delays or abandoned appointments, including routine care.

Patients unable to present their photo card should have an alternative verification route, rather than automatically losing access to subsidised care. National expansion should depend on satisfactory results.

This refines the proposal without abandoning it: **photo identification should become the normal requirement, with evidence determining how it is implemented.** The critic is right to demand evaluation, but has not established that targeted checks would provide comparable protection with less burden.

### 2. Paying more for existing bulk-billed care is not necessarily wasted funding
The critic argues that higher rebates would subsidise appointments already being bulk billed. But that assumes those appointments would remain available on the same terms without additional support. **Maintaining affordable care is a legitimate outcome, not just creating new appointments.** Whether particular increases are necessary still requires evidence.

Targeting increases to bulk-billed GP consultations also gives a clear patient-price safeguard: the practitioner accepts the Medicare benefit as full payment. ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/bulk-billing))

The policy should therefore be judged on both:
- whether it preserves and expands no-gap appointments;
- whether patients can obtain those appointments promptly.

### 3. Workforce reform is a complement, not a rebuttal
Higher rebates will not instantly create more GPs. But additional clinicians alone will not guarantee affordable fees either. Directly funded clinics and workforce investment deserve comparison—and may be needed alongside rebate increases.

**The defensible package is stronger identity assurance introduced with safeguards, and higher GP rebates tied closely to affordable care.** Neither needs to solve every Medicare problem to deserve a place in reform.

## [4] CRITIC

**The advocate’s refinements make the proposal more cautious, but they still do not establish that Australia should adopt both reforms.** Testing a policy is different from deciding it should become the national default.

### 1. Evidence should determine *whether* photo identification is required—not just how
The advocate proposes national expansion only if trials succeed, yet also says photo identification “should become the normal requirement.” Those positions pull in different directions: a genuine trial must allow the conclusion that **no general requirement is justified**.

The advocate also asks the critic to establish that targeted checks offer comparable protection with less burden. That reverses the responsibility. Before imposing a new requirement on everyone, its proponents should demonstrate an advantage over less restrictive alternatives.

There is a further unresolved trade-off:
- If alternative verification remains straightforward, someone seeking to impersonate a patient could potentially use that route.
- If alternatives become demanding, legitimate patients without usable photo identification face greater obstacles.

That is a design risk, not evidence that every system would fail. But it means that safeguards cannot simply be listed as though they eliminate the central problem.

The suggested patient-safety benefit also remains hypothetical. Establishing who presented a card does not, by itself, ensure that clinical information is recorded against the correct patient. A trial would need to measure actual errors prevented, not merely identity checks completed.

### 2. Preserving bulk billing is valuable—but does not establish the necessary subsidy
**The advocate’s strongest point is that maintaining existing affordable appointments can justify spending.** Agreed. But my earlier objection did not assume those appointments would survive unchanged; it questioned how many actually require additional funding.

That distinction matters. A rebate increase might preserve financially precarious services, or pay more for services that would continue anyway. Both possibilities need assessment.

[Bulk billing](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/bulk-billing) guarantees no patient gap for that consultation. It does **not** establish that the additional subsidy changes a practice’s behaviour. The relevant measure is affordable care preserved or added **because of the increase**, not the total number of subsidised visits afterward.

### 3. Complementary policies still compete for funding
Workforce investment and rebates can work together. But calling them complements does not answer how limited funding should be divided. Government should compare their additional benefits before committing to a general rebate increase.

**Bottom line:** The revised argument supports investigation and evaluation—not a presumption in favour of mandatory photo identification and higher rebates. Neither a plausible identity safeguard nor a worthwhile funding objective is enough to justify this particular package.

## [5] ADVOCATE

**The critic is right that proponents must justify a national requirement. But uncertainty supports a reversible, proportionate reform—not necessarily leaving the present arrangements unchanged.** My case is stronger for higher rebates; the photo-ID proposal warrants tighter limits.

### 1. Require a photo credential—not a fresh identity hurdle at every appointment
The unresolved issue is whether identity assurance can improve without obstructing care. I would refine the requirement accordingly:

- Establish photo identification when issuing or renewing Medicare credentials, with exemptions where necessary.
- Let practices rely on an established patient relationship rather than demand the card at every visit.
- Provide assisted verification for patients without usable photo identification, without automatically withholding routine care.

This distinguishes **requiring a more reliable credential** from **requiring every patient to produce it at every consultation**.

The critic correctly identifies a risk in alternative verification, but presents an incomplete trade-off. An alternative need not be either an unchecked bypass or an unreasonable obstacle: it could involve staff-assisted confirmation through Medicare. Whether that works affordably must be tested.

I also accept that evaluation must determine **whether national expansion proceeds**, not merely its details. Supporting a staged requirement is not a promise to expand it despite poor results. My argument is that a photo provides an additional identity check worth introducing under controlled conditions—not that its net national benefit has already been demonstrated.

### 2. Rebate policy cannot sensibly require proof that every funded appointment would otherwise disappear
The critic’s proposed measure—affordable care preserved or added *because of* increased funding—is correct. But it does not follow that support reaching otherwise viable consultations is necessarily a reason against higher rebates.

A common payment schedule inevitably funds practices with different costs. Trying to identify and reimburse only appointments on the brink of becoming unaffordable would itself require administration and imperfect judgments. **The relevant comparison is total benefits and costs, including the cost of targeting—not whether targeting is perfect.**

Higher GP rebates should therefore concentrate on bulk-billed care and longer, clinically appropriate consultations. Bulk billing provides a concrete safeguard: the practitioner accepts the Medicare benefit as full payment. ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/bulk-billing)) Changes in availability, waiting times and patient costs should determine subsequent adjustments.

### 3. Comparison should improve the package, not become an indefinite veto
The [ABS patient-experience survey](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/health/health-services/patient-experiences/latest-release) establishes that cost obstructs GP access. It does not prove an optimal rebate level—but neither has the critic established that clinics or workforce spending would address affordability better.

**I support both reforms, with different safeguards:** a staged photo-identification requirement that must earn national expansion, and targeted higher GP rebates evaluated against affordable access preserved and created. That is a policy commitment with accountability, not an assumption that either reform works at any price.

## [6] CRITIC

**The advocate’s strongest point is that broad funding rules can be cheaper to administer than finely targeted assistance. But that supports comparing realistic options—not presuming that higher rebates and photo credentials are the right choices.**

### 1. The revised photo requirement separates the expense from the protection
A photograph can help detect impersonation **when someone checks it against the person presenting it**. Requiring a photo at enrolment or renewal does not, by itself, prevent someone else subsequently using that Medicare number.

The revised proposal therefore leaves a crucial question unanswered: **which consultations would involve a photo check?**

- Where an established patient relationship already provides identification, what additional protection does the photograph deliver?
- Where practices do not inspect it, how does it prevent misuse?
- Where they do inspect it, the consultation-level verification burden returns.

This is not an argument for checking everyone at every visit. It is an argument against requiring everyone to obtain a credential before establishing where it would actually help.

Staff-assisted verification might be workable, but “could involve confirmation through Medicare” is not yet an operational solution. Its staffing needs, reliability and effect on reception workloads remain unestablished.

Nor is “reversible” equivalent to low-risk: a requirement can be withdrawn, but money spent introducing it and time patients spend complying cannot be recovered. A limited comparative trial is defensible; a presumption that photo credentials should become mandatory is not.

### 2. The rebate defence answers a demand I did not make
I did not ask for proof that **every appointment** would otherwise disappear, or suggest assessing subsidies appointment by appointment. The relevant question is whether the proposed increase delivers enough additional or preserved affordable care **overall** to justify its cost.

The advocate rightly includes the administrative cost of targeting. But broad increases also have an opportunity cost: funding services that need no additional support leaves less money for unmet needs. Neither consideration automatically wins.

[Bulk billing](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/bulk-billing) guarantees no gap **for a consultation billed that way**. It does not guarantee that a practice will offer more such appointments, accept additional patients or shorten waits. Those are the outcomes the funding proposal must credibly address.

### 3. Evaluation needs a decision rule, not just monitoring
“Track outcomes and adjust later” is incomplete. Before spending, government should specify:
- the improvement it expects;
- the cost it considers acceptable;
- how it will distinguish policy effects from changes that would have happened anyway.

This does not demand certainty or indefinite delay. It demands a credible purchasing decision.

**The advocate has justified investigating identity checks and acting on affordability. They have not justified making photo credentials compulsory or selecting higher consultation rebates as the preferred use of additional funding.** Those policy choices still need evidence beyond plausible benefits and promises of later review.

## [7] ADVOCATE

**The critic’s strongest point is that a reform needs a clear operating model and a test of value—not just a useful intention.** Here is how I would make both proposals meet that standard.

### 1. Photo identification should be standard; checking it should be selective
The photograph would be checked **when a practice first treats an unfamiliar patient, or when there is a specific identity discrepancy**. An established patient would not routinely need to produce it again.

That answers where the protection occurs: at the point where staff cannot already identify the patient. Requiring the credential and inspecting it at every appointment are different policies.

Why favour a standard Medicare photo credential rather than leave checks entirely to individual practices? **A common credential could provide a consistent way to verify identity without requiring patients to hold a driving licence or passport.** That is a design argument, not evidence of measured savings. The credential should be free, with accessible enrolment and exceptions.

The critic is right that selective checking leaves some misuse undetected. But incomplete protection does not make standard provision pointless: its value depends on whether those checks prevent enough misuse to justify the cost. Equally, I accept that issuing credentials to people who rarely need checks is a genuine cost—not something safeguards eliminate.

My position remains **a staged photo-ID requirement, with national expansion conditional on demonstrated net benefit**. The unresolved evidence limits the speed and scope of implementation; it does not require abandoning the policy direction.

### 2. Higher rebates directly address the price of providing no-gap care
I accept the correction: the critic asked for overall value, not proof about every appointment.

The positive reason to choose rebates is their connection to consultations patients already seek. A higher payment for a bulk-billed GP consultation increases the practice’s revenue **without increasing that patient’s bill**. Under bulk billing, the practitioner accepts the Medicare benefit as full payment. ([Services Australia](https://www.servicesaustralia.gov.au/bulk-billing))

That does not guarantee additional appointments. It does, however, make affordable consultations better remunerated. Workforce measures address clinician availability; they do not themselves establish a no-gap payment arrangement. This is a reason to include higher rebates in the funding mix—not to give them the entire budget.

### 3. Set the decision rules before rollout
I support the critic’s demand for explicit evaluation:

- **Photo ID:** compare mandatory credentials with targeted existing-ID checks, counting implementation costs, detected misuse, mistaken challenges and delayed care.
- **Rebates:** use staged implementation and credible comparison groups to estimate affordable consultations preserved or added, patient costs and appointment availability.
- **Both:** publish acceptable cost and access thresholds beforehand, and stop expansion if they fail.

**The weakness in the critic’s conclusion is treating incomplete proof as sufficient reason to withhold policy preference.** We can reasonably favour these mechanisms while making expansion earn its funding. That is my case for both reforms—not a claim that their benefits are already conclusively established.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

**The Australian government should require photo identification for Medicare cards and increase Medicare rebates for GP consultations.**

The debate considers whether these reforms would improve identity assurance and affordable access to care, and whether their benefits justify their costs and burdens.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

### Photo identification
- A Medicare number identifies an account but does not establish that the person using it is the cardholder. A photograph could help detect impersonation.
- Photo cards would be a limited safeguard, not a solution to provider fraud such as billing for services never delivered.
- A standard Medicare photo credential could offer consistent identification without requiring patients to possess a driving licence or passport.
- The Advocate progressively narrowed the proposal to:
  - Free credentials, accessible enrolment, exemptions and assisted alternative verification.
  - Photo checks for unfamiliar patients or specific identity discrepancies, rather than at every consultation.
  - Protection against automatically withholding urgent or routine care when identification is unavailable.
  - Staged introduction, with national expansion conditional on demonstrated net benefit.
- A possible reduction in treatment being attributed to the wrong person was offered as an unmeasured, hypothetical safety benefit.

### Higher GP rebates
- Cost prevents some people from obtaining GP care, as documented by the cited ABS patient-experience survey.
- Higher rebates would improve remuneration for affordable consultations. For bulk-billed consultations, the practitioner accepts the Medicare benefit as full payment.
- Increases should concentrate on bulk-billed appointments and longer, clinically appropriate consultations rather than unrestricted payments.
- Preserving existing affordable appointments is valuable, even when funding does not create new appointments.
- Workforce investment and directly funded clinics may complement rebate increases. Rebates should form part of the funding mix, not consume the entire budget.
- Evaluation should consider overall benefits and costs, including the administrative expense of narrowly targeting support.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

### Photo identification
- The debate provides no evidence establishing the scale of patient impersonation, how effectively photographs would detect it, or whether benefits would exceed implementation costs.
- Free cards would not eliminate patients’ time costs, enrolment difficulties, mistaken identity challenges or potential delays in care.
- Alternative verification creates a design challenge: it must avoid becoming either an easy bypass for impersonation or an obstacle for legitimate patients.
- Issuing photo credentials only provides protection when photographs are actually checked. Universal issuance therefore needs justification even if checking is selective.
- Staff-assisted verification remains operationally unproven, and withdrawing a requirement would not recover implementation spending or patients’ time.
- Limited comparative trials are defensible, but evidence should determine whether any general requirement is warranted—not merely how it is introduced.

### Higher GP rebates
- Evidence of affordability problems does not establish that rebate increases are the most effective response.
- Additional funding may support services that would have remained available without it, leaving less funding for unmet needs.
- Bulk billing guarantees no gap for that consultation, but higher payments do not guarantee more appointments, additional patients or shorter waits.
- The relevant measure is affordable care preserved or added **because of** the funding increase, assessed overall.
- Rebates should be compared with directly funded clinics and workforce investment, including their opportunity costs.
- Evaluation needs predetermined expectations, acceptable costs and methods for distinguishing policy effects from changes that would have occurred anyway.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Evidence and the policy default:** The Advocate argued that uncertainty permits a controlled, conditional policy commitment. The Critic maintained that trials must genuinely allow rejection of a general photo-ID requirement and that proponents bear the burden of justifying it.
- **Where photo checks occur:** After the Critic questioned how credentials would prevent misuse without inspection, the Advocate specified checks for unfamiliar patients and identity discrepancies. The wider cost-effectiveness of requiring everyone to obtain the credential remained unresolved.
- **Safeguards and verification:** The Advocate proposed staff-assisted Medicare confirmation as an alternative to refusing care. The Critic argued that its reliability, workload and accessibility still required testing.
- **Funding existing bulk-billed care:** The Advocate argued that maintaining affordable services is a legitimate benefit. The Critic agreed, but distinguished services genuinely preserved by additional funding from those that would continue anyway.
- **Targeting subsidies:** The Advocate cautioned against demanding proof that every funded appointment needed support. The Critic clarified that the objection concerned overall value, not appointment-by-appointment assessment; the Advocate accepted that correction.
- **Evaluation:** The Advocate ultimately endorsed comparative testing, credible comparison groups, published cost and access thresholds, and stopping expansion if those thresholds were not met.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

- GP affordability is a genuine concern, and preserving affordable care is valuable.
- Photo identification would not prevent all Medicare fraud, particularly fraudulent provider billing.
- The national net benefit of mandatory photo credentials has not been established in the debate.
- Higher rebates do not automatically create clinicians, appointments or shorter waits.
- Bulk billing provides a no-gap guarantee for the consultation billed that way.
- Identification safeguards must account for legitimate patients who cannot readily provide photo ID.
- Policy choices should consider costs, administrative burdens, alternatives and measurable outcomes.
- Workforce measures and rebates can be complementary, while still competing for limited funding.
- Evaluation should use explicit decision criteria rather than rely solely on later monitoring.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **Photo-ID policy direction:** Whether the available reasoning justifies favouring a staged requirement, or only investigating identity checks without a presumption of compulsion.
- **Benefits relative to burdens:** Whether selective checking would prevent enough misuse to justify standard photo credentials, enrolment costs and verification difficulties.
- **Rebate funding priority:** Whether the connection between higher bulk-billing payments and affordable consultations is sufficient to favour rebate increases before comparative value is established.
- **Evidence needed before commitment:** How much evidence is necessary to support a policy preference, as distinct from authorising trials or national expansion.

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The Advocate supported both reforms but progressively qualified the package: selective use of photo identification, accessible alternatives, targeted rebate increases and expansion conditional on evaluation. The Critic accepted the underlying concerns and several proposed safeguards, while arguing that plausible mechanisms do not establish that these policies are preferable to alternatives.

The debate converged on the need for comparative evidence, explicit thresholds and protection of access to care. It remained divided over whether those conditions justify a provisional commitment to the proposed reforms or only further investigation before choosing them.
