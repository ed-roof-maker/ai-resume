# A Multi Agent AI Policy Debate
AI can make mistakes. Always critically evaluate the content of the debate.

## Claim

Australia should withdraw from the United Nations Paris Agreement on climate change.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/climate

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

## Opening argument: Australia should leave Paris—not abandon climate action

Australia should withdraw from the Paris Agreement and replace its treaty commitments with an accountable national climate policy. The case is not that climate change is imaginary, but that **an international commitment should earn its place by delivering benefits worth its constraints.**

### 1. Australia should control the pace of its transition

The Paris Agreement requires countries to submit successive climate plans, with each new plan representing a “progression” beyond the previous one and reflecting its “highest possible ambition.” It does **not** impose an enforceable emissions quota on Australia—but it does establish an expectation of continually increasing commitments. [Source: Paris Agreement, Article 4](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)

**My argument:** Australia’s elected government should be able to revise the pace of emissions reduction in either direction when electricity reliability, household affordability or industrial competitiveness warrants it—not have to defend that decision against a treaty’s upward-only expectation.

Withdrawal would not repeal Australian climate laws. Any domestic changes would still need to go through Australia’s democratic processes.

### 2. Paris does not guarantee a fair competitive bargain

Countries set their own contributions. Paris’s compliance mechanism is explicitly “non-punitive”; it cannot guarantee that competitors will undertake comparable emissions cuts or bear comparable costs. [Source: Articles 4 and 15](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)

That creates a legitimate concern for emissions-intensive Australian industries: tighter domestic requirements can encourage production to move elsewhere rather than eliminate the underlying emissions.

This is a **risk, not an inevitable outcome**. But Australia should judge commitments against actual international performance—not assume that everyone submitting a climate plan means everyone is making an equivalent effort.

### 3. Climate cooperation does not require Paris membership

Australia could still fund clean-energy research, improve efficiency, reduce methane, strengthen disaster resilience and negotiate practical international partnerships.

The strongest objection is that withdrawal would weaken diplomatic trust and collective action. That cost is real. However, cooperation should be judged by results, not membership alone. A credible, publicly audited national programme could demonstrate continuing responsibility without an open-ended treaty commitment.

**Bottom line:** Withdrawal is defensible if it replaces Paris’s escalating commitment framework with transparent Australian decisions—not with inaction. Australia should pursue emissions reductions that are durable, affordable and effective, while retaining democratic control over their timing and scale.

## [2] CRITIC

## Australia should stay: withdrawal offers no demonstrated advantage

The advocate’s strongest point is that Australia should not sacrifice affordability or reliability merely to satisfy international expectations. But that does **not establish that leaving Paris would improve either**.

### 1. Paris constrains ambition—not every decision about how to deliver it

The upward expectation is real: successive national climate commitments are supposed to become stronger. That is a constraint, and it should not be dismissed.

However, **Australia sets its own contribution**. Paris does not prescribe Australian electricity prices, mandate particular power-station closures, or dictate a technology mix. It also expressly recognises that developing countries’ emissions will take longer to peak and that national circumstances differ. [Paris Agreement, Article 4](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)

Australia can therefore change policies to address reliability and household costs while remaining a member.

**The unsupported assumption:** that withdrawal is necessary to achieve an affordable transition. The advocate identifies no specific reliability problem that requires abandoning the treaty rather than improving domestic policy. And, as the advocate acknowledges, withdrawal would not itself repeal Australian climate laws.

### 2. Unequal international effort makes accountability more valuable

The advocate correctly says Paris cannot guarantee equal effort or punish every failure. But the relevant comparison is **Paris versus the proposed alternative**, not Paris versus a perfect agreement.

Paris establishes common reporting and review arrangements, plus a collective assessment of progress every five years. These do not guarantee compliance, but they help countries examine what others are actually doing. [Articles 13–15](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)

Leaving would not make competitors cut emissions faster or expose them to stronger penalties. A nationally audited Australian programme could improve accountability **within Australia**, but it cannot replace reciprocal international scrutiny.

The risk of industry relocating deserves targeted attention. It is not, without evidence about particular industries and alternatives, a sufficient argument for treaty withdrawal.

### 3. “We can cooperate outside Paris” does not explain why we should

Research funding, methane reductions, efficiency measures and bilateral partnerships are all compatible with Paris membership. Withdrawal is unnecessary to pursue any of them.

The advocate also concedes a diplomatic cost, while offering only a conditional benefit: withdrawal *could* be accompanied by a credible replacement programme. No replacement has been specified or shown to deliver better outcomes.

**My judgment:** Australia gains more by retaining the common framework while improving its domestic policies. Greater freedom to weaken commitments is not automatically greater capacity to solve energy or economic problems.

**Bottom line:** The case presented supports flexible, accountable climate policy—not withdrawal. Before abandoning an imperfect cooperation framework, Australia should demand evidence that doing so solves a problem that cannot be better addressed from within it.

## [3] ADVOCATE

## The case for withdrawal is policy flexibility—not a promise of cheaper electricity overnight

The critic’s strongest objection is correct: **leaving Paris would not itself lower electricity prices, fix reliability or repeal Australian climate laws.** My case is narrower: Australia should remove an international commitment to continually increasing ambition and replace it with commitments that can be revised transparently when circumstances change.

### 1. Changing the means is not always enough

The critic says Australia can change its technology mix and implementation policies while remaining in Paris. Agreed. But that does not resolve what happens when **the target or timetable itself needs reconsideration**, rather than merely the policies used to pursue it.

Paris Article 4.3 says successive national contributions will represent a “progression”; Article 4.11 provides for adjusting an existing contribution “with a view to enhancing its level of ambition.” That framework favours stronger commitments, not a symmetrical process for strengthening or relaxing them. [Treaty text](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)

**My inference:** retaining the ability to revise a timetable downward has value when technology, infrastructure delivery or international effort falls short of expectations. That is not evidence that Australia must weaken its targets today. It is an argument against making increasingly ambitious targets the standing international obligation.

The critic’s test—show a problem that *cannot* be solved inside Paris—is too demanding. Withdrawal need not be the only conceivable solution; it needs to offer a better balance of flexibility, accountability and cooperation.

### 2. Here is a more concrete replacement

Australia should announce a replacement framework alongside withdrawal:

- **Parliament-approved emissions budgets**, with regular public reviews.
- **Independent assessments** of emissions, electricity reliability, household costs and industrial competitiveness.
- **Explicit grounds for revising the timetable**, backed by published evidence—not simply ministerial preference.
- **Continued public emissions reporting**, using internationally comparable methods.
- **Negotiated sector agreements** that tie additional Australian commitments to measurable action by participating countries.

This would not guarantee better outcomes. Its proposed advantage is specific: **both acceleration and delay would be legitimate decisions requiring public justification**, rather than only acceleration fitting the treaty’s ambition framework.

### 3. International scrutiny is valuable—but does not settle membership

The critic is right that national auditing cannot replace reciprocal international review. Withdrawal would sacrifice participation in Paris’s common accountability framework, and that is a genuine cost.

But the critic moves too quickly from “reporting is useful” to “membership is therefore preferable.” Reporting can reveal unequal effort; it cannot compel comparable effort. Paris expressly makes its compliance mechanism non-punitive. [Article 15](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)

**The unresolved question is whether that scrutiny justifies the ambition constraint.** My judgment is that Australia should prioritise transparent domestic accountability and more explicitly reciprocal agreements.

**Bottom line:** withdrawal should not be sold as an immediate economic windfall. It is a governance choice: Australia should cooperate internationally while retaining the ability to reconsider both its climate policies **and their timetable**, under public scrutiny and parliamentary control.

## [4] CRITIC

## Flexibility has value—but so does a credible commitment

The advocate has clarified the real trade-off: leaving Paris would give Australia greater freedom to weaken its emissions timetable, while sacrificing a shared international framework. **That freedom has some value. But the argument still does not establish that it is worth the exchange.**

### 1. The option to delay is not cost-free

The strongest argument for withdrawal is that infrastructure or technology might fall behind, making an existing timetable unusually expensive. That is a legitimate concern.

But the proposed replacement judges delay mainly against reliability, household costs and competitiveness. It must also count the consequences of additional emissions. The IPCC finds that limiting warming requires limiting **cumulative** carbon emissions—not simply reaching a particular annual emissions level eventually. Delaying reductions can therefore add warming unless those additional emissions are subsequently compensated for. [IPCC, Summary for Policymakers, section B.1](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/)

**The unsupported assumption is that easier downward revision improves the overall balance.** It may reduce near-term adjustment costs while increasing climate damage or leaving a harder transition for later governments. Publicly explaining that decision does not eliminate those costs.

### 2. A commitment’s constraint can be its benefit

The advocate treats the upward ambition framework principally as a loss of flexibility. But international cooperation needs some confidence that commitments will survive inconvenient circumstances.

Consider the replacement’s proposed sector agreements:

- If commitments can be relaxed whenever domestic conditions become difficult, why should partners rely on them?
- If those agreements meaningfully restrict such revisions, Australia has accepted international constraints again.

This does not make sector agreements useless. It shows that **withdrawal cannot remove the tension between flexibility and credible cooperation**. It merely moves that tension into negotiations whose participants, coverage and terms remain unspecified.

My inference is that weakening a shared commitment framework could also encourage others to seek equivalent freedom. That outcome is not certain—but it belongs in the comparison alongside the hoped-for benefits.

### 3. The replacement’s strongest features do not require withdrawal

Parliamentary budgets, independent cost assessments and transparent reviews could improve policy while Australia remains in Paris. The treaty also allows cooperation between countries in implementing their commitments. [Paris Agreement, Article 6](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)

The distinctive benefit of withdrawal is therefore much narrower than the replacement package suggests: greater latitude to lower ambition. The advocate has not supplied negotiated reciprocal agreements or a concrete assessment showing that this latitude outweighs lost international accountability and diplomatic trust.

**Bottom line:** I accept that withdrawal need not be the *only* solution to merit consideration. It must, however, be demonstrably better than staying. The advocate has shown that commitments involve trade-offs—not that Australia would benefit from abandoning this one.

## [5] ADVOCATE

## The strongest case for leaving is selective commitment—not freedom from all commitments

The critic is right that **delaying reductions can increase cumulative emissions and climate damage**. My replacement framework therefore needs an explicit safeguard: any proposed relaxation must publish its expected additional emissions, assess the resulting climate costs, and explain whether later reductions would compensate. Affordability alone should not decide the issue.

But that strengthens the case for an honest review process—not necessarily for Paris’s rule that successive commitments move only toward greater ambition.

### 1. A valuable commitment must be worth its particular constraints

The critic argues that constraints build trust. Agreed. But **“commitments are useful” does not establish that this commitment offers the best bargain**.

Paris combines an expectation of progressively stronger national pledges with no guarantee of comparable effort by trading partners. That is not meaningless cooperation, but neither is it a reciprocal bargain in which Australia’s additional obligations secure specified action from others.

The critic’s challenge to sector agreements presents a false choice: either Australia can revise everything freely, or it has recreated Paris. Australia could instead accept **narrower commitments with defined duration, measurable reciprocal obligations and agreed review clauses**.

Such agreements would still constrain Australia. The proposed advantage is not the absence of constraints; it is a clearer connection between what Australia promises and what partners promise in return. Their successful negotiation remains uncertain—not an established benefit.

### 2. Withdrawal would not end all international reporting obligations

There is an important distinction between leaving Paris and leaving the underlying **UN Framework Convention on Climate Change**.

The Convention separately provides for national emissions inventories and reporting under Article 12. Australia could leave Paris while remaining a Convention party. Paris Article 28 makes withdrawal from the Convention trigger withdrawal from Paris—not the reverse. [Convention text](https://unfccc.int/resource/docs/convkp/conveng.pdf) · [Paris Agreement](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)

That would **not preserve Paris’s enhanced transparency framework**, and I accept that loss. But the comparison is not international accountability versus purely domestic self-policing. Some shared reporting obligations would remain.

### 3. The relevant choice is which decisions need international restriction

The critic correctly notes that better parliamentary scrutiny is possible inside Paris. What is not equally accommodated is a transparent decision that a particular timetable should become less demanding.

That option carries climate risks. Locking in a timetable carries other risks when its underlying assumptions fail. Neither risk disappears because a commitment is described as “credible.”

**My judgment:** Australia should withdraw through the treaty’s lawful process, alongside a legislated replacement that assesses cumulative emissions as well as economic and energy risks. I cannot claim a demonstrated net economic gain. The affirmative case is instead that Australia should retain reversible national targets while seeking more specific, reciprocal international commitments.

The critic establishes why cooperation matters. It does not establish why cooperation must include Paris’s particular upward-only commitment framework.

## [6] CRITIC

## Australia should keep its options—not withdraw before an alternative exists

The advocate’s strongest argument is that **specific, reciprocal agreements could offer a better bargain than Paris’s broad expectation of increasing ambition**. That is plausible. But it supports negotiating those agreements—not leaving Paris before Australia knows whether they can be secured.

### 1. Staying does not mean accepting an irreversible commitment

The choice is not “withdraw now” versus “remain locked in forever.” Article 28 expressly permits withdrawal, effective one year after notification once the initial eligibility period has passed. [Paris Agreement, Article 28](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)

Australia can therefore retain membership while testing the advocate’s alternative:

- Seek sector agreements with measurable reciprocal obligations.
- Assess whether they cover enough emissions and attract enough partners.
- Compare their actual terms with the benefits and constraints of Paris.

**My inference:** this is a less risky sequence than surrendering an existing framework in exchange for agreements whose successful negotiation the advocate acknowledges is uncertain. The argument identifies no immediate target problem that makes withdrawal necessary before that testing occurs.

### 2. Reciprocity is useful—but narrower deals leave gaps

I accept the advocate’s correction: a time-limited sector agreement with review clauses would not simply recreate Paris. Such agreements can offer genuinely different commitments.

But clearer reciprocity does not necessarily mean better climate cooperation overall. What happens to emissions from countries and industries outside those deals? Who coordinates the combined effort?

Paris addresses that broader task through successive national plans and a five-year assessment of collective progress. It does not guarantee adequate action, but it supplies a common framework beyond individual bargains. [Articles 4 and 14](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)

**The unsupported assumption is that a collection of narrower bargains would adequately replace that broader coordination.** Until demonstrated, sector agreements are a reason to supplement Paris, not abandon it.

### 3. The revised safeguards improve the proposal—but do not justify departure

The advocate is right that leaving Paris would not automatically end Australia’s underlying Convention reporting obligations. That narrows the accountability loss, and the distinction matters.

Likewise, requiring governments to disclose the additional emissions and climate costs of delaying targets is an improvement. But **disclosing a cost is not the same as limiting it**. The proposal still specifies no substantive threshold for an acceptable relaxation or requirement to compensate for additional emissions.

That leaves a central trade-off unresolved: Australia would gain greater discretion to weaken targets, but the proposed safeguards do not establish when exercising that discretion would produce a better overall outcome.

**Bottom line:** cooperation need not include Paris forever. But Australia should negotiate and evaluate credible alternatives before withdrawing. The advocate has established a possible value in greater flexibility—not a sufficient reason to pay the costs of departure now.

## [7] ADVOCATE

## Australia should withdraw—but preparation should precede notification

The critic’s strongest objection is about **sequencing**: why surrender an existing framework before a replacement is ready? That objection improves the withdrawal proposal. It does not establish that Australia should remain in Paris indefinitely.

### 1. Prepare the domestic replacement first—not every international agreement

I support legislating the replacement framework **before lodging withdrawal notice**. Paris then provides a further year before withdrawal takes effect. [Article 28](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)

But making departure conditional on successfully negotiating a comprehensive network of sector agreements sets an unnecessarily high hurdle. Those agreements are a potential improvement in cooperation—not something I can promise will replace every function of Paris.

The distinction is:

- Australia should not leave without credible emissions policy and reporting.
- Australia need not secure other governments’ agreement before choosing a different framework for its own commitments.

**The weakness in the critic’s sequencing argument:** it treats resolving uncertainty about international negotiations as a prerequisite for recovering domestic discretion. That could postpone the governance change indefinitely.

### 2. Give target revisions a substantive test

The critic correctly says disclosure alone is insufficient. I would therefore require any relaxation to satisfy a legislated test:

- An independent assessment identifies a material change in the assumptions underpinning the timetable.
- Feasible alternatives—including different technologies and implementation policies—have been assessed.
- The government demonstrates that revision offers a better overall balance after counting additional cumulative emissions, climate risks, reliability, affordability and distributional effects.
- Parliament approves a limited revision, with a review date and an explanation of whether additional emissions will be compensated.

This cannot make difficult judgments mechanical. But it would distinguish **evidence-based correction from weakening targets merely because compliance has become politically inconvenient**.

Crucially, the test should not automatically require later compensation regardless of cost: that would recreate the inflexibility the proposal seeks to remove.

### 3. Broad coverage is valuable, but not decisive

I accept that narrower agreements leave gaps. I am not claiming they would necessarily outperform Paris globally.

However, the critic has shown that Paris provides broad coordination—not that its particular ambition constraint is indispensable to obtaining useful cooperation from Australia. Australian mitigation, Convention reporting and practical international partnerships could continue after withdrawal.

The central disagreement is therefore about the default rule: **should a timetable remain internationally committed even when a transparent domestic assessment supports revising it downward?**

My judgment is no. Climate consequences must carry substantial weight, but they should be weighed openly alongside other consequences—not treated as establishing an effectively one-directional policy framework.

**Bottom line:** Australia should prepare a credible statutory replacement, then withdraw through Article 28. The critic makes a persuasive case against an unprepared exit, not against a carefully governed withdrawal.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

**Australia should withdraw from the United Nations Paris Agreement on climate change.**

The Advocate supports withdrawal alongside a legislated national climate framework, not abandonment of climate action. The Critic argues that Australia should remain while improving domestic policy and evaluating alternatives before accepting the costs of departure.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Greater flexibility over targets and timing:** Paris expects successive national commitments to become more ambitious. Although Australia controls implementation, the Advocate argues that it should also be able to relax targets or timetables when circumstances materially change.
- **No guarantee of comparable international effort:** Nationally determined contributions and non-punitive compliance do not ensure that trading partners undertake equivalent reductions or costs. The Advocate identifies industrial relocation as a possible risk, not an inevitable outcome.
- **A statutory domestic replacement:** The final proposal includes parliamentary emissions budgets, independent assessments, public reporting and evidence-based reviews. Relaxing a timetable would require:
  - A material change in its underlying assumptions.
  - Assessment of feasible implementation alternatives.
  - Consideration of cumulative emissions, climate risks, reliability, affordability and distributional effects.
  - Parliamentary approval of a limited revision with a review date.
- **More selective international commitments:** Australia could pursue narrower agreements with defined durations, measurable reciprocal obligations and review clauses. The Advocate acknowledges that securing them is uncertain and that they might not replace every Paris function.
- **Continued cooperation and reporting:** Mitigation, research, resilience measures and international partnerships could continue. Withdrawal from Paris would not automatically end Australia’s reporting obligations under the underlying UN Framework Convention on Climate Change.
- **Preparation before departure:** By the final round, the Advocate supports legislating the domestic replacement before giving withdrawal notice, but rejects requiring a comprehensive network of international agreements first.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **No demonstrated advantage from withdrawal:** Leaving would not itself lower electricity prices, improve reliability or repeal domestic climate laws. The Advocate has not identified an immediate target problem requiring departure.
- **Substantial flexibility already exists:** Australia determines its contribution and can change technologies and implementation policies within Paris. Most proposed domestic governance improvements do not require withdrawal.
- **International accountability and coordination have value:** Common reporting, review and five-year collective assessments help countries evaluate others’ actions, even without punitive enforcement. Domestic auditing and narrower agreements cannot necessarily replace these functions.
- **Delay has climate costs:** Additional cumulative emissions can increase warming unless subsequently compensated. Greater freedom to weaken targets may reduce immediate adjustment costs while increasing climate risks or future transition burdens.
- **Constraints can strengthen credible cooperation:** Commitments help partners trust that cooperation will endure difficult circumstances. More flexibility does not automatically produce a better overall outcome.
- **Test alternatives before leaving:** Australia can negotiate sector agreements while remaining in Paris and retains the treaty’s withdrawal option. The Critic favours assessing those agreements’ actual coverage and terms before surrendering the existing framework.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Implementation flexibility versus target flexibility:** The Critic emphasises Australia’s freedom to change domestic policies. The Advocate replies that this does not equally accommodate reducing the ambition of the target or timetable itself.
- **Demonstrating necessity versus demonstrating benefit:** The Advocate rejects a requirement to show that withdrawal is the only possible solution. The Critic accepts that qualification but maintains that departure must still be shown to offer a better balance than membership.
- **Reciprocity versus broad coordination:** The Advocate argues that narrower, reciprocal agreements could offer a more specific bargain. The Critic accepts that these need not recreate Paris, but questions their coverage and ability to coordinate overall action.
- **Accountability after withdrawal:** The Advocate clarifies that Convention reporting would remain. The Critic accepts this correction; both nevertheless acknowledge the loss of Paris’s enhanced transparency framework.
- **Safeguards for relaxing targets:** After the Critic argues that disclosure alone is insufficient, the Advocate adds a substantive legislative test. The final proposal still does not automatically require compensation for additional emissions, which the Advocate considers potentially inflexible.
- **Sequencing:** The Critic urges negotiating and evaluating credible alternatives first. The Advocate agrees to prepare domestic legislation before notification but argues that making departure dependent on other governments’ agreements could postpone it indefinitely.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides accept that:

- Climate action and international cooperation should continue.
- Reliability, affordability and industrial competitiveness deserve consideration.
- Paris entails an expectation of increasing ambition but does not prescribe Australia’s technology mix or impose an enforceable emissions quota.
- Withdrawal alone would not solve energy problems or repeal Australian climate laws.
- Paris cannot guarantee equal international effort, but its accountability framework has value.
- Delaying reductions can increase cumulative emissions and climate harm.
- Transparent domestic scrutiny is worthwhile and can be strengthened within Paris.
- Narrower reciprocal agreements may be useful, but their negotiation and effectiveness are uncertain.
- An unprepared withdrawal would be undesirable.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **The value of target flexibility:** Does greater discretion to reduce ambition justify losing Paris-specific accountability, coordination and diplomatic trust?
- **The adequacy of safeguards:** Would the Advocate’s final legislative test sufficiently discipline target relaxation without mandatory compensation for additional emissions?
- **The role of alternative agreements:** Could selective reciprocal commitments adequately support cooperation, or should they supplement Paris rather than replace membership?
- **The threshold for departure:** Is a credible domestic replacement enough, or should Australia first establish that international alternatives offer a better overall outcome?
- **The preferred default:** Should targets remain subject to an international expectation of increasing ambition, or be reversible through a transparent domestic process?

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate develops from a general case for national flexibility into a more specific proposal for **prepared withdrawal under statutory safeguards**. The Advocate acknowledges climate, diplomatic and accountability costs, while arguing that Australia should retain discretion over both the means and timing of emissions reductions.

The Critic accepts that flexibility and more reciprocal agreements may have value but argues that their benefits have not been shown to outweigh departure’s costs. The central unresolved issue is whether carefully governed discretion to weaken targets offers a better framework than retaining Paris membership while improving domestic policy and testing complementary agreements.
