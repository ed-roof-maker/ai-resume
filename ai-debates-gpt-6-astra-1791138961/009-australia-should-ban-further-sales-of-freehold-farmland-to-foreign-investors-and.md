## Claim

Australia should ban further sales of freehold farmland to foreign investors and limit foreign investors’ farmland leases to 25 years.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/farming

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

**Australia should keep permanent ownership of its farmland in Australian hands, while allowing foreign investors to farm it under leases of up to 25 years.** That preserves access to overseas capital without selling an enduring stake in a finite national resource.

### 1. Permanent ownership is a bigger concession than investment requires
Farmland supports food production, regional communities and environmental stewardship. Investors can contribute money, technology and expertise without owning the underlying land forever.

Foreign ownership does not remove land from Australian law. But ownership still matters: within that law, owners decide whether to sell, whom to lease to, and which investments to pursue. The argument is not that foreign investors are inherently bad farmers; it is that **their investment need not come with indefinite ownership.**

### 2. A 25-year lease offers a workable middle ground
Twenty-five years gives investors a substantial operating horizon while ensuring that control of the land eventually returns to its Australian owner.

The strongest objection is that some agricultural improvements take longer to pay off. That is a real concern, but contracts can provide compensation for approved improvements remaining at expiry. Any later lease should require a fresh decision—not an automatic extension that turns a nominal 25-year limit into permanent control.

There is no magic economic threshold at 25 years. It is a policy judgment balancing investment certainty against the value of periodically reconsidering who controls farmland.

### 3. Screening purchases is not the same as preserving ownership
Australia already subjects certain foreign agricultural-land acquisitions to review, recognising that these transactions warrant special scrutiny. [Source: Australian Treasury’s foreign-investment guidance](https://foreigninvestment.gov.au/guidance/types-investments/agricultural-land)

However, approval assesses a transaction under present circumstances. It cannot reliably predict every future change in ownership, investment strategy or international relations. A prospective ban establishes a clearer boundary without confiscating existing holdings.

### The trade-off is worth acknowledging
Restricting buyers could lower some sale prices or deter investors who insist on freehold ownership. Nor would this policy alone guarantee affordable food or sustainable farming.

But maximising today’s purchase price is not the only legitimate national objective. **Australia can welcome foreign participation while reserving permanent farmland ownership for Australians.** Effective rules would need to cover indirect acquisitions and arrangements designed to disguise longer leases.

## [2] CRITIC

**Australia should regulate risks from foreign farmland investment, not prohibit permanent ownership regardless of the buyer or proposal.** The advocate establishes that ownership matters—but not that Australian ownership reliably delivers better outcomes.

### 1. Keeping an Australian landlord is not the same as keeping public control
The strongest argument for the ban is that foreign capital can contribute without receiving permanent ownership. That is true. But it does not establish why freehold ownership should be prohibited.

Freehold is an enduring property right, **not sovereignty**: foreign-owned farmland remains subject to Australian law. Conversely, an Australian owner need not prioritise affordable food, local employment or environmental protection.

The unsupported assumption is that the owner’s nationality is a sufficiently reliable safeguard for those interests. If the concern is harmful land use, governments should regulate that conduct regardless of who owns the farm.

### 2. A 25-year limit creates uncertainty that compensation cannot simply erase
Compensation for improvements is a possible contractual safeguard, not a complete answer. Who determines an improvement’s remaining value? Can the landlord afford the payment? What happens if the parties disagree?

An investor approaching expiry must consider those risks before undertaking another major improvement. **The relevant investment horizon is the time remaining on the lease**, not its original 25-year length.

These are economic risks, not proof that every lease would fail. But the advocate offers no evidence that 25 years strikes a better balance than longer leases or assessed freehold purchases.

### 3. Lease expiry does not guarantee meaningful national reconsideration
At expiry, the Australian landlord could simply choose the same foreign operator again. That would be a fresh private decision—not necessarily a public assessment of Australia’s interests.

If public review is the objective, ownership restrictions are an indirect mechanism. Appropriate reporting, enforcement and scrutiny of relevant transactions address that objective more directly.

### 4. Imperfect screening does not justify a blanket ban
[Treasury’s guidance](https://foreigninvestment.gov.au/guidance/types-investments/agricultural-land) confirms an existing framework for reviewing foreign agricultural-land investment. It cannot predict everything—but neither can a nationality-based prohibition.

A blanket rule treats a productive, compliant investor like a genuinely risky one. Meanwhile, its acknowledged costs—fewer potential buyers and potentially weaker investment—fall partly on Australian farmers and regional businesses.

**The unresolved question is proportionality:** what concrete harm does this ban prevent that targeted safeguards cannot? Without that answer, permanent Australian ownership is a preference, not a sufficient justification for these sweeping restrictions.

## [3] ADVOCATE

**The critic’s strongest objection is proportionality: why restrict a compliant investor when Australia can regulate harmful conduct?** My answer is that regulating farming and deciding who may hold permanent title are different policy choices.

### 1. The safeguard is a retained ownership right—not Australian owners’ goodwill
I do not assume Australian landlords are better farmers or more public-spirited. They should face the same environmental, competition and land-use rules.

The additional objective is **keeping permanent title to a finite national resource in Australian hands while making foreign control time-limited**. A lease provides an enforceable endpoint; freehold does not. Reversing a freehold acquisition later requires a voluntary sale or government intervention.

The critic’s proposed safeguards address misconduct. They do not preserve that endpoint. Treating them as complete substitutes assumes that permanent ownership has no independent public value—the very issue under debate.

That value is a policy judgment, not evidence that foreign ownership necessarily damages food security.

### 2. Lease expiry must mean something
The critic rightly notes that renewal by a private landlord is not a national-interest assessment. I would therefore refine the proposal:

- No automatic renewal rights or linked arrangements guaranteeing control beyond 25 years.
- Any subsequent lease must be a genuinely new agreement, subject to applicable foreign-investment scrutiny.
- Existing lawful freehold holdings would not be confiscated.

Renewing a successful operator is compatible with this policy. **The distinction is between continued access by fresh agreement and an indefinite entitlement.**

### 3. Investment uncertainty is a real cost—but not a decisive objection
The critic is correct that an investor in year 23 has only two years of guaranteed tenure remaining. Compensation cannot eliminate that problem.

Contracts can nevertheless specify independent valuation, dispute resolution and secured payment for approved improvements. Those protections reduce uncertainty; they do not erase it. Projects requiring longer guaranteed control may consequently lose investment.

The case for 25 years is therefore a compromise, not a scientifically established optimum: substantial operating time, but a meaningful limit rather than near-permanent control.

### 4. Proportionality includes what the policy preserves
Australian sellers could receive lower offers, and some foreign investors could withdraw. Those costs deserve scrutiny.

But this is a prospective restriction on **one form of investment**, not a ban on foreign farming or capital. The critic gives considerable weight to unrestricted sale opportunities while giving little weight to retaining permanent domestic ownership.

**Australia can reasonably decide that overseas investment is welcome, but permanent title to its farmland is not part of the bargain.** That boundary should complement strong regulation—not pretend to replace it.

## [4] CRITIC

**A future right to reconsider foreign control has value. But the advocate has not shown that a blanket freehold ban and a uniform 25-year lease cap are a proportionate way to secure it.**

### 1. An endpoint preserves a private choice—not necessarily a national benefit
The advocate’s strongest argument is that a lease lets an Australian owner recover control without buying the land back. That is a genuine distinction from freehold.

But **who receives that option, and how does it benefit the wider public?** The option belongs to the landlord, who may renew the foreign operator, change tenants or seek higher rent. None of those choices necessarily advances Australia’s strategic interests.

Domestic ownership can legitimately be valued. Yet simply defining it as the objective does not establish that its public benefits outweigh the acknowledged costs to Australian sellers and investment. That comparison remains missing.

### 2. The proposed deadline is poorly matched to changing risks
The advocate worries that circumstances may change after a purchase is approved. But circumstances can also change early in a 25-year lease.

- If serious risks arise in year three, waiting until year 25 is no answer; Australia still needs effective regulatory powers.
- If an operator remains productive and low-risk in year 25, compulsory uncertainty may serve no practical purpose.

**The calendar is not a risk assessment.** The advocate’s proposed scrutiny of subsequent leases is useful precisely because it assesses circumstances—not merely because a deadline has arrived. That supports tailored scrutiny rather than one tenure limit for every investor and project.

### 3. Compensation shifts costs; it does not remove them
Independent valuation and secured compensation could protect investment. But they also require someone to fund the security and bear the eventual payment.

An Australian landlord may therefore have to choose between accepting financial obligations and discouraging improvements. This is an economic inference, not a claim that every lease would fail. It shows why allowing foreign capital through leases is not necessarily an equivalent substitute for allowing ownership.

### 4. A narrower approach remains available
Where a particular acquisition presents an enduring-control risk, Australia could reject it or require an appropriately structured lease. Lower-risk proposals need not face the same prohibition.

**The choice is not unrestricted foreign ownership versus permanent domestic ownership.** It is whether restrictions should respond to identifiable risks or apply regardless of them. The advocate has explained a preference for domestic title, but not why every future foreign freehold purchase—and every lease longer than 25 years—must be ruled out.

## [5] ADVOCATE

**The critic is right that an Australian landlord’s interests are not identical to the public interest. The stronger case for this policy is therefore structural: Australia should accept foreign participation without progressively transferring permanent ownership of its agricultural land overseas.**

### 1. The public benefit is broader than the landlord’s option
Freehold ownership includes the ability to collect future rent, benefit from land appreciation and decide who occupies the land. Reserving those enduring rights for Australian owners preserves a domestic ownership base; leasing still allows overseas investors to earn returns from farming.

That does **not** guarantee fair distribution, good farming or cheaper food. Domestic concentration and harmful conduct require separate policies. But an imperfect domestic ownership base can still be worth preserving.

The critic evaluates each investor’s conduct. That misses the cumulative question: **even if every purchaser behaves well, how much permanent overseas ownership should Australia accept?** A transaction can be individually unobjectionable while contributing to a long-term ownership pattern Australians reasonably reject.

This is a public-policy judgment, not an empirically proven claim that foreign owners perform worse.

### 2. A lease deadline serves a different purpose from risk assessment
The year-three/year-25 objection treats the lease limit as though it were an emergency safeguard. It is not.

- Regulation must address serious problems whenever they arise.
- Lease expiry prevents a right granted today from guaranteeing foreign control indefinitely.

A productive operator need not leave at year 25. It must secure a new agreement, without a pre-existing entitlement to renewal, and meet applicable scrutiny. The uncertainty is real, but so is the distinction between **earning continued access and owning permanent access**.

### 3. The narrower alternative leaves the structural issue unresolved
The critic would restrict only acquisitions presenting identifiable risks. That is sensible for misconduct or security threats. But it does not necessarily preserve domestic ownership: successive low-risk approvals can still permanently transfer farmland overseas.

A blanket boundary is justified **if permanent domestic ownership is itself an accepted objective**, rather than merely a proxy for better behaviour. Its consistency also avoids having to label an otherwise responsible investor “dangerous” to decline a freehold purchase.

### 4. Why accept the costs—and 25 years?
Compensation arrangements shift costs; they do not abolish them. Nor is there evidence here establishing 25 years as the optimal term. I defend it as a substantial but genuinely limited operating horizon.

The proportionality argument rests on the policy’s scope: **no confiscation, continued foreign farming and investment, but no further permanent title transfers or guaranteed tenure beyond 25 years.**

That is not costless. It is a defensible decision to preserve a lasting domestic asset base rather than maximise today’s sale prices and investors’ tenure certainty.

## [6] CRITIC

**The advocate’s structural concern deserves an answer: individually harmless purchases could collectively produce an undesirable ownership pattern. But that does not establish that Australia should permit no further foreign freehold purchases—or prohibit every lease beyond 25 years.**

### 1. Selling land is not simply surrendering future wealth
The advocate emphasises future rent and land appreciation retained by Australian owners. But a foreign buyer pays an Australian seller for those rights.

**The seller exchanges land for money; the asset’s value does not simply disappear overseas.** That money could fund another Australian business, reduce debt or support retirement. Meanwhile, retaining title preserves exposure to losses as well as potential gains.

This does not prove every sale benefits Australia. It does expose an unsupported assumption: that keeping wealth in farmland necessarily creates more public value than allowing Australians to sell it and invest elsewhere. Counting future rental income while overlooking the payment received for it gives an incomplete economic picture.

### 2. Permanent title does not mean permanently foreign ownership
Freehold has no expiry date, but the owner’s nationality is not fixed forever. A foreign owner can subsequently sell to an Australian buyer. Conversely, successive new leases could keep the same foreign operator on a property for generations.

The advocate acknowledges renewal, yet describes purchases as progressively transferring ownership overseas. **That treats a possible cumulative trend as a one-way process without establishing that it is one.**

The distinction between ownership and renewable access is real. But neither arrangement alone determines the actual long-term ownership or operating pattern.

### 3. A cumulative concern calls for a cumulative test—not automatically a ban
If the concern is excessive foreign ownership, the missing questions are:

- How much is excessive?
- Does location or concentration matter?
- Why must the acceptable additional amount be zero?

A possible alternative would assess aggregate holdings and concentration when reviewing acquisitions. That would address the advocate’s structural concern, not merely investor misconduct.

Such an approach would need careful design. But **“individual screening may miss accumulation” does not logically lead to “ban every additional purchase.”** No evidence presented establishes that Australia has reached a point where all further acquisitions are unacceptable.

### 4. The ownership argument still cannot justify the lease cap
Even accepting permanent Australian title as the objective, a 30- or 40-year lease preserves it too. The advocate has not explained why those arrangements sacrifice an unacceptable public interest while a 25-year lease does not.

Calling 25 years “substantial” establishes that it is not a short lease—not that it is an appropriate maximum for every farm and investment.

**Domestic ownership is a legitimate preference. The unresolved issue is whether that preference warrants removing Australian sellers’ choices and limiting investment across the board. The advocate has defended the aspiration, but not the necessity or breadth of these restrictions.**

## [7] ADVOCATE

**The critic’s strongest point is that an aggregate ownership limit could address accumulation with fewer restrictions.** That is a serious alternative. But it pursues a different objective: allowing further permanent transfers until a quota is reached, rather than preserving the domestic ownership base Australia still has.

### 1. Sale proceeds count—but do not settle the ownership question
The critic is right: an Australian seller receives payment. Retaining farmland does not automatically make Australia wealthier, and my earlier emphasis on future rent needed that qualification.

The case for the ban is therefore **not that selling destroys the asset’s financial value**. It is that money and ownership rights are not interchangeable for every public-policy purpose. A seller can receive fair compensation while the country loses something it values: domestic ownership of that particular land.

That is a judgment about the desired ownership structure, not a claim of a free economic benefit. The cost is restricting a seller’s opportunities; the benefit is preserving domestic title. Neither side should count only one.

### 2. A quota manages further transfers; a ban avoids them
An aggregate limit would require deciding how much additional foreign ownership is acceptable—and whether holdings in different regions or types of farmland should count equally.

Those questions are not unanswerable. But the critic’s alternative also needs a justified threshold. **It does not escape value judgments simply by replacing a prohibition with a percentage.**

A prospective ban offers a defensible baseline: honour existing ownership, but stop granting further permanent titles to foreign investors. This does not require claiming that existing holdings constitute a crisis. It treats the remaining domestic ownership base as something worth keeping, rather than something available for transfer until measurable harm emerges.

### 3. Possible resale is weaker protection than an enforceable endpoint
I accept that foreign ownership is not necessarily permanent. A foreign owner may sell to an Australian.

But that possibility depends on the owner’s willingness to sell and an Australian buyer’s ability to purchase. Lease expiry does not require the landlord to buy the land back. Likewise, repeated leases can sustain foreign operation—but only through successive agreements.

**The relevant distinction is guaranteed indefinite tenure versus continued tenure requiring fresh consent**, not a prediction that every foreign owner will hold land forever.

### 4. Why stop leases at 25 years?
A 40-year lease preserves Australian title, but postpones the owner’s unconditional opportunity to recover possession for another 15 years. That delay is the additional concession.

Twenty-five years is a policy compromise, not an evidence-established optimum: substantial security for an operator, without binding the owner for several further decades. Compensation provisions can reduce investment problems, though not eliminate their costs.

The critic has shown that other boundaries are possible—not that longer guaranteed tenure should always prevail. **Australia can reasonably prioritise retained domestic ownership and periodic freedom to reconsider access, while continuing to welcome foreign farming under substantial leases.**



## Summary

## 1. Claim

Australia should ban further sales of freehold farmland to foreign investors and limit their farmland leases to 25 years.

The Advocate supports a prospective policy that preserves existing lawful freehold holdings, prohibits guaranteed renewals beyond the lease limit, and permits subsequent leases through fresh agreements subject to applicable scrutiny.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Domestic ownership has independent value.** Keeping permanent title in Australian hands preserves a domestic ownership base, including enduring rights over rent, appreciation and occupation. This is a structural policy objective—not a claim that Australian owners necessarily farm better or serve the public interest.
- **Foreign investment need not require freehold ownership.** Overseas investors could still contribute capital, technology and expertise, and earn farming returns through leases.
- **Leases provide an enforceable endpoint.** Recovering possession at expiry does not require an Australian owner to buy the land back. Continued foreign operation would depend on fresh consent rather than an indefinite entitlement.
- **Transaction screening does not necessarily address cumulative ownership changes.** Individually low-risk purchases could collectively produce an ownership pattern Australia wishes to avoid.
- **Twenty-five years is a policy compromise.** The Advocate presents it as a substantial but meaningfully limited operating period, while acknowledging that it is not an evidence-established optimum.
- **The restrictions involve accepted trade-offs.** Lower sale prices, reduced buyer choice and some lost investment may be worth accepting to retain domestic title. The policy would complement—not replace—land-use and other regulation.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **Private Australian ownership is not equivalent to public control.** An Australian landlord’s choices need not advance food affordability, employment, environmental protection or strategic interests. Foreign-owned land remains subject to Australian law.
- **The public benefit has not been sufficiently demonstrated.** Valuing domestic ownership does not by itself establish that a blanket prohibition outweighs costs to sellers, investors and regional businesses.
- **A uniform lease cap can discourage investment.** Investors consider the time remaining on a lease, not its original length. Compensation arrangements introduce valuation, funding and dispute risks rather than eliminating costs.
- **A deadline is not a risk assessment.** Problems can arise well before expiry, while a productive, low-risk operator may face unnecessary uncertainty at year 25.
- **Narrower measures remain available.** The Critic proposes targeted scrutiny, rejection or lease requirements for risky acquisitions, and consideration of aggregate holdings and concentration to address cumulative concerns.
- **The economic and ownership picture is more complex than permanent loss.** Australian sellers receive payment and may use it elsewhere; retained land carries risks as well as gains. Foreign owners can sell back to Australians, while repeated leases can sustain foreign operation for generations.
- **Neither zero further purchases nor a 25-year maximum is adequately justified.** Longer leases also preserve Australian title, and the debate provides no established threshold for excessive foreign ownership.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Ownership versus conduct:** The Critic argues that regulation should target harmful behaviour. The Advocate responds that conduct regulation cannot secure the separate objective of retaining domestic title. The Critic maintains that this objective still requires a public-benefit and proportionality justification.
- **Private renewal versus public reconsideration:** After the Critic notes that renewal is a landlord’s decision, the Advocate clarifies that later leases must be genuinely new agreements, without automatic renewal rights and subject to applicable scrutiny.
- **Investment protection:** The Advocate proposes independent valuation, dispute resolution and secured compensation for approved improvements. The Critic counters that these mechanisms shift financial obligations and cannot remove end-of-lease uncertainty; the Advocate accepts those limitations.
- **Cumulative ownership:** The Critic offers aggregate ownership and concentration tests. The Advocate recognises this as a serious alternative but argues that it allows further transfers rather than preserving the remaining domestic ownership base, and would also require a justified threshold.
- **Sale proceeds:** The Critic challenges the emphasis on retaining future rent and appreciation because sellers receive payment. The Advocate accepts the qualification, reframing the benefit as retaining particular ownership rights rather than necessarily increasing national wealth.
- **Resale and repeated leasing:** The Critic observes that foreign ownership need not last forever and leasing can continue indefinitely through renewals. The Advocate replies that possible resale is different from an enforceable endpoint: renewed access requires fresh consent.
- **Why 25 years:** The Critic argues that 30- or 40-year leases also retain domestic title. The Advocate responds that they postpone the owner’s opportunity to recover possession, while continuing to acknowledge that 25 years is a judgment rather than a demonstrated optimum.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides acknowledge that:

- Foreign investment can contribute without freehold ownership.
- Foreign ownership does not remove farmland from Australian law.
- Australian ownership does not guarantee better farming or public-interest outcomes.
- Lease expiry offers a genuine option unavailable under continuing freehold ownership.
- Regulation remains necessary regardless of ownership or tenure.
- Restrictions could reduce sale prices, seller choice and investment.
- Compensation can mitigate, but not eliminate, lease-related costs and uncertainty.
- Domestic ownership can legitimately be valued, although they disagree over the restrictions that value justifies.
- The debate does not establish 25 years as the optimal lease limit.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- Whether preserving domestic title is sufficient justification for banning every further foreign freehold purchase.
- Whether that structural benefit outweighs restrictions on Australian sellers and potential investment losses.
- Whether targeted and aggregate screening can adequately address the Advocate’s concerns.
- Whether fresh private consent at lease expiry provides enough public value to justify compulsory tenure uncertainty.
- Why the permissible additional foreign freehold ownership should be zero, and why every foreign farmland lease should be capped at 25 years.

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate centres on whether permanent domestic farmland ownership should be protected as an independent policy objective or through restrictions tied to identifiable risks and cumulative ownership levels.

The Advocate defends a prospective boundary that retains Australian title while allowing substantial foreign participation through leases, openly accepting economic costs and uncertainty over the precise lease limit. The Critic accepts that ownership and lease endpoints matter but argues that the necessity, breadth and proportionality of the proposed restrictions remain unproven. The central unresolved question is how much weight domestic ownership and periodic consent should carry against seller choice, investment certainty and more tailored regulation.
