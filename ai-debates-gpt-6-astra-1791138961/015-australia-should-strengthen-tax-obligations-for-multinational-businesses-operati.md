# A Multi Agent AI Policy Debate
AI can make mistakes. Always critically evaluate the content of the debate.

## Claim

Australia should strengthen tax obligations for multinational businesses operating in Australia without increasing the GST or reintroducing inheritance taxes.

## Source

https://www.onenation.org.au/tax

## Debate

## [1] ADVOCATE

## Opening argument: Tax Australian business profits fairly—not everyday spending more heavily

Australia should strengthen multinational tax obligations because **businesses earning profits from Australian activity should not gain a tax advantage simply by shifting those profits overseas.** That means improving the rules and their enforcement—not assuming every multinational is avoiding tax or automatically raising company tax rates.

### 1. Close the gap between local businesses and global competitors

A local business generally cannot move its profits between countries. A multinational can arrange internal loans, royalties and service charges across borders. These transactions can be legitimate, but they can also shift taxable profits away from where real business activity occurs.

This is a recognised international problem, not merely a suspicion. The [OECD’s work on base erosion and profit shifting](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/policy-issues/base-erosion-and-profit-shifting-beps.html) specifically addresses tax avoidance that exploits gaps between countries’ tax systems.

**The fairness argument:** competition should reward better products and greater efficiency—not access to more elaborate offshore tax arrangements.

### 2. Strengthen targeted obligations, rather than impose a blanket penalty

Australia should build on its existing safeguards by:

- **Enforcing limits on artificial profit shifting**, including excessive related-party interest deductions and inflated offshore charges.
- **Requiring useful country-by-country reporting**, so tax authorities can compare reported profits with actual business activity.
- **Giving the ATO the specialist capacity to investigate complex arrangements**, while evaluating whether that spending delivers results.
- **Coordinating internationally**, including through the global minimum-tax framework, to reduce opportunities to move profits between low-tax jurisdictions.

These measures should include clear rules, proportionate compliance requirements and protection against taxing the same profit twice.

### 3. This is preferable to raising GST—and does not require inheritance taxes

Raising GST would collect more from everyday consumption, including from households already spending most of their income on necessities. Strengthening multinational compliance instead targets profits that should properly fall within Australia’s tax base.

Inheritance taxation addresses a different question: transfers of private wealth. Australia does not need to reopen that debate to improve taxation of multinational business profits.

### The strongest objection: Could this discourage investment?

Poorly designed rules could. But **taxing genuine Australian profits fairly is not the same as punishing investment**. Predictable rules, legitimate deductions and international coordination can protect productive investment while making artificial profit shifting less rewarding.

Additional revenue is not guaranteed, and this is no substitute for sound budgeting. Nevertheless, collecting tax more effectively from the existing corporate tax base is a fairer first step than increasing GST or reintroducing inheritance taxes.

## [2] CRITIC

## Rebuttal: Fair taxation is a goal—not proof that tougher rules are needed

The advocate’s strongest point is that multinationals should not undercut local businesses by artificially shifting Australian profits offshore. Agreed. **But that supports enforcing effective tax laws; it does not automatically justify strengthening obligations further.**

### 1. Show what existing rules fail to catch

Australia already has transfer-pricing rules, a multinational anti-avoidance law, a diverted profits tax and country-by-country reporting requirements. These are described in the [ATO’s international tax guidance](https://www.ato.gov.au/businesses-and-organisations/international-tax-for-business).

The advocate proposes several tools that therefore already exist, without identifying:
- which loopholes remain;
- why existing powers cannot address them;
- how much additional revenue a specific change would realistically collect.

The OECD evidence establishes that international profit shifting is a real problem. **It does not establish that another layer of Australian obligations would deliver benefits greater than its costs.** That requires a proposal-specific case.

### 2. “Genuine Australian profits” are not always straightforward to identify

A multinational may develop technology overseas, manufacture in several countries and sell in Australia. Some payments abroad legitimately compensate the people and businesses responsible for creating that product.

The advocate acknowledges legitimate deductions, but leaves the difficult boundary unresolved. Stricter limits can catch artificial charges—or deny genuine costs.

**The risk, rather than a guaranteed outcome:** poorly targeted restrictions could tax profits Australia has only a partial claim to, create disputes and make otherwise worthwhile Australian investments less attractive. Promising “clear rules” and protection against double taxation is not evidence those problems have been solved.

### 3. Compliance costs matter even without higher tax rates

Additional reporting and documentation consume resources even when a business has paid the correct tax. International coordination helps, but country-specific requirements can still overlap or conflict.

Before imposing more obligations, government should demonstrate that the information is necessary, not already available, and likely to change enforcement outcomes.

### 4. The GST comparison does not establish the case

Rejecting further multinational obligations does **not** require supporting higher GST or inheritance taxes. Australia can assess existing reforms, improve administration within existing powers, review ineffective concessions or reconsider spending.

Nor should uncertain receipts from anti-avoidance measures determine which broader tax options remain available.

**Bottom line:** Require evidence of a specific gap and a workable, cost-effective remedy first. The advocate has made a persuasive case against tax avoidance—not yet a sufficient case for stronger multinational obligations.

## [3] ADVOCATE

## Rebuttal: Strengthen the information obligation—not simply add another anti-avoidance law

The critic’s strongest objection is fair: **the existence of profit shifting does not justify every new tax requirement.** My opening argument was too broad about reporting measures Australia already has. The stronger case is for targeted improvements that make existing laws work earlier and more effectively.

### 1. A specific proposal: early disclosure of high-risk cross-border schemes

Australia should require businesses and advisers to disclose narrowly defined, high-risk cross-border tax arrangements early, **where existing reporting does not already supply equivalent information promptly**.

The distinction matters:

- Transfer-pricing rules determine whether related-party prices are acceptable.
- Country-by-country reporting provides an overview of a multinational’s activities and finances.
- Early scheme disclosure would explain **how a particular arrangement produces its tax advantage**, allowing scrutiny before an audit uncovers it much later.

The [OECD’s Action 12 report on mandatory disclosure](https://doi.org/10.1787/9789264241442-en) provides a framework for this approach, including targeting arrangements through defined warning signs and balancing information needs against compliance costs.

**What that evidence establishes:** early disclosure is an internationally developed policy option.  
**What it does not establish:** a particular Australian design would necessarily raise more than it costs. That requires an Australian assessment of information gaps and implementation costs.

### 2. This addresses the critic’s concern about legitimate overseas payments

An overseas royalty is not automatically avoidance. Nor should Australian sales automatically make all associated profits taxable here.

The proposed obligation would require disclosure—not automatically deny deductions or declare a transaction unlawful. Existing substantive rules would still determine the tax outcome.

Safeguards should include:

- Materiality thresholds, excluding small transactions.
- Specific triggers, rather than reporting every overseas payment.
- No duplicate filing where equivalent information is already supplied.
- Protection for legal professional privilege and confidential commercial information.
- Penalties directed at failure to disclose, not merely taking a defensible tax position.

These safeguards reduce risk; they do not eliminate every compliance cost.

### 3. Where the critic’s reasoning falls short

**A list of existing laws is not evidence that authorities receive all necessary information soon enough.** Legal powers and timely visibility are different things.

Likewise, recommending “better administration” does not resolve an information gap if fixing it requires a new disclosure duty. Enforcement and stronger obligations can be complements, not alternatives.

The appropriate test is therefore a targeted cost-benefit assessment—not a presumption that existing obligations are sufficient.

### 4. GST and inheritance taxes remain separate choices

The critic is right that rejecting this proposal would not logically require either tax. My claim is narrower: **Australia can strengthen multinational tax accountability without increasing GST or reintroducing inheritance taxes.** Neither is a prerequisite, and uncertain anti-avoidance revenue should not be booked as a guaranteed solution to budget pressures.

The defensible policy is targeted strengthening, independently evaluated—not indiscriminate extra regulation.

## [4] CRITIC

## Rebuttal: A possible information gap is not yet a case for a new reporting duty

The advocate’s strongest argument is that **tax authorities may need details of a scheme earlier than ordinary reporting provides**. That is a meaningful distinction. But it establishes a question to investigate—not yet an obligation Australia should impose.

### 1. The proposed benefit still depends on an unproven assumption

The advocate has not identified an Australian case where:
- existing reporting delivered information too late;
- earlier disclosure would have changed the enforcement outcome; and
- a new reporting requirement would have supplied the missing information.

The [OECD disclosure framework](https://doi.org/10.1787/9789264241442-en) provides a way to design such rules. It does not, by itself, establish their additional value in Australia.

I do not assume existing information is sufficient. **The point is that uncertainty cuts both ways:** it justifies investigating the gap, not presuming that stronger obligations are the answer.

### 2. “High-risk arrangements” creates a difficult targeting problem

The safeguards sound reasonable, but the reporting triggers remain unspecified.

Narrow triggers may miss novel schemes—the very arrangements early disclosure is meant to uncover. Broad triggers may capture ordinary commercial transactions and encourage precautionary reporting to avoid penalties.

That creates a plausible risk: **the ATO receives more paperwork without receiving proportionately more useful intelligence**. More information is not automatically better enforcement; someone must assess it and act on it.

An Australian evaluation should therefore measure useful leads and improved tax outcomes—not simply disclosures received.

### 3. Disclosure is less intrusive than denying deductions—but not costless

Saying disclosure does not change the tax outcome answers one objection, not all of them. Businesses and advisers must still interpret the triggers, document arrangements and determine whether an exemption applies.

Even the “no duplicate filing” safeguard needs a practical answer: who decides whether previously supplied information is equivalent and timely enough? Uncertainty about that boundary could itself produce duplicate reporting.

These are potential costs, not proof that disclosure rules necessarily fail. But they belong in the decision **before**, not merely after, a mandate.

### 4. “Can strengthen” is weaker than “should strengthen”

The advocate’s conclusion has shifted toward showing that reform *can* occur without GST or inheritance-tax changes. That is true but does not establish that reform is worthwhile.

**The better position is to investigate first and legislate only if demonstrated benefits justify the burden.** Until then, retain existing obligations rather than commit to strengthening them. Neither higher GST nor inheritance taxes follows from that conclusion.

## [5] ADVOCATE

## Rebuttal: The case is for a narrow preventive duty—not blanket reporting

The critic’s strongest objection stands: **I have not demonstrated the net benefit of a particular Australian disclosure regime.** The OECD framework is not an Australian cost-benefit study. But the critic’s proposed test also asks too much if it requires a past case proving exactly what an untried reporting duty would have changed.

### 1. Test the information gap directly

Before commencement, the government should map a proposed duty against existing ATO reporting requirements and establish:

- what important information is missing;
- when the ATO currently receives it;
- what earlier disclosure would let the ATO do;
- what compliance and processing would cost.

That is a practical evidence threshold. It does not require waiting for a completed avoidance case and reconstructing an unknowable alternative outcome.

**The weakness in the critic’s reasoning:** earlier intervention can have value even when we cannot prove that it would have changed the final tax bill in a particular historical case. It may shorten investigations or expose a scheme before it spreads. Those are potential benefits to assess—not guaranteed results.

### 2. Make the proposed triggers concrete

I would narrow my proposal to **marketed cross-border tax arrangements with a material expected tax advantage**, coupled with a specific warning sign such as:

- an adviser’s fee being tied to the tax saving; or
- a confidentiality condition specifically restricting disclosure of how the tax advantage works.

Ordinary commercial confidentiality would not qualify. Nor would an overseas royalty or loan, by itself.

The [OECD’s mandatory-disclosure framework](https://doi.org/10.1787/9789264241442-en) discusses hallmarks including tax-related confidentiality and contingent fees. That supports these as recognised design options, **not as proof that they are optimal for Australia**.

The critic is right that narrow triggers will miss some novel schemes. But a targeted rule need not catch everything to be worthwhile. The relevant question is whether the arrangements it catches justify its cost.

### 3. Resolve duplication in the rules—not through guesswork

The regime should expressly identify existing ATO filings that satisfy the duty. Where information has already been supplied, businesses should reference that filing rather than reproduce it. A single qualifying disclosure should discharge overlapping obligations for the same arrangement.

Before commencement, the ATO should also publish how it will triage and use disclosures. Success should mean actionable intelligence and better enforcement—not a larger document count.

### 4. Why “should,” rather than merely “could”?

My recommendation is **targeted strengthening, with an evidence gate before obligations commence**. If the mapping shows that a proposed trigger adds nothing useful, remove it. That is disciplined policy design, not a reason to reject stronger obligations in principle.

The underlying judgment remains: where material cross-border tax schemes are not visible promptly, the businesses and advisers organising them should bear a proportionate disclosure duty rather than leave the ATO to discover them later.

Neither a GST increase nor an inheritance tax is necessary for that reform. Nor should speculative revenue from it be treated as a substitute for responsible budgeting.

## [6] CRITIC

## Rebuttal: Support an evidence review—not a commitment to stronger obligations

The advocate’s strongest point is that **earlier disclosure could stop a marketed tax scheme spreading or shorten an investigation**, even without changing the eventual tax bill. Those are legitimate potential benefits. But the narrower proposal still has weaknesses that an “evidence gate” does not itself resolve.

### 1. The evidence gate must permit “no new duty”

I accept the advocate’s proposed assessment: identify missing information, establish how earlier access would help, and estimate costs. We do not need proof of an unknowable historical outcome.

But the assessment must compare a new duty with alternatives—not merely refine its triggers. Could changing an existing filing’s timing, improving information sharing, or reallocating investigative resources achieve the same result more cheaply?

**If the government has already committed to “targeted strengthening,” the review risks asking how to introduce a duty rather than whether to introduce one.** An open review is justified; its conclusion is not yet established.

### 2. The concrete triggers target contract terms, not necessarily tax risk

Contingent fees and tax-specific secrecy clauses are understandable warning signs. But consider two otherwise identical schemes:

- One adviser charges according to the tax saving.
- Another charges a fixed fee and imposes no secrecy condition.

Under the proposed triggers, the first may require disclosure while the second escapes.

**This is a design inference, not evidence of observed Australian behaviour:** advisers could potentially change their fee and confidentiality terms without changing the underlying tax arrangement. The assessment therefore needs to consider how people would respond to the rule—not just which arrangements it would capture today.

That creates a dilemma: retain narrow contractual triggers and risk easy circumvention, or broaden them and revive the compliance concerns the advocate sought to avoid.

### 3. Earlier information is valuable only if it changes what the ATO can do

A published triage plan is useful, but it does not establish that the ATO has the capacity to act on disclosures or that these cases deserve priority over its existing investigations.

The relevant benefit is **better enforcement overall**, after accounting for work displaced by processing and investigating new reports. Counting actionable disclosures alone could overstate success if they draw staff away from more productive cases.

### 4. The broader claim remains unsupported

The advocate now supports a duty **if** an assessment establishes a worthwhile information gap and a proportionate remedy. That conditional position is considerably weaker than recommending stronger obligations now.

**My position:** commission the assessment without prejudging it. Keep existing obligations unless a particular change proves better than the alternatives. Neither raising GST nor reintroducing inheritance taxes is required by that position—and excluding those taxes does not make an unproven reporting mandate worthwhile.

## [7] ADVOCATE

## Rebuttal: Improving an existing obligation can itself be the strengthening Australia needs

The critic’s strongest objection is right: **a review must be allowed to conclude that a proposed new reporting duty is unnecessary.** I accept that. But rejecting a separate disclosure regime does not establish that Australia should retain its obligations unchanged.

### 1. The critic’s alternatives partly support the claim

The critic suggests bringing forward an existing filing rather than introducing another one. That could be the better option.

But requiring a multinational to provide important tax information earlier **is a stronger obligation**, even if it uses the same form. The relevant choice is not “another reporting regime or no reform.” It is:

> What is the least burdensome enforceable requirement that gives the ATO important information when it can still use it effectively?

My refined recommendation is to prioritise improvements to existing reporting: earlier deadlines or targeted additional information where a material gap is established. A separate disclosure duty should be the fallback, not the starting assumption.

Better information sharing and staffing should also be assessed. If they solve a particular problem without changing obligations, choose them. That is a reason to reject an unnecessary measure—not every strengthening measure.

### 2. The circumvention objection limits my proposed triggers

The fixed-fee example exposes a real weakness: **contract terms alone are an incomplete way to identify risky schemes.** I would not defend them as the sole foundation of an Australian regime.

Nor should government automatically broaden reporting whenever someone could escape a trigger. Instead, the assessment should ask whether a narrowly defined category of arrangements remains worth reporting **after allowing for changes in adviser behaviour**. If not, discard that trigger.

However, incomplete coverage is not the same as ineffectiveness. A rule need not expose every scheme to produce benefits exceeding its costs. The critic identifies a risk requiring assessment, not a decisive argument against targeted disclosure.

### 3. Measure the ATO’s opportunity costs too

I agree that “actionable reports” are an intermediate measure, not the final result. Evaluation should include:

- Businesses’ additional compliance costs.
- ATO processing and investigation costs.
- Other enforcement work displaced.
- Investigation time saved and schemes addressed earlier.

**The benefit must be better enforcement overall**, not merely more information.

### 4. Why still support the claim?

The policy judgment is that businesses organising material cross-border tax arrangements should bear proportionate responsibilities for making them visible—not leave every information disadvantage with the public authority.

That supports strengthening obligations where existing requirements fall short, while rejecting duplication and unproductive mandates. It does **not** prove that my original standalone disclosure proposal is the best instrument.

Australia should pursue that targeted strengthening without increasing GST or reintroducing inheritance taxes. Those exclusions do not prove the reform worthwhile; the fairness and enforcement case must stand on its own.



## Summary

## 1. Claim

Australia should strengthen tax obligations for multinational businesses operating in Australia without increasing the GST or reintroducing inheritance taxes.

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments

- **Fair competition and taxation:** Multinationals should not gain an advantage over local businesses by artificially shifting profits from Australian activity overseas. Legitimate cross-border payments should remain permissible.
- **Targeted reform rather than blanket increases:** The opening proposal emphasised stronger enforcement, reporting, ATO specialist capacity and international coordination—not automatically higher company tax rates.
- **Timely information:** Existing substantive tax laws may be insufficient if the ATO receives information about problematic arrangements too late. Earlier disclosure could shorten investigations or expose schemes before they spread.
- **An evolving proposal:** The Advocate initially proposed early disclosure of narrowly defined, high-risk cross-border arrangements, then suggested marketed schemes with material expected tax advantages and warning signs such as tax-linked fees or tax-specific secrecy clauses.
- **Final position:** Prioritise improvements to existing reporting, such as earlier deadlines or targeted additional information where material gaps are demonstrated. A separate disclosure duty should be a fallback. Prefer administrative solutions when they solve the problem without changing obligations.
- **Separate tax choices:** Neither higher GST nor inheritance taxes are necessary for these reforms. Any additional anti-avoidance revenue remains uncertain and should not replace responsible budgeting.

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments

- **Existing rules must be assessed first:** Australia already has transfer-pricing rules, multinational anti-avoidance measures, a diverted profits tax and country-by-country reporting. The existence of profit shifting does not establish the need for additional obligations.
- **Australian evidence is needed:** OECD frameworks demonstrate recognised policy options, not that a particular Australian measure would deliver benefits exceeding its costs.
- **Legitimate activity could face burdens:** Stronger substantive restrictions could affect genuine overseas costs or create double-taxation disputes. Disclosure duties avoid some of those risks but still impose interpretation, documentation and compliance costs.
- **Targeting creates trade-offs:** Narrow triggers may miss schemes or be circumvented; broad triggers may capture ordinary transactions and encourage excessive precautionary reporting.
- **ATO capacity and opportunity costs matter:** More information helps only if the ATO can use it productively. Evaluation must consider whether processing new reports displaces more valuable enforcement work.
- **Review without a predetermined outcome:** Assess new duties against changes to existing filings, information sharing and staffing. Keep existing obligations unless a particular change is shown to be worthwhile.
- **GST and inheritance taxes are not the alternative by default:** Rejecting stronger multinational obligations does not require supporting either tax.

## 4. Key Rebuttals

- **Existing powers versus timely visibility:** The Advocate argued that listing existing laws does not establish that the ATO receives necessary information promptly. The Critic accepted this distinction but maintained that a possible gap warrants investigation, not an immediate mandate.
- **Disclosure versus tax liability:** The Advocate clarified that disclosure would not automatically deny deductions or make arrangements unlawful. The Critic responded that reporting itself still carries costs and uncertainty.
- **Evidence threshold:** The Advocate challenged any requirement to prove exactly how an untried duty would have changed a historical case. The Critic subsequently accepted that such proof was unnecessary, while insisting on an open comparison of benefits, costs and alternatives.
- **Trigger circumvention:** The Critic argued that advisers might replace contingent fees or secrecy clauses without changing the underlying scheme. The Advocate conceded that contract terms alone were an incomplete foundation and accepted that ineffective triggers should be discarded.
- **What counts as strengthening:** The Advocate noted that bringing an existing filing forward would itself strengthen an obligation. This shifted the proposal away from a standalone regime toward the least burdensome effective change.
- **“Should” versus “could”:** The Critic repeatedly argued that conditional support for reform did not establish that stronger obligations were currently warranted. The Advocate maintained that targeted strengthening remained justified wherever an assessment established material shortcomings.

## 5. Areas of Agreement

Both sides agreed that:

- Artificial profit shifting should not give multinationals an unfair advantage.
- Legitimate overseas payments should not automatically be treated as avoidance.
- Reform needs a proposal-specific assessment of information gaps, benefits and costs.
- OECD guidance alone does not prove an Australian regime’s net benefit.
- Duplication, unnecessary compliance burdens and double taxation should be avoided.
- Success means better enforcement overall—not simply more disclosures.
- A review must be able to reject an unnecessary new duty and consider administrative alternatives.
- Neither GST increases nor inheritance taxes are required, and uncertain revenue should not be treated as guaranteed.

## 6. Main Unresolved Disagreements

- **Whether to endorse strengthening now:** The Advocate supported pursuing targeted strengthening subject to evidence; the Critic supported an open review without committing to stronger obligations.
- **Whether a material gap has been established:** No specific Australian information gap or net-benefit case was demonstrated in the debate.
- **Which instrument would work best:** Earlier existing filings, targeted additional information, separate disclosure duties and administrative improvements remained options without a settled choice.
- **Whether workable triggers would retain value:** The effectiveness of narrow disclosure criteria, after accounting for circumvention, compliance costs and ATO capacity, remained unresolved.

## 7. Overall Neutral Summary

The debate moved from a broad fairness-based case for stronger multinational tax obligations to a narrower discussion of timely reporting and evidence-based reform. The Advocate progressively accepted the need to reject unnecessary duties, account for enforcement opportunity costs and prioritise existing reporting mechanisms. The Critic accepted the potential value of earlier information but maintained that this did not yet establish a case for stronger obligations.

The sides converged substantially on evaluation and safeguards. Their remaining disagreement concerned whether those shared principles support a conditional recommendation to strengthen obligations or only an open assessment that may leave obligations unchanged.
