2. 2. **Financial Incentives as a Key Motivator:**
While the critic correctly notes that financial incentives alone do not solve all barriers, they are a foundational motivator, especially for newly qualified professionals burdened by HECS-HELP debt. This incentive can tip the balance in favor of choosing regional practice over metropolitan options, especially when combined with a defined contract period. The critic’s argument underestimates the weight of financial relief in career decision-making.

3. **Complementary Measures Can Be Added:**
The critic points out other barriers such as professional isolation and infrastructure. The proposed policy does not preclude complementary strategies to address these issues. Rather, it provides a necessary baseline incentive to attract professionals in the first place. Without attracting sufficient numbers, other support measures cannot be effective. Thus, the policy is a critical first step, not a standalone solution.

4. **Cost-Effectiveness and Long-Term Benefits:**
The critic doubts cost-effectiveness without robust data. However, existing government programs that offer partial HELP debt reductions for rural service demonstrate that debt relief is a recognized and effective tool to encourage rural practice. The critic’s claim that this is speculative overlooks this established rationale.

5. **Quality and Continuity of Care:**
The critic fears forced short-term contracts might reduce quality. This policy incentivizes longer stays than the current norm, improving care stability.

6. **Existing Programs Support the Concept:**
The critic notes that current HELP debt reduction programs require longer service periods. This suggests that shorter contracts with full loan repayment could be even more attractive and effective in recruiting new graduates who may not commit to longer terms initially. The policy can be designed with flexibility to encourage extensions beyond three years, but the initial contract and loan repayment are crucial to get professionals to regional areas in the first place.

In summary, the critic’s arguments underestimate the significance of financial incentives and the feasibility of a three-year contract as a meaningful commitment.
