The government should reduce the cost of access to whole plant medicinal cannabis for Australians with chronic pain and serious health conditions.

---

## 2. Advocate's Main Arguments  
- **Improved Patient Well-being:** Lowering costs would enable more patients to access medicinal cannabis, which can provide meaningful relief from chronic pain and symptoms of serious conditions such as multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, and cancer, thereby improving quality of life.  
- **Reduction in Harmful Pharmaceutical Use:** Medicinal cannabis is presented as a safer alternative to opioids and other pharmaceuticals, potentially reducing opioid dependence and its associated public health harms.  
- **Promotion of Healthcare Equity:** High costs currently restrict access primarily to wealthier patients; reducing costs would promote fairness and equitable access to treatment across income groups.  
- **Pragmatic and Compassionate Policy:** Even though evidence is still evolving, real-world and observational data suggest benefits that justify reducing cost barriers now, alongside strengthening regulatory oversight.  
- **Harm Reduction Potential:** Medicinal cannabis may reduce opioid use substantially, as some observational studies report significant opioid dose reductions or cessation among patients using cannabis.  
- **Addressing Financial Barriers:** The current out-of-pocket expense (~$350/month) is prohibitive and may push patients toward unregulated black market products; cost reduction would improve safety by encouraging use of quality-assured products.

---

## 3. Critic's Main Arguments  
- **Limited and Modest Evidence of Effectiveness:** Systematic reviews and authoritative Australian bodies (e.g., TGA, ANZCA) indicate that medicinal cannabis shows only modest efficacy for chronic pain, with benefits largely limited to specific neuropathic pain conditions. Overall evidence quality is low to moderate and inconsistent.  
- **Uncertain Long-Term Safety:** There are concerns about adverse effects, including cognitive and motor impairment, psychiatric symptoms, and serious adverse events. Long-term consequences remain unclear.  
- **Lack of Strong Evidence for Opioid Reduction:** Clinical studies have not conclusively demonstrated that medicinal cannabis reduces opioid use or related harms; claims of opioid-sparing effects are premature.  
- **Regulatory and Quality Concerns:** Most medicinal cannabis products in Australia are unapproved and lack rigorous safety and efficacy assessments. Lowering costs without addressing these issues risks exposing patients to unsafe products.  
- **Equity Should Not Override Evidence-Based Resource Allocation:** Subsidizing treatments with uncertain benefits and safety may divert resources from more effective therapies. Equity requires fair access to proven, safe, and effective care, not all treatments regardless of evidence.  
- **Risk of Increased Use of Unregulated Products:** Lowering costs without regulatory safeguards might encourage black market use, increasing public health risks.

---

- Equity includes access to treatments with real-world benefits and acceptable safety, even if evidence is evolving.

- Regulatory and quality issues must be resolved before expanding access to ensure patient safety.
