In summary, while transparency and learning are essential, a Royal Commission is not necessarily the best or only path. Existing inquiries, combined with targeted reviews and ongoing government accountability mechanisms, may achieve these goals more efficiently and without the high costs and delays associated with Royal Commissions.

According to the Parliament of Australia, Royal Commissions are more effective than many existing permanent bodies, including anti-corruption agencies, because of these powers and their extensive resources to examine evidence forensically (source: https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Senate/Legal_and_Constitutional_Affairs/COVID19RC47/Report/Chapter_1_-_Introduction_and_context).

2. **Consolidation vs. Comprehensive Investigation**: While consolidating existing inquiry findings is important, it is not sufficient on its own. The fragmented nature of previous inquiries means gaps and inconsistencies remain. A Royal Commission’s national scope and authority allow it to unify these findings into a coherent, comprehensive report with systemic recommendations. This is essential for addressing the pandemic’s multifaceted impacts across jurisdictions and sectors, which piecemeal inquiries cannot fully capture.

3. **Cost and Duration Are Investments, Not Just Expenses**: The critic rightly notes Royal Commissions can be costly and lengthy. However, these are investments in producing credible, systemic recommendations that reduce the risk of ineffective or fragmented reforms. The government can set clear terms of reference and timelines to manage duration. The long-term benefits of a thorough, authoritative inquiry outweigh the upfront costs, especially given the pandemic’s profound health, social, and economic consequences.

4. **Restoring Public Trust Through Transparency and Accountability**: The critic doubts that Royal Commissions restore trust, citing politicization risks. Yet, the transparency of public hearings combined with the legal obligation for government response creates a stronger accountability mechanism than other inquiry types. This openness is vital for rebuilding trust after a crisis that affected all Australians. Without such transparency, skepticism and distrust may persist.

5. **Actionable Recommendations and Implementation**: While implementation challenges exist for any inquiry, Royal Commissions’ comprehensive and authoritative findings carry greater weight, increasing the likelihood of meaningful policy changes. Their systemic approach addresses root causes rather than isolated symptoms, which smaller inquiries may miss.

6. **Weakness in Critic’s Reasoning**: The critic assumes a government-led review can replicate the independence, legal powers, and scope of a Royal Commission, but this is not supported by Australia’s statutory framework or historical practice. Parliamentary committees and government-led reviews lack the compulsory powers and legal protections that Royal Commissions possess. Moreover, the critic overlooks that the pandemic’s complexity and national scale demand a unified, empowered inquiry to ensure no critical issues are overlooked.

In conclusion, while the critic raises practical concerns about cost and duration, these do not outweigh the unique value of a Royal Commission’s independence, legal authority, and comprehensive national scope. Establishing a Royal Commission remains the best approach to thoroughly investigate Australia’s COVID-19 response, ensure accountability, restore public trust, and provide a clear roadmap for future pandemic preparedness.

Therefore, the Australian government should establish a Royal Commission to transparently investigate the management of the COVID-19 pandemic and evaluate the effectiveness of pandemic measures.
