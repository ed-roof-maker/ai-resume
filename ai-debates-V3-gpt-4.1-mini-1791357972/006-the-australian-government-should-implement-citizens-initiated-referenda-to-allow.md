The advocate's strongest argument is that Citizens Initiated Referenda (CIR) would enhance democratic participation by giving citizens a direct role in shaping policy, thus empowering them and increasing government accountability.

However, this argument assumes that CIR would function effectively and fairly in practice, which is questionable. Evidence from Australia's experience with CIR shows significant drawbacks:

3. **Potential to Tie Government Hands:** Binding referenda initiated by citizens could force governments to implement policies that may be unworkable or harmful, reducing the flexibility needed for effective governance. Politicians have expressed concerns that CIR could "tie the hands" of government, forcing them to act on referenda that may not consider complex policy implications (ABC source above).

4. **Dilution of Representative Democracy:** While CIR aims to increase participation, it risks undermining the role of elected representatives who are accountable for balancing competing interests and making informed decisions. Direct democracy tools like CIR may oversimplify complex issues into yes/no votes, leading to populist or short-term decisions rather than thoughtful policy.

In summary, while CIR sounds appealing in theory for increasing democratic engagement, in practice it risks being dominated by special interests, is costly and difficult to use for ordinary citizens, can constrain effective governance, and may weaken the representative democratic system Australia currently relies on. These practical and structural challenges undermine the claim that CIR would strengthen Australian democracy as proposed.
